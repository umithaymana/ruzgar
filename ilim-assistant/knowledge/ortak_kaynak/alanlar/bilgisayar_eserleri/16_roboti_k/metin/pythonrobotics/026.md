Graph SLAM Formulation  Author Jeff Irion Problem Formulation  Let a robot’s trajectory through its environment be represented by a sequence of \(N\) poses: \(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_N\) . Each pose lies on a manifold: \(\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathcal{M}\) . Simple examples of manifolds used in Graph SLAM include 1-D, 2-D, and 3-D space, i.e., \(\mathbb{R}\) , \(\mathbb{R}^2\) , and \(\mathbb{R}^3\) . These environments are rectilinear , meaning that there is no concept of orientation. By contrast, in \(SE(2)\) problem settings a robot’s pose consists of its location in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and its orientation \(\theta\) . Similarly, in \(SE(3)\) a robot’s pose consists of its location in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and its orientation, which can be represented via Euler angles, quaternions, or \(SO(3)\) rotation matrices. As the robot explores its environment, it collects a set of \(M\) measurements \(\mathcal{Z} = \{\mathbf{z}_j\}\) . Examples of such measurements include odometry, GPS, and IMU data. Given a set of poses \(\mathbf{p}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_N\) , we can compute the estimated measurement \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_j(\mathbf{p}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_N)\) . We can then compute the residual \(\mathbf{e}_j(\mathbf{z}_j, \hat{\mathbf{z}}_j)\) for measurement \(j\) . The formula for the residual depends on the type of measurement. As an example, let \(\mathbf{z}_1\) be an odometry measurement that was collected when the robot traveled from \(\mathbf{p}_1\) to \(\mathbf{p}_2\) . The expected measurement and the residual are computed as \[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{z}}_1(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2) &amp;= \mathbf{p}_2 \ominus \mathbf{p}_1 \\ \mathbf{e}_1(\mathbf{z}_1, \hat{\mathbf{z}}_1) &amp;= \mathbf{z}_1 \ominus \hat{\mathbf{z}}_1 = \mathbf{z}_1 \ominus (\mathbf{p}_2 \ominus \mathbf{p}_1),\end{aligned}\end{split}\] where the \(\ominus\) operator indicates inverse pose composition.

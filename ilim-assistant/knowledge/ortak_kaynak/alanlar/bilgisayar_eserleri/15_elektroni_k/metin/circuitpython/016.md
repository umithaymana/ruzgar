Basic digital logic gates perform logical operations of AND , OR and NOT on binary numbers. In digital logic design only two voltage levels or states are allowed and these states are generally referred to as Logic &ldquo;1&rdquo; and Logic &ldquo;0&rdquo;, or HIGH and LOW, or TRUE and FALSE. These two states are represented in Boolean Algebra and standard truth tables by the binary digits of &ldquo; 1 &rdquo; and &ldquo; 0 &rdquo; respectively. A good example of a digital state is a simple light switch. The switch can be either &ldquo;ON&rdquo; or &ldquo;OFF&rdquo;, one state or the other, but not both at the same time. Then we can summarise the relationship between these various digital states as being: Boolean Algebra Boolean Logic Voltage State Logic &ldquo;1&rdquo; TRUE (T) HIGH (H) Logic &ldquo;0&rdquo; FALSE (F) LOW (L) Most digital logic gates and digital logic systems use &ldquo;Positive logic&rdquo;, in which a logic level &ldquo;0&rdquo; or &ldquo;LOW&rdquo; is represented by a zero voltage, 0v or ground and a logic level &ldquo;1&rdquo; or &ldquo;HIGH&rdquo; is represented by a higher voltage such as +5 volts, with the switching from one voltage level to the other, from either a logic level &ldquo;0&rdquo; to a &ldquo;1&rdquo; or a &ldquo;1&rdquo; to a &ldquo;0&rdquo; being made as quickly as possible to prevent any faulty operation of the logic circuit. There also exists a complementary &ldquo;Negative Logic&rdquo; system in which the values and the rules of a logic &ldquo;0&rdquo; and a logic &ldquo;1&rdquo; are reversed but in this tutorial section about digital logic gates we shall only refer to the positive logic convention as it is the most commonly used. The exact switching voltage required to produce either a logic &ldquo;0&rdquo; or a logic &ldquo;1&rdquo; depends upon the specific logic group or family.

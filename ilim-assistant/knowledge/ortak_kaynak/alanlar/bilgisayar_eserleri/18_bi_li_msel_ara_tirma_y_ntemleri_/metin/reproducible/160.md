Fox Keller (2003: 200) has argued that we ought to consider computer simulation a &ldquo;qualitatively different way of doing science&rdquo;. The literature in general tends to follow Kaufmann and Smarr (1993) in referring to computer simulation as a &ldquo;third way&rdquo; for scientific methodology (theoretical reasoning and experimental practice being the first two ways.) It should also be noted that the debates around these issues have tended to focus on the form of computer simulation typical in the physical sciences, where models are based on dynamical equations. Other forms of simulation might not have the same problems, or have problems of their own (see the entry on computer simulations in science). Now, the use of AI has become pervasive in scientific practice (Wang et al. 20203, for an early survey) and has already, therefore, significantly altered scientific practice. All of the above issues seem only heightened by the advent of AI, including machine learning, deep learning, generative AI. Not only are AI systems replacing some forms of computer modelling, they seem also to be replacing significant roles formerly played by human scientists. There is the question of whether AI can provide novel evidence (Khosrowi and Finn 2025; Rowbottom et al., 2023). The notions of &ldquo;discoverer&rdquo; and &ldquo;discovery&rdquo; have been problematized (Clark and Khosrowi 2022; Duede 2023), as has the concept of &ldquo;explanation&rdquo; (Zerilli 2022); AI is considered by some less a tool and more of an &ldquo;active collaborator&rdquo; (Eren and Perez 2025 [Other Internet Resources]) or even part of a &ldquo;collective&rdquo; of agents, including AI systems (Clark and Khosrowi ibid). The rapid development of machine learning techniques has prompted some scholars to suggest that the scientific method, or some part of it, has become &ldquo;obsolete&rdquo; (Anderson 2008; Boge 2022; Carrol and Goodstein 2009). Predictions of radical changes to science have been made, based on apparently dramatic advances in the capabilities of AI systems (in addition toe the articles by Anderson and Boge just cited, see the joint paper by The Royal Society and the Alan Turing Institute 2019 [Other Internet Resources]); or, if not outright revolution, at least that constraints on novelty have been overestimated (Champion 2025). In response, criticisms have been given of the assumptions underlying the revolutionary claims. Andrews (2025), e.g., argues that expectations of radical change rely on a mistaken belief that induction is theory neutral. For the pluralist, the rise of AI may merely add to the plurality of scientific methods which already exist. For the non-pluralist, AI could be merely another tool at the scientist&rsquo;s disposal, like the telescope or particle accelerator, without altering the proper scientific method underlying its usage. In either case, the salient question for scientific method is whether AI systems have or will impact method in ways which undermine or enhance the value of scientific claims. Any answer to that question will depend on how science is changed through the growing use of AI systems. 6. Discourse on scientific method Despite philosophical disagreements, the idea of the scientific method still figures prominently in contemporary discourse on many different topics, both within science and in society at large. Often, reference to scientific method is used in ways that convey either the legend of a single, universal method characteristic of all science, or grants to a particular method or set of methods privilege as a special &lsquo;gold standard&rsquo;, often with reference to particular philosophers to vindicate the claims. Discourse on scientific method also typically arises when there is a need to distinguish between science and other activities, or for justifying the special status conveyed to science. In these areas, the philosophical attempts at identifying a set of methods characteristic for scientific endeavors are closely related to the philosophy of science&rsquo;s classical problem of demarcation (see the entry on science and pseudo-science ) and to the philosophical analysis of the social dimension of scientific knowledge and the role of science in democratic society. 6.1 &ldquo;The scientific method&rdquo; in science education and as seen by scientists One of the settings in which the legend of a single, universal scientific method has been particularly strong is science education (see, e.g., Bauer 1992; McComas 1996; Wivagg &amp; Allchin 2002). [ 5 ] Often, &lsquo;the scientific method&rsquo; is presented in textbooks and educational web pages as a fixed four or five step procedure starting from observations and description of a phenomenon and progressing over formulation of a hypothesis which explains the phenomenon, designing and conducting experiments to test the hypothesis, analyzing the results, and ending with drawing a conclusion. Such references to a universal scientific method can be found in educational material at all levels of science education (Blachowicz 2009), and numerous studies have shown that the idea of a general and universal scientific method often form part of both students&rsquo; and teachers&rsquo; conception of science (see, e.g., Aikenhead 1987; Osborne et al. 2003). In response, it has been argued that science education need to focus more on teaching about the nature of science, although views have differed on whether this is best done through student-led investigations, contemporary cases, or historical cases (Allchin, Andersen &amp; Nielsen 2014) Although occasionally phrased with reference to the H-D method, important historical roots of the legend in science education of a single, universal scientific method are the American philosopher and psychologist Dewey&rsquo;s account of inquiry in How We Think (1910) and the British mathematician Karl Pearson&rsquo;s account of science in Grammar of Science (1892).

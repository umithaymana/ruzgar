Structure of Scientific Revolutions , Kuhn articulates an evolutionary analogy for scientific change. He notes that the most contentious of Darwin&rsquo;s ideas was not the idea of evolution per se but the idea that evolution is not teleological. That is, while species evolve away from primitive beginnings, they are not evolving towards any particular form; not guided towards any idea of man or other species &lsquo;present from the first creation of life, perhaps in the mind of God&rsquo; (Kuhn 1962: 172). This is not to say that there is no important difference between earlier and later states. For over time more species emerge and they are more elaborate and specialized than those that went before. There can be evolution from without evolution to . This, says Kuhn, provides a good analogy for the development of science. This discussion takes place within a chapter entitled &lsquo;Progress through Revolutions&rsquo;, a key aim of which is to understand what we are referring to when we talk about scientific progress, especially when a science has gone through a scientific revolution. The immediate context for the evolutionary analogy is Kuhn&rsquo;s first discussion of the place of truth in the development of science. Truth, as Kuhn himself notes, is scarcely mentioned in Structure until this point. In effect summarizing much of the argument of Structure , Kuhn argues that the existence of science requires specialist communities that have shared standards for the evaluation of proposed puzzle-solutions; even in revolutionary science, the community makes its choice of new paradigm on the basis of its solving important hitherto unsolved problems while also preserving as much of the problem-solving power of the preceding paradigm as possible. Kuhn raises the question of whether truth is among the shared criteria of evaluation. Rather than answer this question directly, Kuhn appeals to the analogy with biological evolution just mentioned. For if one thinks that science is evolving over time, one might think that it is evolving towards the truth. Even if our theories are not exactly true now, they may be getting closer to the truth over time. And this, one might think, is the goal of science, to get as close to the truth as possible. This is what Laudan (1981) calls &lsquo;convergent realism&rsquo;. The analogy with Darwin&rsquo;s anti-teleological conception of biological evolution is Kuhn&rsquo;s response to the convergent realist picture and his answer to the broader question of the nature of scientific progress. There is genuine progress, not mere change or development. Later science is genuinely better than earlier science. But this improvement is not a matter of getting closer to the truth. It is a matter of solving more puzzles than before, and more detailed and specific puzzles at that. In order to solve such puzzles, science itself is more sophisticated and specialized than it was. So the development of science looks much like the evolution of life: there is progress away from primitive kinds (early life forms; pre-paradigm science) but without there being any progress towards a goal (no ideal form of a species; no final, true theory). Progress away leads to greater variety, complexity, and specialisation. But that kind of progress does not have an end point.

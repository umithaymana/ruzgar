It contains Lakatos&rsquo;s important paper &ldquo;Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes&rdquo; (FMSRP) which we have discussed already. A briefer account of this methodology had already appeared (Lakatos 1968a), in which Lakatos distinguished dogmatic, na&iuml;ve and sophisticated falsificationist positions, attributing them to &ldquo;Popper 0 , Popper 1 and Popper 2 &rdquo;&mdash;or as he otherwise put it, &ldquo;proto-Popper, pseudo-Popper and proper-Popper&rdquo;. (Popper did not appreciate being disassembled into temporal or ideological parts and protested &ldquo;I am not a Trinity&rdquo;.) Lakatos&rsquo;s methodology has been seen, rightly, as an attempt to reconcile Popper&rsquo;s falsificationism with the views of Thomas Kuhn. Popper saw science as consisting of bold explanatory conjectures, and dramatic refutations that led to new conjectures. Kuhn (and Polanyi before him) objected that No process yet disclosed by the historical study of scientific development at all resembles the methodological stereotype of falsification by direct comparison with nature (Kuhn 1962: 77). Instead, science consists of long periods of &ldquo;normal science&rdquo;, paradigm-based research, where the task is to force nature to fit the paradigm. When nature refuses to comply, this is not seen as a refutation, but rather as an anomaly. It casts doubt, not on the ruling paradigm, but on the ingenuity of the scientists&mdash;&ldquo;only the practitioner is blamed, not his tools&rdquo;. It is only in extraordinary periods of &ldquo;revolutionary science&rdquo; that anything like Popperian refutations occur. Lakatos proposed a middle-way, in which Kuhn&rsquo;s socio-psychological tools were replaced by logico-methodological ones. The basic unit of appraisal is not the isolated testable theory, but rather the &ldquo;research programme&rdquo; within which a series of testable theories is generated. Each theory produced within a research programme contains the same common or &ldquo;hard core&rdquo; assumptions, surrounded by a &ldquo;protective belt&rdquo; of auxiliary hypotheses. When a particular theory is refuted, adherents of a programme do not pin the blame on their hard-core assumptions, which they render &ldquo;irrefutable by fiat&rdquo;. Instead, criticism is directed at the hypotheses in the &ldquo;protective belt&rdquo; and they are modified to deal with the problem. Importantly, these modifications are not random&mdash;they are in the best cases guided by the heuristic principles implicit in the &ldquo;hard core&rdquo; of the programme. A programme progresses theoretically if the new theory solves the anomaly faced by the old and is independently testable, making new predictions. A programme progresses empirically if at least one of these new predictions is confirmed. Notice that a programme can make progress, both theoretically and empirically, even though every theory produced within it is refuted. A programme degenerates if its successive theories are not theoretically progressive (because it predicts no novel facts), or not empirically progressive (because novel predictions get refuted). Furthermore, and contrary to Kuhn&rsquo;s idea that normally science is dominated by a single paradigm, Lakatos claimed that the history of science typically consists of competing research programmes.

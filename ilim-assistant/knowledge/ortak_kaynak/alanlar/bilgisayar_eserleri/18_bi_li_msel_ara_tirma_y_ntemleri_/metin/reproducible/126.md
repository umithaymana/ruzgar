No-one who attended Imre&rsquo;s groups has forgotten the intensity and brilliance of the atmosphere. &ldquo;He opened the world to me!&rdquo; a participant said. Even those who were later disillusioned with communism or ashamed of acts they committed, remember the sense of inspiration, clear thinking and hope for a new society they felt in Imre&rsquo;s secret seminars. (Long 2002: 265) However, in Lakatos&rsquo;s group the emphasis was on preparing the young cadres for the coming communist revolution, rather than engaging in public propaganda or antifascist resistance activities (Bandy 2009: ch. 3). In March 1944 the Germans invaded Hungary to forestall its attempts to negotiate a separate peace. (The Hungarian government had allied with the Axis powers, in the hopes of recovering some of the territories lost at the Treaty of Trianon in 1920. By 1944 they had begun to realize that this was a mistake.) Admiral Horthy, whose anti-Semitism was a more gentlemanly affair than that of the Nazis (he was fine with systematic discrimination but apparently drew the line at mass-murder), was forced to accept a collaborationist government led by D&ouml;me Szt&oacute;jay as prime minister. The new regime had none of Horthy&rsquo;s humanitarian scruples and began a policy of enthusiastic and systematic cooperation with the Nazi genocide program. In May, Lakatos&rsquo;s mother, grandmother and other relatives were forced into the Debrecen ghetto, thence to die in Auschwitz&mdash;the fate of about 600,000 Hungarian Jews. Lakatos&rsquo;s father, a wine merchant, managed to get away and survived the war, eventually ending up in Australia. A little earlier, in March, Lakatos himself had managed to escape from Debrecen to Nagyv&aacute;rad (now Oradea in Romania) with false papers under the name of Moln&aacute;r. Later, a Hungarian friend, Vilma Bal&aacute;zs, recalled that Imre [had been] very close to his mother and they were quite poor. He often blamed himself for her death and wondered if he could have saved her (Bandy 2009: 32). In Nagyv&aacute;rad Lakatos restarted his Marxist group. The co-leader was his then-girlfriend and subsequent wife, &Eacute;va R&eacute;v&eacute;sz. In May, the group was joined by &Eacute;va Izs&aacute;k, a 19-year-old Jewish antifascist activist who needed lodgings with a non-Jewish family. Lakatos decided that there was a risk that she would be captured and forced to betray them, hence her duty, both to the group and to the cause, was to commit suicide. A member of the group took her across country to Debrecen and gave her cyanide (Congden 1997, Long 2002, Bandy 2009, ch. 5). To lovers of Russian literature, the episode recalls Dostoevsky&rsquo;s The Possessed/Demons (based in part on the real-life Nechaev affair).

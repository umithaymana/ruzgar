Marcum (2015) argues that Kuhn later work amounts to an evolutionary philosophy of science that differs from his earlier historical philosophy of science. Wray (2011) connects Kuhn&rsquo;s evolutionary epistemology to his social epistemology. The development of an evolutionary approach to the epistemology of science is found most clearly in work that was not published in Kuhn&rsquo;s lifetime, in particular his Shearman Memorial lectures, which Kuhn gave at University College London in 1987 and the draft of a book (for which the lectures were preparatory sketches), The Plurality of Worlds: An Evolutionary Theory of Scientific Change, on which he worked in the 1990s. (See Kuhn 2022 for these previously unpublished works.) Wray and Marcum both emphasize the role of the evolution of lexicons as a particularly important element of scientific change. Incommensurability is no longer just a matter of the problems of comparability between science and its paradigms either side of a scientific revolution. Kuhn now sees incommensurability also as a mechanism that isolates communities synchronically, which occurs as branches of science become increasingly specialized and distinct. As discussed above, lexicons provide the conceptual frameworks within which scientific knowledge is articulated, dividing things into categories and kinds. The no-overlap principle, which requires that lexicons are organised hierarchically, means that distinct lexicons covering overlapping subject matters cannot be merged. The conceptual frameworks of related but distinct scientific specialties will thus be separate and incommensurable. While T he Plurality of World s continues the emphasis on incommensurability that characterizes Kuhn&rsquo;s later work, it also shows a return to the naturalism that is distinctive of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions . Kuhn draws on developmental psychology to argue that children have innate mechanisms for recognizing and tracking objects and kinds. These pre-linguistic capacities are the products of evolution. While these are distinct capacities in infants, they become integrated as children acquire language. There is a no-overlap principle for objects&mdash;objects do not split so that they are in multiple places simultaneously, nor do they merge such that one piece of matter is two objects. So objects have a single, non-branching path through space and time. This parallels (and indeed leads to) the no-overlap principle for kinds. As a child&rsquo;s language develops, its cognitive capacities and conceptual frameworks evolve. Language does not simply add concepts to those innate capacities and structures; rather, it refines and restructures them. Science does the same. It refines and restructures existing lexicons, rather than adding to or replacing existing ones. In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions Kuhn saw gestalt shifts both as a metaphor for paradigm change and also as indicative of the psychological mechanisms underlying both kinds of change. Something similar is true in The Plurality of Worlds . For Kuhn sees conceptual development in children as a model for conceptual change in science. And he also regards the latter as an extension of the former. That is, the facts of human developmental psychology exhibited in childhood also explain how conceptual change in science occurs. More broadly, biological evolution is not just an analogy for scientific change, it also explains scientific change. 6. History of Science Kuhn&rsquo;s historical work covered several topics in the history of physics and astronomy. During the 1950s his focus was primarily on the early theory of heat and the work of Sadi Carnot. However, his first book concerned the Copernican revolution in planetary astronomy (1957). This book grew out of the teaching he had done on James Conant&rsquo;s General Education in Science curriculum at Harvard but also presaged some of the ideas of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions . In detailing the problems with the Ptolemaic system and Copernicus&rsquo; solution to them, Kuhn showed two things. First, he demonstrated that Aristotelian science was genuine science and that those working within that tradition, in particular those working on Ptolemaic astronomy, were engaged in an entirely reasonable and recognizably scientific project. Secondly, Kuhn showed that Copernicus was himself far more indebted to that tradition than had typically been recognized. Thus the popular view that Copernicus was a modern scientist who overthrew an unscientific and long-outmoded viewpoint is mistaken both by exaggerating the difference between Copernicus and the Ptolemaic astronomers and in underestimating the scientific credentials of work carried out before Copernicus. This mistaken view&mdash;a product of the distortion caused by our current state of knowledge&mdash;can be rectified only by seeing the activities of Copernicus and his predecessors in the light of the puzzles presented to them by tradition that they inevitably had to work with. While Kuhn does acknowledge the influence of causes outside science (such as a resurgence in Sun worship (1962/70a, 152&ndash;3)), he nonetheless emphasizes the fact that astronomers were responding primarily to problems raised within science. What appealed to them in Copernicus&rsquo; model was its ability to do away with ad hoc devices in Ptolemy&rsquo;s system (such as the equant), to explain key phenomena in a pleasing fashion (the observed retrograde motion of the planets), and to explain away otherwise inexplicable coincidences in Ptolemy&rsquo;s system (such as the alignment of the Sun and the centres of the epicycles of the inferior planets). In the 1960s Kuhn&rsquo;s historical work turned toward the early history of quantum theory, culminating in his book Black-Body Theory and the Quantum Discontinuity . According to classical physics a particle could possess any energy in a continuous range and if it changes energy it does so in a continuous fashion, possessing at some point in time every energy between the initial and final energy states. Modern quantum theory denies both these classical principles. Energy is quantised&mdash;a particle may possess only one of a set of discrete energies.

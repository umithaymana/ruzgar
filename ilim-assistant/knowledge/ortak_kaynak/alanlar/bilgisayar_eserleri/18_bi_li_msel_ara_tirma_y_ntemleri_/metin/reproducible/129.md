Lakatos left Hungary in November 1956 after the Soviet Union crushed the short-lived Hungarian revolution. He walked across the border into Austria with his wife and her parents. Within two months he was at King&rsquo;s College Cambridge, with a Rockefeller Fellowship to write a PhD under the supervision of R.B. Braithwaite, which he completed in 1959 under the title &ldquo;Essays in the Logic of Mathematical Discovery&rdquo;. If we set aside his romantic adventures, the story of Lakatos&rsquo;s life thereafter is largely the story of his work, though we should not forget his activities as an academic politician. Even his friendship with Feyerabend and his friendship and subsequent bust-up with Popper were very much work-related. In Britain his academic career was meteoric. In 1960 he was appointed Assistant Lecturer in Karl Popper&rsquo;s department at the London School of Economics. By 1969 he was Professor of Logic, with a worldwide reputation as a philosopher of science. During the student revolts of the 1960s, which in Britain were centred on the LSE, Lakatos became an establishment figure. He wrote a &ldquo;Letter to the Director of the London School of Economics&rdquo; defending academic freedom and academic autonomy, which was widely circulated. It denounces the student radicals for allegedly trying to do what he himself had done at Debrecen and E&ouml;tv&ouml;s (though he was careful to conceal the parallel, citing Nazi and Muscovite precedents instead) (LTD: 247). Lakatos died suddenly in 1974 of a heart attack at the height of his powers. He was 51. 2. Lakatos&rsquo;s Big Ideas Imre Lakatos has two chief claims to fame. 2.1 Against Formalism in Mathematics The first is his Philosophy of Mathematics, especially as set forth in &ldquo;Proofs and Refutations&rdquo; (1963&ndash;64) a series of four articles, based on his PhD thesis, and written in the form of a many-sided dialogue. These were subsequently combined in a posthumous book and published, with additions, in 1976. The title is an allusion to a famous paper of Popper&rsquo;s, &ldquo;Conjectures and Refutations&rdquo; (the signature essay of his best-known collection), in which Popper outlines his philosophy of science. Lakatos&rsquo;s point is that the development of mathematics is much more like the development of science as portrayed by Popper than is commonly supposed, and indeed much more like the development of science as portrayed by Popper than Popper himself supposed. What Lakatos does not make so much of (though he does not conceal it either) is that in his view the development of mathematics is also much more like the development of thought in general as analysed by Hegel than Hegel himself supposed. There is thesis, antithesis and synthesis, &ldquo;Hegelian language, which [Lakatos thinks would] generally be capable of describing the various developments in mathematics&rdquo; (P&amp;R: 146). Thus there is a certain sense in which Lakatos out-Hegels Hegel, giving a dialectical analysis of a discipline (mathematics) that Hegel himself despised as insufficiently dialectical (see Larvor 1998, 1999, 2001). Hence Feyerabend&rsquo;s gibe (which Lakatos took in good part) that Lakatos was a Pop-Hegelian, the bastard child of Popperian father and a Hegelian mother (F&amp;AM: 184&ndash;185). Proofs and Refutations is a critique of &ldquo;formalist&rdquo; philosophies of mathematics (including formalism proper, logicism and intuitionism), which, in Lakatos&rsquo;s view, radically misrepresent the nature of mathematics as an intellectual enterprise. For Lakatos, the development of mathematics should not be construed as series of Euclidean deductions where the contents of the relevant concepts has been carefully specified in advance so as to preclude equivocation. Rather, these water-tight deductions from well-defined premises are the (perhaps temporary) end-points of an evolutionary, and indeed a dialectical , process in which the constituent concepts are initially ill-defined, open-ended or ambiguous but become sharper and more precise in the context of a protracted debate. The proofs are refined in conjunction with the concepts (hence &ldquo;proof-generated concepts&rdquo;) whilst &ldquo;refutations&rdquo; in the form of counterexamples play a prominent part in the process. [One might almost say, paraphrasing Hegel, that in Lakatos&rsquo;s view &ldquo;when Euclidean demonstrations paint their grey in grey, then has a shape of mathematical life grown old&hellip;The owl of the formalist Minerva begins its flight only with the falling of dusk&rdquo; (Hegel 2008 [1820/21]: 16).] Lakatos is also keen to display the development of mathematics as a rational affair even though the proofs (to begin with) are often lacking in logical rigour and the key concepts are often open-ended and unclear The idea&mdash;expressed so clearly by Seidel [and clearly endorsed by Lakatos himself]&mdash;that a proof can be respectable without being flawless, was a revolutionary one in 1847, and, unfortunately, still sounds revolutionary today. (P&amp;R: 139) A corollary of this is that in mathematics many of the &ldquo;proofs&rdquo; are not really proofs in the full sense of the word (that is, demonstrations that proceed deductively from apodictic premises via unquestionable rules of inference to certain conclusions) and that many of the &ldquo;refutations&rdquo; are not really refutations either, since something rather like the &ldquo;refuted&rdquo; thesis often survives the refutation and arises refreshed and invigorated from the dialectical process.

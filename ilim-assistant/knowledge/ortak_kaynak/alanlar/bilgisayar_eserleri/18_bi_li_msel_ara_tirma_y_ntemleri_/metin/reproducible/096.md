In order to explain the distribution of energy within a cavity (black-body radiation), Planck used the device of dividing up the energy states into multiples of the unit or &lsquo;quantum&rsquo; h &nu; (where &nu; is the frequency of radiation and h is what subsequently became known as Planck&rsquo;s constant). Planck did this in order to employ a statistical technique of Boltzmann&rsquo;s whereby the range of possible continuous energies is divided into &lsquo;cells&rsquo; of similar energies that could be treated together for mathematical purposes. Kuhn notes that Planck was puzzled that in carrying out his derivation, only by fixing the cell size at h &nu; could he get the result he wanted&mdash;the technique should have worked for any way of dividing the cells, so long as they were small enough but not too small. This work of Planck&rsquo;s was carried out in the period 1900&ndash;1, which is the date tradition has accorded to the invention of the quantum concept. However, argued Kuhn, Planck did not have in mind a genuine physical discontinuity of energies until 1908, which is after Albert Einstein and Paul Ehrenfest had themselves emphasized it in 1905&ndash;6. Many readers were surprised not to find mention of paradigms or incommensurability. Kuhn later added an Afterword, &ldquo;Revisiting Planck&rdquo;, explaining that he had not repudiated or ignored those ideas but that they were implicit in the argument he gave. Indeed the whole essay may be seen as a demonstration of an incommensurability between the mature quantum theory and the early quantum theory of Planck which was still rooted in classical statistical physics. In particular the very term &lsquo;quantum&rsquo; changed its meaning between its introduction by Planck and its later use. Kuhn argues that the modern quantum concept was introduced first not by Planck but by Einstein. Furthermore, this fact is hidden both by the continued use of the same term and by the same distortion of history that has affected our conception of Ptolemy and Copernicus. As in Copernicus&rsquo; case, Planck has been seen as more revolutionary than in fact he was. In Planck&rsquo;s case, however, this misconception was also shared by Planck himself later in life. 7. Criticism and Influence Kuhn&rsquo;s work met with a largely critical reception among philosophers. Some of this criticism became muted as Kuhn&rsquo;s work became better understood and as his own thinking underwent transformation. At the same time other developments in philosophy opened up new avenues for criticism. That criticism has largely focussed on two areas. First, it has been argued that Kuhn&rsquo;s account of the development of science is not entirely accurate. Secondly, critics have attacked Kuhn&rsquo;s notion of incommensurability, arguing that either it does not exist or, if it does exist, it is not a significant problem. Despite this criticism, Kuhn&rsquo;s work has been hugely influential, both within philosophy and outside it.

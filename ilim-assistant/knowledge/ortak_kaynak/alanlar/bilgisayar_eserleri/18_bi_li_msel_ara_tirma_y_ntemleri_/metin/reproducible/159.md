Computers also allow for more elaborate experimentation (higher speed, better filtering, more variables, sophisticated coordination and control). The use of computer simulations, computer models, AI and machine learning in scientific practice has grown with the growth of computing power. The roles of computers in science have likewise been examined for decades, and most of those issues remain salient. But especially now, in response to various forms of AI systems, the potential for serious &ldquo;disruptions&rdquo; (Hopster and L&ouml;hr 2023; L&ouml;hr 2023) to science and scientific method is being considered. A number of issues related to what is now called &ldquo;classical&rdquo; computer simulations have been raised. Computers have been used to automate measurements, quantifications, calculations, and statistical analyses which, for practical reasons, could not be otherwise carried out. Many of the steps involved in reaching a conclusion on the basis of an experiment are now made inside a &ldquo;black box&rdquo;, without the direct involvement or awareness of a human. This is even more so for AI systems. This &ldquo;opacity&rdquo; has epistemological implications, regarding what we can know, and how we can know it (Creel 2020; Humphreys 2009; Lipton 2018; Zerilli 2022). To have confidence in the results, computer methods were classically subjected to tests of verification and validation. Verification ensures that the system is properly running or calculating the intended model; validation tests whether reliable inferences can be made about the phenomena from the system running the intended model. The use of validity and verification as the testing methods has been criticized. Oreskes et al. (1994) raise concerns that &ldquo;validiation&rdquo;, because it suggests deductive inference, might lead to over-confidence in the results of simulations. The distinction itself is probably too clean since actual practice in the testing of simulations mixes and moves back and forth between the two (Weissart 1997; Parker 2008a; Winsberg 2010). Computer simulations do seem to have a deductive character. The principles by which they operate are built in by the programmers, and any results of the simulation follow from those in-built principles in such a way that those results could, in principle, be deduced from the program code and its inputs. The status of simulations as experiments has therefore been examined (Kaufmann and Smarr 1993; Humphreys 1995; Hughes 1999; Norton and Suppe 2001). This literature considers the epistemology of these experiments: what we can learn by simulation, and also the kinds of justifications which can be given in applying that knowledge to the &ldquo;real&rdquo; world.

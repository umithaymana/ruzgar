A single token lookahead recursive descent parser can&rsquo;t see far enough to tell that it&rsquo;s parsing an assignment until after it has gone through the left-hand side and stumbled onto the = . You might wonder why it even needs to. After all, we don&rsquo;t know we&rsquo;re parsing a + expression until after we&rsquo;ve finished parsing the left operand. The difference is that the left-hand side of an assignment isn&rsquo;t an expression that evaluates to a value. It&rsquo;s a sort of pseudo-expression that evaluates to a &ldquo;thing&rdquo; you can assign to. Consider: var a = &quot;before&quot; ; a = &quot;value&quot; ; On the second line, we don&rsquo;t evaluate a (which would return the string &ldquo;before&rdquo;). We figure out what variable a refers to so we know where to store the right-hand side expression&rsquo;s value. The classic terms for these two constructs are l-value and r-value . All of the expressions that we&rsquo;ve seen so far that produce values are r-values. An l-value &ldquo;evaluates&rdquo; to a storage location that you can assign into. In fact, the names come from assignment expressions: l -values appear on the left side of the = in an assignment, and r -values on the right . We want the syntax tree to reflect that an l-value isn&rsquo;t evaluated like a normal expression. That&rsquo;s why the Expr.Assign node has a Token for the left-hand side, not an Expr. The problem is that the parser doesn&rsquo;t know it&rsquo;s parsing an l-value until it hits the = .

In the past, the use of a film positive (not the original negative) allowed the editor to do as much experimenting as he or she wished, without the risk of damaging the original. With digital editing, editors can experiment just as much as before except with the footage completely transferred to a computer hard drive. When the film workprint had been cut to a satisfactory state, it was then used to make an edit decision list (EDL). The negative cutter referred to this list while processing the negative, splitting the shots into rolls, which were then contact printed to produce the final film print or answer print . Today, production companies have the option of bypassing negative cutting altogether. With the advent of digital intermediate ("DI"), the physical negative does not necessarily need to be physically cut and hot spliced together; rather the negative is optically scanned into the computer(s) and a cut list is confirmed by a DI editor. Women in film editing [ edit ] In the early years of film, editing was considered a technical job; editors were expected to "cut out the bad bits" and string the film together. Indeed, when the Motion Picture Editors Guild was formed, they chose to be "below the line", that is, not a creative guild, but a technical one. Women were not usually able to break into the "creative" positions; directors, cinematographers, producers, and executives were almost always men. Editing afforded creative women a place to assert their mark on the filmmaking process. The history of film has included many women editors such as Dede Allen , Anne Bauchens , Margaret Booth , Barbara McLean , Anne V. Coates , Adrienne Fazan , Verna Fields , Blanche Sewell and Eda Warren . [ 13 ] Post-production editing [ edit ] Main article: Post-production See also: Rough cut , Offline editing , and Online editing The post-production process of editing a film is typically divided into three distinct stages, commonly referred to as the editor's cut , the director's cut , and the final cut . In the 21st century, it is extremely rare to perform editing by literally cutting strips of film. [ 14 ] It would be more technically accurate to say that an editor prepares an "assembly" of digital video clips, but the word "cut" remains in widespread use. [ 14 ] An editor's cut (sometimes referred to as the " assembly edit " or "rough cut") is normally the first pass of what the final film will be when it reaches picture lock . It is the "most basic cut possible". [ 15 ] For many reasons, as explained further in the production board article, the scenes in most films are not shot in the order in which they appear in the script. The editor's main task at this stage is to arrange all the scenes in the correct order. [ 15 ] The film editor usually starts working while principal photography starts. [ 16 ] Sometimes, prior to cutting, the editor and director will have seen and discussed " dailies " (raw footage shot each day) as shooting progresses. As production schedules have shortened over the years, this co-viewing happens less often. Screening dailies gives the editor a general idea of the director's intentions. Because it is the first pass and generally includes everything, the editor's cut is usually much longer than the final film. [ 17 ] [ 18 ] The editor continues to refine the cut while shooting continues, and often the entire editing process goes on for many months and sometimes more than a year, depending on the film. The editor's cut is an opportunity for the editor to shape the story and present their vision of how the film should unfold. It provides a solid foundation for further collaboration with the director, allowing them to assess the initial assembly and provide feedback or guidance on the creative direction. Meanwhile, the producer, the studio, and the investors are anxious at this point and are curious about viewing the work in progress, but the director and editor always insist that they cannot see the editor's cut because "it in no way resembles a well-made movie". [ 17 ] The pace is choppy and flat, there is no music, the dialogue has not been properly mixed and sounds hollow. [ 18 ] When shooting is finished, the director can then turn his or her full attention to collaborating with the editor and further refining the cut of the film. This is the time that is set aside where the film editor's first cut is molded to fit the director's vision. [ 15 ] In the United States, under the rules of the Directors Guild of America (DGA), directors receive a minimum of ten weeks after completion of principal photography for a major motion picture intended for theatrical distribution to prepare their first cut. [ 19 ] Under DGA rules, the director and editor are supposed to be protected from micromanagement by the studio and the producer during this part of the editing process; the studio cannot force the director to screen the film for anyone until the minimum period of ten weeks is complete. [ 17 ] [ 18 ] While collaborating on what is referred to as the "director's cut", the director and the editor go over the entire movie in great detail; scenes and shots are reordered, removed, shortened and otherwise tweaked. [ 15 ] It is a "painstakingly slow process". [ 18 ] Every mistake captured on camera during production will be played again and again in the editing room, so "the editor must be sensitive to the director's ego". [ 20 ] The narrative pace of an editor's cut is usually too slow. [ 15 ] Scenes that seemed like good ideas during pre-production and principal photography often turn out to be boring, time-consuming tangents on the screen and must be ruthlessly trimmed and tightened up. [ 15 ] The narrative is sharpened and the story is polished. [ 17 ] Often it is discovered that there are plot holes , missing shots or even missing segments which might require that new scenes be filmed (i.e., pick-up shots ). The director's cut typically involves multiple iterations and discussions until both the director and editor are satisfied with the overall direction of the film. The goal is to align the film with the director's artistic vision and narrative objectives. Directors and editors may form a strong working relationship during editing, which can take more time than all preceding stages of production combined. [ 20 ] A director may trust an editor to supervise the remaining stages of post-production. [ 21 ] Delegating this responsibility frees up the director's workload, allowing them to engage other tasks, e.g. working on other films. [ 21 ] Film editors traditionally discarded unused shots by literally throwing strips of film onto the floor. [ 22 ] This led to the common entertainment industry expression that actors' performances ended up on the "cutting-room floor". [ 22 ] Nowadays, it is rare for unused shots to be literally discarded or destroyed. [ 22 ] More often, they are saved for bonus reels or blooper reels for home video releases. [ 22 ] Often after the director has had their chance to oversee a cut, the subsequent cuts are supervised by one or more producers, who represent the production company or film studio . The studio "usually has the final say" if the director does not have contractual control over the final cut. [ 15 ] Sometimes the producer is able to provide valuable input, especially if the director has become too close emotionally to the project. [ 17 ] There have been several conflicts in the past between directors and studios, sometimes leading to the use of the " Alan Smithee " credit signifying when a director no longer wants to be associated with the final release. Sometimes, a studio may show a nearly finished version of the final cut to a test audience to assess how an audience will react to the film, then revise the film further based on audience feedback. [ 22 ] The final cut is the last stage of post-production editing and represents the definitive version of the film. It is the result of the collaborative efforts between the director, editor, producer, and other key stakeholders. The final cut reflects the agreed-upon creative decisions and serves as the basis for distribution and exhibition. At that point, the sound editor can begin to finalize the soundtrack and the composer can time scenes to finish writing the film score . [ 22 ] Mise-en-scène [ edit ] Main article: Mise-en-scène Mise-en-scène is a complex film studies concept which is difficult to define. Ed Sikov has attempted to define it as "the totality of expressive content within the image". [ 23 ] It is used to describe all the visual content seen on the screen and all the information communicated by that content, both in each individual shot and as an interrelated sequence of shots.

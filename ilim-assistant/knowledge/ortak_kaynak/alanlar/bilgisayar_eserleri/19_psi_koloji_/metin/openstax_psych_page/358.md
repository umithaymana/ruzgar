Biofeedback is a technique that uses electronic equipment to accurately measure a person’s neuromuscular and autonomic activity—feedback is provided in the form of visual or auditory signals. The main assumption of this approach is that providing somebody biofeedback will enable the individual to develop strategies that help gain some level of voluntary control over what are normally involuntary bodily processes (Schwartz &amp; Schwartz, 1995). A number of different bodily measures have been used in biofeedback research, including facial muscle movement, brain activity, and skin temperature, and it has been applied successfully with individuals experiencing tension headaches, high blood pressure, asthma, and phobias (Stein, 2001). Stress Management for Students Prevalence and Impact of Stress in College The college years bring unprecedented independence, opportunity, and growth — but also a surge in responsibilities and, often, stress that many students are unprepared to handle. Research consistently shows that stress is nearly universal in the college population. A Penn State McNair Scholar study found that in 2019 alone, more than half of college students reported feeling "overwhelming anxiety and tremendous stress," a figure that climbed even higher the following year (Fall, 2021). This is not simply a matter of feeling overwhelmed before a midterm. Chronic stress in students has been linked to impaired concentration, weakened memory, difficulty with problem-solving, and reduced academic performance. It also carries serious health consequences, as discussed earlier in the chapter. Stress in academic environments can also impact student' relationships, increasing conflict and social withdrawal at a time when building a support network is especially important. Student stress can be significantly compounded by other factors and life experiences. For example, about one in five U.S. undergraduates is a parent (ACE, 2023). In one study, over 43% student parents report feeling stressed nearly all of the time, with their stress associated with burnout, difficulty balancing school work and child care, financial insecurity, and guilt related to self-perception of reduced focus on their children (Aspen Institute, 2021). Emotional and Lifestyle-Based Coping Strategies Effective stress management is a learnable skill, not an innate trait. Making intentional, consistent efforts to manage stress allows students to protect both their mental health and their academic trajectory. Managing stress effectively begins with tending to the body and building emotional awareness. As described earlier, regular physical activity is one of the most well-supported interventions available. Adequate, consistent sleep is equally critical, as sleep deprivation dramatically amplifies the stress response and undermines cognitive performance the next day. A balanced diet, while easy to neglect during busy academic periods, also plays a measurable role in how the body and mind regulate stress. Beyond physical health, social connection is a powerful buffer against chronic stress. Talking with friends, family, or a campus counselor provides emotional release and perspective.

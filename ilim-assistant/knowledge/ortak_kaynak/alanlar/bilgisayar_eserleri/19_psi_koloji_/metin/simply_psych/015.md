Cognitive Dissonance In Psychology: Definition and Examples Related Articles Attribution Theory Conformity Types Compliance Techniques Social Learning Theory Attribution Error Moscovici Influence Skip to content Cognitive Dissonance By Saul McLeod, PhD | Updated on November 10, 2026 Reviewed by Olivia Guy-Evans, MSc &amp; Cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort experienced when a person holds two conflicting beliefs or when their actions don’t align with their beliefs. To reduce this uncomfortable feeling, people often change their thoughts, behavior, or attitudes to make everything feel more consistent. For example, when people smoke (behavior) and they know that smoking causes cancer (cognition), they are in a state of cognitive dissonance. Cognitive dissonance happens when what we believe clashes with how we act. For example, valuing healthy living while smoking creates inner tension. That tension pushes us to change our behavior, adjust our beliefs, or find a way to justify the gap. Key Takeaways Cognitive Clash: Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort of holding two conflicting beliefs, or acting against a belief. It pushes people to restore consistency. Magnitude: How much discomfort you feel depends on how important the clashing beliefs are and how many dissonant cognitions you hold relative to consonant ones. Four Main Fixes: People typically change their behaviour, reject or downplay the conflicting belief, add new supportive beliefs, or decide the conflict simply doesn&#8217;t matter that much. Less-Leads-to-More: Festinger and Carlsmith&#8217;s (1959) classic experiment found that a $1 reward for lying produced more attitude change than a $20 one. The small payment left no easy excuse for lying. Individual Differences: Highly anxious people tend to show stronger dissonance effects, while others tolerate inconsistency without much discomfort. The classic paradigms also rely heavily on small student samples. Modern Evidence: Brain-imaging studies now link dissonance to real arousal in the brain. A 2019 meta-analysis of over 7,800 participants confirms that deliberately triggered dissonance produces robust, real-world behaviour change. Verdict: Mixed or contested. Writing against one&#8217;s own view shifts attitudes, but a large preregistered replication did not confirm that this depends on free choice. In This Article: Toggle Cognitive Dissonance Theory Festinger&#8217;s Theory: Dissonance as a Drive State Festinger (1957) described dissonance as a negative drive state, like hunger or thirst. Hunger motivates eating. Dissonance motivates people to restore consistency. It also makes them avoid situations and information likely to raise the tension. Festinger defined cognitions as &#8220;the things a person knows about himself, about his behaviour and about his surroundings.&#8221; That covers beliefs, attitudes, values, and knowledge of one&#8217;s own actions. Any two cognitions can relate in one of three ways: Consonant: One cognition follows from or fits the other, so they are consistent. Dissonant: The opposite of one cognition follows from the other, so they clash. Irrelevant: The two cognitions have no bearing on each other. &#8220;I smoke&#8221; and &#8220;smoking causes cancer&#8221; are dissonant, assuming the person does not want cancer. Festinger&#8217;s central claim was that attitude change is a major way of reducing dissonance. The theory belongs to a family of cognitive consistency theories. These include Heider&#8217;s balance theory and Osgood and Tannenbaum&#8217;s congruity theory. All assume that inconsistency is aversive and drives change toward coherence. The Doomsday Cult Study: When Prophecy Fails Leon Festinger first studied cognitive dissonance in a doomsday cult. He used covert participant observation , and published the study with Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter as When Prophecy Fails (1956). The cult&#8217;s leader prophesied that a great flood would destroy the Earth on a set date. The believers, known as the Seekers, expected a flying saucer to rescue them at midnight. The study asked what happened next. It focused on the most committed members.

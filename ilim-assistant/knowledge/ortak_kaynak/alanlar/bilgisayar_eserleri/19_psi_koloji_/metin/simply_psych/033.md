In one of Timberlake and Allison’s rat experiments, rats in a free-choice situation gravitated toward drinking the sweet solution 60% of the time. They chose the dry solution the other 40%. However, the researchers then changed the schedule. The rats now had to drink the sweet solution at ten times the rate of the dry solution. This forced their dry-solution drinking below its bliss point. That gave the rats motivation to do whatever would let them drink more dry solution, even though they did not prefer it in free choice (Allison, 2019). Deprivation, not preference, drove the behavior. Premack’s principle is therefore a special case. It holds only when the more probable response is also the one the schedule deprives (Timberlake and Allison, 1974). Strengths and Limitations of the Premack Principle The principle has three main strengths. Explanatory Advance: Reinforcement becomes a predictable relation derived from freely measured baselines. This explains why the same reward works for one person and not another. Reversibility and Symmetry: The relation reverses within the same animals (Premack, 1962). Reinforcement and punishment become two directions of one contingency (Premack, 1965). Practical Reach: It works in classrooms (Homme et al., 1963), self-management (Horan and Johnson, 1971), clinics (Dougher, 1983), and developmental disability programs (Allen and Iwata, 1980). Its limitations go beyond those described above. A Special Case: A less preferred response can reinforce a more preferred one (Eisenberger et al., 1967; Timberlake and Allison, 1974). The principle holds only when preference and deprivation coincide. Unmeasured Deprivation: Premack’s manipulations (deprivation, effort, satiation) may themselves have caused effects he attributed to probability. He never measured deprivation independently (Klatt and Morris, 2001). Unstable Rankings: Satiation changes an activity’s rank during the intervention itself, and schedule ratios can decide the outcome (Mazur, 1975). Molecular Versus Molar: The principle analyzes one pair of responses, whereas the equilibrium theory of Timberlake (1980) models an organism’s whole distribution of time across activities. The debate is unsettled. Contemporary Research Contemporary work follows two tracks. One refines the theory of what makes a contingency reinforcing. The other checks whether applied studies that invoke Premack’s name tested what he described. Weighed by the hierarchy of evidence, the theoretical refinement rests on a quantitative tutorial model and a conceptual reappraisal. The applied evidence, once reviewed systematically, is thinner than the principle’s textbook ubiquity suggests. From Probability Ranking to Disequilibrium Jacobs, Morford, King, and Hayes (2017) set out the disequilibrium model as a practical tool for behavior analysts. The model starts from a person’s free-choice baseline. It then calculates whether a schedule forces an activity below baseline (reinforcement) or above it (punishment), and by how much. The model predicts effects the Premack principle misses, including reinforcement by a less preferred activity and reversals when only the schedule ratio changes (Jacobs et al., 2017). Practitioners should therefore reason about deprivation relative to baseline, not preference ranking. Killeen and Jacobs (2016) go further. They argue that a reinforcer is not an intrinsic property of an object but a disposition that depends on the organism’s state and context. As their title puts it, food is not a reinforcer in the way coal is black. Together, these papers suggest Premack’s relational insight was correct but probability was the wrong way to measure it. As a rule of thumb, the original principle still works. In most classroom and household cases, the preferred activity is also the one a sensible contingency restricts (Geiger, 1996; Knapp, 1976). Does Applied Research Match the Theory? Konarski et al. (1980) found little evidence that interventions labeled “Premack principle” had applied reinforcer relativity as Premack defined it. Herrod et al. (2023) addressed this gap with a systematic review of the published applied literature. They judged each study against Premack’s own definition, in which access to an independently measured higher-probability response is made conditional on a lower-probability one. Using the label “Premack principle” was not enough. The review closed with recommendations for practitioners and researchers on applying the principle. That suggests the literature needed methodological guidance, not that it already offered a settled procedure. The concern has not gone away. Maag (2025) reviewed the related high-probability request sequence and reports concerns about how well the intervention translates into practice. A companion guide for elementary teachers makes the same point. Teachers must first identify which activity is genuinely higher-probability for the class (Speer and Maag, 2025). Read together, the two literatures move at different speeds. The theory has become more precise (Jacobs et al., 2017). The evidence for the simpler, popular version of the rule has become more qualified (Herrod et al., 2023).

Key Takeaways Definition: Thin-slicing is judging people or situations accurately from a very brief sample of their behaviour, sometimes just a few seconds long. Key Finding: Ambady and Rosenthal&#8217;s (1992) meta-analysis of 38 studies found brief ratings correlated r = .39 with later outcomes. Length of observation mattered little across studies, though later reviewers call that comparison confounded (Murphy &amp; Hall, 2021). Cues: Judgements rely mainly on non-verbal signals such as facial expression, posture, and tone of voice, not on what is actually said. Limits: Publication bias and unclear accuracy criteria may inflate the pooled result, and high agreement between observers does not prove a judgement is correct. Verdict: Supported with qualifications. Short samples of behaviour predict outcomes on average, but the claim that exposure length hardly matters is less secure. Modern Research: Recent work shows accuracy depends on whether a cue is genuinely diagnostic, and that some snap judgements reflect shared cultural bias rather than truth. Omnivert Light Triad of Personality Solomon Asch&#8217;s (1946) work on central traits made a complementary point from the perceiver&#8217;s side. People do not average information mechanically. They organise it into a coherent whole. One salient trait can colour the entire impression. By the late 1980s, researchers had begun asking a related question: how much do complete strangers agree about someone&#8217;s personality after only seeing them, with no interaction at all? Albright, Kenny and Malloy (1988) found that strangers agreed substantially about traits such as extraversion, and that this agreement was often accurate. Watson (1989) reported a similar &#8220;surprising convergence&#8221; between strangers&#8217; ratings and people&#8217;s own self-reports. That convergence was strongest for observable, expressive traits. It was weakest for internal ones such as neuroticism, which leave few outward cues. Two lessons from this era carry through the whole literature. Consensus is not the same as accuracy. And accuracy depends on whether the trait is actually visible in behaviour. The Cues We Rely On Thin-slicing leans on non-verbal cues, not on what is said. The raw material includes facial expression, eye contact, posture, gesture, and the prosody of the voice: its pitch, pace, loudness, and intonation. Likeability and confidence load onto the same two axes. A single vivid cue, such as a confident tone, can end up colouring the whole impression, the same halo effect that shapes judgements formed over years of acquaintance. Because the process is fast and largely automatic, it is also cue-driven: the impression is only as good as whatever the perceiver happens to notice. When the cues are genuinely diagnostic, thin-slicing is accurate. When a perceiver latches onto something merely stereotypical, such as a babyish face read as incompetent, the same speed becomes a liability. This is why thin-slicing sits naturally alongside the study of heuristics and biases : it trades exhaustive information-gathering for a fast, cue-based guess. The same fast, associative machinery that reads warmth accurately in one moment can misread competence in the next. Confidence is no guarantee it is correct. Why Some Slices Succeed: The Realistic Accuracy Model Thin-slicing research shows that fast judgement can be accurate. David Funder&#8217;s (1995) Realistic Accuracy Model explains why it sometimes is and sometimes is not. A judgement is accurate only if a cue passes four stages: Relevance: the trait being judged must produce some visible cue. Availability: that cue must appear in the sample the perceiver sees. Detection: the perceiver must notice the cue. Utilisation: the perceiver must read the cue correctly, not be misled by a salient but irrelevant feature. This is why strangers judge extraversion well but neuroticism poorly (Albright et al., 1988; Watson, 1989). Extraversion is expressed in behaviour. Neuroticism leaves few outward cues. Zebrowitz and Collins (1997) offer a more perceptual alternative. They argue that expressive behaviour can specify affordances: action-relevant properties of a person that perceivers pick up directly, not step by step. That may explain why accurate judgements arrive so fast and change so little with deliberation (Willis &amp; Todorov, 2006). Ambady and Rosenthal&#8217;s (1992) Meta-Analysis In 1992, Nalini Ambady and Robert Rosenthal set out to test how accurately people can size someone up from just a few seconds of behaviour. The idea builds on Allport&#8217;s (1937) observation that expressive styles are fairly stable. Their meta-analysis remains the field&#8217;s starting point. Aim: To test, across the published literature, whether people can judge others accurately from very brief samples of expressive behaviour. Method: The authors pooled 38 studies (from an initial 44) rating expressive behaviour under 300 seconds long. Each study had a median of 32 targets (range 2–271) and 37 raters (range 2–446), linked to a criterion such as a teacher&#8217;s evaluation. Results: The effect size was r = .39. Slices under 30 seconds predicted about as well as those of four to five minutes. Conclusion: Brief observations carry real, useful social information.

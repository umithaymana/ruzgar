It is the logic behind “eat your vegetables, then you can have dessert.” Key Takeaways Core Rule: Also called the relativity theory of reinforcement and the differential-probability hypothesis, it says a more probable activity (eating chocolate) can reinforce a less probable one (writing an essay). Origins: David Premack established the principle through experiments on rats, children, and later chimpanzees. Reversibility: The same activity can reinforce or be reinforced, depending on which is more probable at the time. Limitations: The principle sometimes predicts wrongly, for example when a less preferred activity reinforces a more preferred one. Response Deprivation: Timberlake and Allison’s (1974) successor theory says restricting an activity below its usual level makes it reinforcing, and it still shapes learning psychology. The Premack principle states that more probable behaviors can be used to reinforce less probable behaviors. For example, a student is more likely to complete their essay (less probable behavior) if they know they will get to eat chocolate (more probable behavior) afterward. For example, a pigeon conditioned to seek pellets would always see pellets as a reinforcer, regardless of their abundance. Premack’s view is different. He located reinforcing power in the response of engaging with a stimulus, not in the stimulus itself. Animals order their responses by preference, and stronger responses will reinforce weaker responses (Knapp, 1976). Because the ordering belongs to the individual, the same activity can reinforce in one context and be the target behavior in another. The Probability-Differential Effect The principle emerged from Premack’s 1959 research on the “rate-differential” or “probability-differential” effect. The effect states the following rule: “any response A will reinforce any response B, if and only if the independent rate of A is greater than that of B.” In other words, a response that happens at a high rate can reinforce a response that happens at a low rate. Independent rate is the key term. What does recent research say about Premack Principle? Response Reduction and Response Deprivation A second effect accompanied the first (Premack, 1965). When a high-probability behavior is made contingent on a low-probability one, the low-probability behavior acts as a barrier. The high-probability behavior then occurs less often, so the chance to perform it becomes more valuable. This is called response reduction. Any response A can reinforce a response B if A is suppressed below its baseline, its independent rate (Mazur, 1975; Timberlake and Allison, 1974). It does not matter which response happens more often. (1967) tested this in people. They had participants turn a wheel and press a bar. Participants pressed the bar more when wheel turning was suppressed below baseline, however much more frequent wheel turning was than bar pressing. Response suppression then evolved into response deprivation, in which a response restricted below its baseline becomes a reinforcer (Timberlake and Allison, 1974). Applications in changing human behavior followed. For example, Dougher (1983) found that response deprivation of a preferred beverage could increase socially appropriate behavior in adults diagnosed with schizophrenia. Access to drinking depended on that behavior (Klatt and Morris, 2001). Examples The principle appears in everyday routines as well as in research. Dessert: A child eats vegetables first, then gets dessert. Grandparents have used this tactic for generations, so it is nicknamed Grandma’s rule. Essay writing: A student is more likely to finish an essay (less probable behavior) if eating chocolate (more probable behavior) follows. Classroom: Nursery-school children earned short bursts of running or chair pushing by first sitting quietly (Homme et al., 1963). Exercise: Adults with developmental disabilities earned access to preferred games by exercising (Allen and Iwata, 1980). Self-control: People used a frequent habit, such as sitting in a particular chair, to cue weight-loss thoughts (Horan and Johnson, 1971). Using the Principle in the Classroom Classrooms were an early test of the principle. Aim: To test whether the principle could control off-task behavior in a real nursery school, using the children’s own preferred activities as reinforcers. Method: Homme et al. (1963) studied nursery-school children whose favorite behaviors included running around, screaming, and pushing chairs. Brief chances to do these were made contingent on first sitting quietly and attending to the board. Results: Making the children’s preferred, high-rate activities contingent on desired behavior produced “near-perfect” control of the class. Conclusion: The principle works in ordinary classrooms. It also reframes misbehavior as a resource: behaviors that are too frequent become reinforcers for behaviors that are too rare. The study’s strength is its real classroom setting and practical ingenuity. Its outcomes were reported qualitatively, so the size and durability of the effect are hard to judge. Geiger (1996) later described classroom use of the principle as balancing a time to learn with a time to play. Language Training in Chimpanzees Premack (1971) used the principle to teach a chimpanzee the basics of language. His seminal article, Language in Chimpanzee? , asked whether apes could be taught language. He saw this as important not just biologically but for the fundamental question of what language is. The study illustrates the principle. Premack used a high-probability behavior (a chimpanzee wanting to eat various fruits) to reinforce a low-probability one (learning the fundamentals of language). Aim: To test whether a chimpanzee could be taught the rudiments of language, using access to preferred fruit to reinforce producing correct symbols. Method: Premack taught a chimpanzee, Sarah, to place metal-backed plastic pieces on a magnetized “language board.” Each piece stood for a word, and trainers introduced them gradually while feeding her. Results: She learned to build strings such as “Mary give apple Sarah,” to answer same/different and yes-no questions, and to use symbols for concepts such as color and size.

Girls face less pressure than boys to conform to gender norms. They are also less likely to receive negative attention for joining &#8220;cross-gender&#8221; activities and games (Wharton, 2005). The most detailed account of how peers do this comes from a long ethnography of children at school. Aim: to understand how school-age children themselves, not only adults, create and enforce the meaning of gender in everyday peer interaction. Method: an ethnography, meaning long-term observation in a natural setting. The researcher spent two years watching fourth- and fifth-grade children in classrooms and on playgrounds at two American elementary schools (Thorne, 1993). Results: children policed and negotiated gender boundaries themselves. They did so through &#8220;chase and kiss&#8221; games, gossip about who was developing physically, and shifting alliances that crossed or hardened gender lines. Conclusion: peer culture is not just a channel for adult-taught norms. Children are active co-constructors of gender, improvising, contesting, and sometimes subverting the categories adults hand them. The close-up method captures how children build gender moment to moment, which surveys and experiments cannot. But two schools in one place and decade cannot show how typical the patterns are. School socialization Teachers and other educators may place expectations on children based on their gender. They could do this by labeling and organizing students in group activities or creating different activities for boys and girls. As with parents, teachers may use gendered language when speaking to and about boys and girls. Small cues can add up. Schools may encourage boys to engage in science or mathematics whereas they may be more forgiving if girls are not succeeding in these subjects. Teachers may also discipline students in different ways depending on their gender, which may reinforce children’s beliefs and assumptions. Schools also teach gender through a hidden curriculum . Seating arrangements, praise, discipline, and subject encouragement can all track gender, even when no formal policy requires it. Usually, nobody intends this. Gender-differentiated treatment persists as an unexamined side effect of ordinary classroom practice. Media socialization Gender socialization through media happens through movies, television, and literature. It can reinforce stereotypes about what it means to be a boy or a girl. Men are often cast as heroes going on adventures. Women, by contrast, are usually outnumbered by male characters. They are often shown as physically attractive and frequently introduced only as the male lead’s love interest. Female characters are often passive, needing to be rescued by men, and dressed to appeal to the male gaze. Children who watch a lot of TV absorb this. They tend to hold more sex-stereotypical views of men and women. This shapes their toy choices, career aspirations, and self-esteem (Burn, 1996). Media portrayal is starting to shift. Even so, much older media still shows women doing housework or as stay-at-home mothers, and men engaged in a wider range of activity. The classic demonstration of how skewed portrayals can be comes from an analysis of the books young children are read. Aim: to document systematically how male and female characters are portrayed in the picture books most used with pre-school children. Method: a content analysis, which systematically counts and classifies the content of texts, of a large sample of commonly used pre-school picture books. It coded the number and activities of male and female characters (Weitzman, Eifler, Hokada, &amp; Ross, 1972). Results: males outnumbered females roughly eleven to one among humans, and by far more among animals. Males were active and outdoors; females were mostly domestic, and no book showed an adult woman with an occupation outside the home. Conclusion: children met a narrow, lopsided model of what each gender does and can do before they could read for themselves. Counting rather than relying on impression makes the finding hard to dismiss. But the sample is now more than fifty years old. Later research using the same method finds the imbalance has narrowed considerably without disappearing. Theories to Explain Gender Socialization Each theory below explains gender socialization through a different mechanism: Social learning: Children copy same-sex models and are rewarded or punished for gender-appropriate and gender-inappropriate behavior. Gender codes: Unspoken assumptions about men’s and women’s proper roles are passed down and renegotiated by each generation. Cognitive development: Children’s ideas about gender change with age, and gender constancy is thought to motivate gender-appropriate behavior. Gender schema: Children use gender as a category for organizing information, and these schemas tend to be polarized. Psychoanalytic: Unconscious identification with the mother is said to shape boys’ and girls’ gender identity in different ways. Biological: Early hormone exposure may nudge children’s starting play preferences, working alongside social input. Social learning theory Social learning theory is most closely associated with psychologist Albert Bandura. It rests on the behaviorist approach, which explains learning through stimulus and response. The theory holds that gender socialization comes from reinforcement. Children are rewarded, or punished, for gender-appropriate and gender-inappropriate behavior (Wharton, 2005). For example, a boy who plays with a &#8220;gender appropriate&#8221; toy such as a football may get positive reinforcement from his father. If he instead plays with a &#8220;gender inappropriate&#8221; toy such as a doll, he may be ignored or met with negative attention. Children are also thought to learn from their same-sex parent. A girl learns what it means to be female by observing her mother; a boy learns what it means to be male by observing his father. Research has undercut the theory on one key point. Parents who show sex-stereotypical behavior are no more likely than other parents to raise children who do the same (Stockard, 2006). The meta-analysis described under Contemporary Research points the same way. It found no detectable effect of parental gender or sexual orientation on children’s gender-typed development (Fedewa et al., 2015). Children are also not the passive learners the theory assumes. They are more active participants in their own socialization than social learning theory suggests (Wharton, 2005). Gender codes Gender codes are hidden, unspoken assumptions about the proper roles of men and women which are transmitted through the socialization process and are negotiated by each generation. Gender codes are not uniform but vary with location. Traditional gender codes tend to be stronger in the working class than the middle class. Still, everyone has access to both conservative and radical gender codes, and must negotiate their own balance between them. Cognitive development theory Cognitive development theories of gender socialization build on the research of psychologist Jean Piaget . These theories treat the socialization process as developmental. Children are thought to play an active role in shaping their own gender identity (Stockard, 2006). Psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg was among the first to apply cognitive-developmental ideas to gender identity. He argued that children’s views of appropriate gender roles change as they age, reflecting their changing cognitive development (Kohlberg, 1966). Younger children hold the most rigid definitions of gender. They are also more likely to punish others for violating gender norms. As cognitive flexibility develops, gender stereotypes and roles become more flexible too. Kohlberg held that once children develop gender constancy, the recognition that their own gender is stable and unchanging, they become more motivated to display gender-appropriate behavior (Wharton, 2005). Children are not just passive learners here. Cognitive development theory expands on social learning theory in this respect. It holds that ideas about gender keep changing as children get older. However, children show clearly gendered behavior as young as two or three. This is well before gender constancy typically develops. It undercuts Kohlberg’s claim that constancy is the trigger for gender-appropriate behavior (Martin &amp; Ruble, 2004). One early test did support part of Kohlberg’s mechanism. Aim: to test Kohlberg’s prediction that children attend selectively to same-sex models only once they have achieved gender constancy. Method: children of different ages were assessed for gender constancy, then tested on their attention to same-sex and opposite-sex adult models (Slaby &amp; Frey, 1975). Results: children who had achieved gender constancy paid markedly more attention to same-sex models than children who had not. Conclusion: gender constancy does seem to raise children’s motivation to attend to same-sex models, which supports Kohlberg’s mechanism over a purely reinforcement-based account. The two findings fit together if constancy sharpens an orientation toward same-sex models that already exists, rather than triggering gender-typed behavior. That is a more modest role than Kohlberg claimed. Gender schema theory Gender schema theory was introduced by psychologist Sandra Bem (1981). She argued that children learn about gender roles from the culture they live in. Cultures vary in this respect.

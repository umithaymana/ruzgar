Everyday Examples of Thin-Slicing These intuitive, natural judgements happen automatically, without any deliberate reasoning. Common everyday examples include: Feeling like someone is untrustworthy based on brief interaction Sensing when someone is angry just from their tone of voice Having an immediate positive or negative reaction to someone&#8217;s appearance Strengths and Limitations Strength: The systematic meta-analytic method and clear inclusion criteria let the findings generalise across many original studies. Landmark Thin-Slicing Studies The meta-analysis shows that thin slices predict. Three further studies show how thin a slice can get, and how far the idea travels. Ambady and Rosenthal (1993): Half a Minute in the Classroom Ambady and Rosenthal (1993) asked how thin a slice could get. Aim: To test whether strangers&#8217; ratings of a few seconds of silent video of a teacher predict the end-of-term evaluations given by that teacher&#8217;s own students. Method: Observers rated three 10-second silent clips per teacher (30 seconds in total) on molar non-verbal dimensions such as warmth, confidence and enthusiasm. Their composite rating was correlated with real end-of-semester evaluations, with physical attractiveness controlled. Results: The 30-second rating predicted term-end evaluations at around r = .76. Clips trimmed to roughly six seconds predicted almost as well, and controlling for attractiveness did not remove the effect. Conclusion: A stranger&#8217;s reaction to a few seconds of silent non-verbal behaviour anticipates the settled judgement of students who watched the teacher all semester. The silent clips rule out lecture content as the driver. A parallel study of high-school teachers, rated by their principals, found the same pattern. One caution remains: student evaluations may reward the same warmth and enthusiasm the observers rated, so both may track charisma rather than teaching quality. Institutions that lean on student ratings may therefore be measuring expressive style as well as instruction. Willis and Todorov (2006): One Hundred Milliseconds Willis and Todorov (2006) pushed thin-slicing to its temporal extreme using static faces. Aim: To find out how quickly people form trait judgements from a face, and whether snap impressions differ from those formed with unlimited viewing time. Method: Participants viewed photographs of unfamiliar faces for 100, 500 or 1,000 milliseconds and rated each on a trait such as trustworthiness or competence. A control group saw the same faces with no time limit. Results: Judgements after only 100 milliseconds already correlated strongly with unlimited-time judgements, with trustworthiness highest at around r = .73. More time mainly raised confidence. Conclusion: Trait inferences from faces form almost instantly, and further deliberation reinforces them rather than revising them. This study measures speed and consensus, not accuracy. A 100-millisecond judgement matching a considered one shows both tap the same fast process, not that either is correct. Static photographs also strip out the movement and voice that carry much of the valid signal in Ambady and Rosenthal&#8217;s studies. Carrère and Gottman (1999): The First Three Minutes of a Marriage Thin-slicing also works on relationships, not just strangers. Aim: To test whether the first three minutes of a couple&#8217;s conflict discussion predict whether the marriage later ends in divorce. Method: 124 newlywed couples discussed an ongoing disagreement for 15 minutes on video. Trained coders scored positive and negative affect second by second using the Specific Affect Coding System, and couples were followed for roughly six years. Results: How much positive relative to negative emotion partners expressed in the first three minutes distinguished couples who later divorced from those who stayed married. Couples who opened with harsh start-up, meaning negativity, criticism or contempt, were far more likely to have divorced. Conclusion: The start of a conflict discussion carries much of the predictive information in the whole interaction. The study grounds thin-slicing in fine-grained behavioural coding, not global impressions. The cautions are familiar ones. The sample is modest for divorce prediction, and models built on one cohort can shrink when applied to new couples. Negotiation, Sexual Orientation and the Perils of Pondering Researchers have carried the paradigm into many other settings. Curhan and Pentland (2007) studied negotiation. Conversational dynamics in the first five minutes, such as activity level and conversational engagement, predicted the eventual economic outcome. Ambady, Hallahan and Conner (1999) found that observers judged sexual orientation at above-chance accuracy from very short clips of expressive behaviour. Accuracy held even for extremely brief exposures. Ambady (2010) drew the practical moral in The Perils of Pondering . Thin-slice judgements come from intuitive processes, so asking people to deliberate or explain their reasoning can lower accuracy. Analysis can disrupt a judgement the intuitive system was making well. Across these studies the pattern is consistent. Brief samples inform outcomes that seem to need far more data, and for some tasks the intuitive read beats the reasoned one. Examples of Thin-Slicing in Real Life Thin-slicing has both power and peril in applied settings. It is an asset where the available cues are diagnostic of the outcome that matters. It becomes a liability when a decision-maker lets it replace a more structured judgement. Practical implications include efficiency for research, selection, training and evaluation that rely on interpersonal judgements. Four settings show the pattern. Hiring and Job Interviews Unstructured job interviews are a natural home for thin-slicing. Not always for the better. Barrick, Swider and Stewart (2010) found that interviewers&#8217; first evaluations of a candidate strongly predicted their later ratings and the job-offer decision. What followed made little difference. A thin slice of the candidate&#8217;s opening manner set the interviewer&#8217;s course before the content could matter. That helps explain why unstructured interviews predict job performance relatively poorly. The remedy is structure. Every candidate gets the same questions and the same scoring rubric, which blunts the pull of a snap first impression. Medicine and Therapy Ambady and colleagues carried the paradigm into healthcare. In the first study, judges rated the tone of surgeons&#8217; voices after the words had been made unintelligible, leaving only the prosody. Voices rated as more dominant and less concerned or anxious predicted which surgeons had a history of malpractice claims (Ambady, LaPlante, et al., 2002). The second study looked at physical therapists. Thin-sliced ratings of their nonverbal warmth and lack of negativity predicted older patients&#8217; functional health outcomes months later (Ambady, Koo, et al., 2002). The pattern is the same in both. The diagnostic information was not in what the clinician said. It was in how they said or carried it, the non-verbal channel that does most of the work across the thin-slicing literature. Courtrooms and Legal Decisions Porter and ten Brinke (2009) apply the same logic to a high-stakes setting. Their framework concerns how judges assess credibility. A judge&#8217;s or juror&#8217;s rapid read of a defendant&#8217;s face and demeanour can produce an early, confident impression of trustworthiness. It then colours how all later evidence is interpreted. The decision-maker experiences this as reasoned deliberation, not as the aftershock of a thin slice. It feels like intuition, so it resists correction by contrary evidence. Dating and Relationships Place, Todd, Penke and Asendorpf (2009) showed observers video clips of speed dates. Clips as short as ten seconds let observers predict a dater&#8217;s romantic interest in their partner above chance. They read men&#8217;s interest more accurately than women&#8217;s. The same logic scales up to established relationships. The first three minutes of a couple&#8217;s conflict discussion forecast divorce years later, as described above. An even thinner slice has been tested. Mason, Sbarra and Mehl (2010) studied a newly separated or divorced adult&#8217;s account of how the relationship ended. Ratings of just the first thirty seconds, for warmth and negativity, predicted changes in psychological adjustment over the following three months. Thirty seconds was enough. Findings like these support brief-observation coding as a low-burden screening or outcome tool in relationship counselling and research. They also caution against over-interpreting any clinician&#8217;s unaided snap read of a couple. Evidence Check: Does Thin-Slicing Hold Up? Verdict: Supported with qualifications.

Ambiguity in the Term &#8220;Dissonance&#8221; Is it a perception (as &#8220;cognitive&#8221; suggests), a feeling, or a feeling about a perception? Aronson (1968, 1999) reframed dissonance as an inconsistency between a person&#8217;s self-concept and their behaviour. Some critics argue that these studies may therefore be measuring guilt or self-image threat rather than a purely cognitive clash. Is Dissonance a Real Drive State? This raises a deeper question: is dissonance a genuine, arousal-based tension, as Festinger claimed, or just a cognitive inference with no real feeling behind it? Zanna and Cooper (1974) tested this directly. Aim: To test whether dissonance is a real state of arousal that people are motivated to reduce, or only an inference. Method: Participants wrote essays arguing against their own views, under high or low choice. Each then took a placebo pill described as making them feel tense, relaxed, or nothing in particular. Results: The usual attitude change appeared with the &#8220;relaxed&#8221; and &#8220;no-effect&#8221; pills. It disappeared when participants could blame their arousal on the &#8220;tense&#8221; pill. Conclusion: Dissonance is an unpleasant state of arousal that people are motivated to reduce, not just a belief inferred afterwards. The most serious challenge to this view comes from Bem&#8217;s (1967) self-perception theory. Bem argued dissonance itself is unnecessary. People lack privileged access to their own attitudes. Instead, they infer them from watching their own behaviour, the same way they would infer someone else&#8217;s. A participant paid just $1 to lie has no other obvious reason for doing so. They infer: &#8220;I must have found it interesting.&#8221; A participant paid $20 simply attributes the lie to the money. The two theories predict the same less-leads-to-more pattern. Attitude data alone therefore cannot tell them apart (Eiser &amp; van der Pligt, 1988). The pill result favours Festinger. Self-perception theory needs no internal tension, so it cannot explain why attitude change vanishes when people blame their arousal on a pill. Fazio, Zanna and Cooper (1977) proposed a division of labour. Dissonance theory applies where behaviour is clearly counter-attitudinal, which produces measurable arousal. Self-perception theory better explains behaviour within a person&#8217;s existing &#8220;latitude of acceptance.&#8221; On this view the two are complementary accounts of different cases, not rivals. Individual Differences Highly anxious people are more likely to show the dissonance effects the theory predicts. Many others cope with considerable dissonance without feeling the tension the theory predicts. Aronson&#8217;s self-consistency revision makes one difference testable. People with high self-esteem have more positive self-regard to protect, so they should show more dissonance reduction than people with low self-esteem. Experiments testing this prediction have produced only mixed results (Harmon-Jones &amp; Harmon-Jones, 2007). Dissonance reduction is therefore not a universal, automatic drive. Ecological Validity and Student Samples Many studies supporting the theory have low ecological validity. For example, turning pegs (as in Festinger&#8217;s experiment) is an artificial task that doesn’t happen in everyday life. Also, the majority of experiments used students as participants, which raises issues of a biased sample . Could we generalize the results from such experiments? Revised Accounts: Inconsistency or Self-Threat? Later work questioned whether inconsistency itself is what feels aversive. Four revisions move the trigger elsewhere: The New Look: Dissonance arises when people feel responsible for a foreseeable, unwanted consequence. Self-affirmation: Reducing dissonance is one way to protect the self, and affirming another value removes the need. Impression management: Some attitude change reflects the need to look consistent, not a private shift. Self-standards: Whichever standard is accessible decides which revision holds. The New Look: Aversive Consequences On Cooper and Fazio&#8217;s (1984) account, the $1 participant feels dissonance not because two cognitions clash. They feel it because they freely misled another person for a trivial reward. That outcome is hard to disown. Dissonance becomes a threat to a moral, competent self, not an abstract logical inconsistency. Self-Affirmation Theory People who first reflect on an unrelated but important personal value no longer need to rationalise a counter-attitudinal act. The usual attitude change disappears (Steele, 1988). Their self-integrity has already been shored up elsewhere. Steele&#8217;s account differs from the New Look mainly in scope: any blow to global self-worth counts, not just one specific unwanted consequence. Impression Management Schlenker (1982) and Tedeschi and Rosenfield (1981) argued that many experiments reflect impression management : the need to appear consistent to others. On this view, the $1 group&#8217;s reported change is real. It is driven by self-presentation, such as avoiding social embarrassment, rather than by cognition. The Self-Standards Model Stone and Cooper (2001) proposed that these revisions can all be correct, under different conditions. Dissonance begins when a person judges a committed behaviour against some standard. Which standard is accessible then decides which revision holds. When a normative standard (what anyone ought to do) is salient, judgement is fairly uniform, so self-esteem should not moderate dissonance. When a personal standard tied to the self-concept is salient, self-esteem-linked effects should emerge. The model can therefore predict in advance which pattern a given experiment should produce. None of these rivals fully displaces Festinger&#8217;s theory. Each narrows the range of cases where an aversive drive is the best explanation. Together they suggest three overlapping mechanisms: inference, self-threat and genuine arousal. How do people resolve the dissonance when two central aspects of their identity conflict? Pitt (2010) studied Black gay Christian men who were strongly identified with their church. How did they deal with anti-gay messages from their ministers? One way to resolve the dissonance would be to change their behavior: switch churches or leave the religion. Many chose to stay. Those who stayed resolved dissonance by focusing on the shortcomings of the minister. For example, they said, “It’s not my religion that promotes this prejudice—it’s the bigotry of this preacher” (Pitt, 2010). Cultural Aspects of Cognitive Dissonance Cognitive dissonance is a universal psychological phenomenon. But the specific beliefs or behaviours that trigger it, and how people resolve it, vary significantly across cultures with different social norms. Heine and Lehman (1997) used Brehm&#8217;s free-choice method with Japanese and Canadian participants rating Western rock and pop CDs. Canadians showed the usual spreading-of-alternatives effect. The Japanese participants did not. Culture shapes how dissonance is triggered and resolved: Group harmony: In collectivist societies , where maintaining group harmony often takes precedence over individual desires, outward dissonance-reducing behaviors may be less visible. Shame: Individuals in these cultures may experience stronger internal dissonance when their actions bring shame or disappointment to their family or community. Vicarious dissonance: Japanese individuals might feel dissonance on behalf of close friends or family who behave inconsistently (Heine &amp; Lehman, 1997). They may then adjust their own attitudes to match. Public choices: Choices made in public, where social evaluation is high, create more pressure for dissonance reduction, especially in cultures emphasizing social conformity. The dissonance still exists in every case. Culture shapes whether it arises, and how urgently people need to resolve it. Contemporary Research Dissonance research has moved in three directions since the classic studies. One strand asks what the &#8220;discomfort&#8221; of dissonance really is. A second strand tests whether the classic lab methods measure it cleanly. A third checks whether deliberately triggering dissonance changes real-world behaviour at scale. Reconceiving the Drive State The most significant modern refinement holds that logical inconsistency itself is not what makes dissonance unpleasant. On Harmon-Jones, Harmon-Jones and Levy&#8217;s (2015) action-based model , our thoughts automatically prime the actions linked to them. In other words, dissonance strikes when thoughts pull toward competing actions, not simply when ideas clash. Discomfort arises specifically when two thoughts imply competing actions. Deciding what to do next becomes hard in that moment. This reframes Festinger&#8217;s original clash of cognitions as a functional alarm. The signal says the conflict needs resolving before a person can act effectively. Logical inconsistency, on its own, isn&#8217;t what hurts. Brain imaging backs this up. Activity in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex tracks dissonance arousal. This region monitors conflict. Ventral striatum activity, linked to approach motivation, tracks its resolution (Harmon-Jones et al., 2015). Harmon-Jones and colleagues treat this pairing of behavioural and brain evidence as mutual support. The neuroscience corroborates the theoretical shift, rather than standing apart from it as an isolated finding. Harmon-Jones and Harmon-Jones (2007) tested the link causally. In one study, students who freely chose to write an essay supporting an unpopular tuition increase showed more attitude change than students given no choice.

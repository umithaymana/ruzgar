Does a child need a same-sex parent to develop typically gendered behavior? This question sits at the heart of social learning theory. The strongest available test comes from a large-scale synthesis of children raised by same-gender parents. Aim: to test whether parental gender and sexual orientation measurably affect children’s gender-typed behavior and identity. Method: a meta-analysis (a statistical method that combines the results of many studies) of 33 published and unpublished studies. It covered over 5,000 children and adolescents raised by same-gender or different-gender parents (Fedewa, Black, &amp; Ahn, 2015). Results: across studies that specifically assessed gender-role development, parental gender and sexual orientation showed no significant effect on children’s gender-typed behavior or identity. Conclusion: this is difficult to reconcile with a strong version of same-sex-modelling theory. Other agents, such as peers and media, appear to do most of the work of gender socialization, largely independent of parental gender. With over 5,000 children pooled, this meta-analysis sits near the top of the evidence hierarchy for this question. It does not show that modelling never matters, though. Children in every family type still meet many same-sex models outside the home, such as peers, teachers, and media figures. The finding suggests parental modelling is not necessary for typical gender development. The pooled studies also mostly use small, volunteer samples. Why Parents Socialize Gender What in parents&#8217; own minds drives their gender socialization? The evidence above describes what parents do, not why. A narrative review of the cognitive and neural processes involved points to parents&#8217; implicit and explicit gender stereotypes, attitudes, and gendered attributions (Portengen, van Baar, &#038; Endendijk, 2023). Other candidates, such as gender essentialism and the motivation to parent without stereotypes, are relevant in theory. They need more testing. Links between parents&#8217; implicit stereotypes and gender-differentiated parenting come from few studies. Evidence on neural processes comes mostly from work on stereotypes in general. The neural model is still a proposal. Parents&#8217; treatment of sons and daughters may therefore depend on their own beliefs, not on a child&#8217;s sex alone. A narrative review cannot weigh effect sizes the way a meta-analysis can. Early Hormones and Gender Stereotypes Early biology adds a further strand. A study of 81 typically developing boys found that penile growth over the first three months predicted later sex-typed play (Pasterski et al., 2015). The link held independently of prenatal androgen exposure. Gender stereotypes also take hold early. Solbes-Canales et al. (2020) gave two tasks to 149 Spanish schoolchildren aged four to nine. Even the youngest children had already absorbed traditional gender roles, especially when assigning masculine attributes. The bias grew much stronger as they got older. Gender Roles in Late Childhood Do children&#8217;s gender-role beliefs track their family and neighborhood by late childhood? Social learning accounts predict that they should. A survey in Rome tested this when primary socialization is largely complete. Aim: to measure how strongly children aged 8 to 11 adhere to traditional gender roles, and whether this varies with family and school context. Method: a classroom questionnaire completed in spring 2021 by 412 children in the last three years of primary school. They came from two Rome districts with different socioeconomic profiles (Cerbara, Ciancimino, &amp; Tintori, 2022). Results: 22.2% showed high adherence to male roles (boys 26%, girls 18%) and 20% to female roles (girls 23%, boys 18%). Adherence to male roles fell with age. Family and environmental variables showed no significant link. Conclusion: children had already internalized traditional gender roles along sex lines by late childhood. Beliefs did not vary with family or district, which the authors link to stereotype-laden primary socialization at this age. Randomly selecting classes is a strength. But the sample covers two districts of one city, so it cannot represent Italy. The data are cross-sectional self-reports collected during the pandemic. The null result for family variables is hard to read. It may reflect coarse measures rather than a true absence of influence. The age trend also compares different children rather than following the same ones. Gender Identification in Autism Is feeling part of a gender group automatic once a child has a sex and a gender label? Autistic people are reported to have higher rates of gender variance (a gender identity that differs from the sex assigned at birth), which makes them a useful natural contrast. Aim: to test whether autistic adults, particularly those assigned female at birth, identify less with a gender group and view it less positively than non-autistic adults. Method: an online survey of 486 autistic and non-autistic people aged 16 to 80 without intellectual disability. They rated how strongly they identified with a gender group and how positively they viewed it (Cooper, Smith, &amp; Russell, 2018). Results: autistic participants reported lower gender identification and lower gender self-esteem. Autistic people assigned female at birth identified less than autistic people assigned male at birth and non-autistic people assigned female at birth. Conclusion: attachment to a gender group is not an automatic by-product of being assigned a sex and learning its label. It differs between autistic and non-autistic people. The comparison groups are a strength. But the design is cross-sectional, so it cannot say what produces the difference. Autism, differing social experience, and few available in-group members are all candidates. The authors offer more than one interpretation. Diagnosis was self-reported and unverified. The sample also showed unusually high rates of gender and sexual-orientation diversity and mental health problems, which suggests selection bias. It is a single, unreplicated study that tests no socialization mechanism directly. The lower scores describe a difference in how autistic people relate to gender groups, not a deficit. How Does Gender Socialization Affect Society? Inequalities in the workplace Gender socialization shapes several documented forms of workplace and economic inequality: Devalued labor: Tasks and behaviors associated with femininity, such as housework and childcare, are often unpaid and unappreciated because they are coded feminine. Employer bias: Because feminine-coded labor is devalued, some employers hold the bias that women are weaker workers, especially given assumed extra labor at home. Wage gap: Women can still be paid less than men for equivalent work, and women-dominated fields such as healthcare are often underpaid relative to male-dominated roles. Motherhood penalty: Mothers can face a workforce &#8220;penalty&#8221; in opportunities and advancement that is not extended to fathers. Official earnings figures give one test of the motherhood penalty claim. Among full-time employees in the UK in April 2025, the gender pay gap was 6.9% overall. This gap is the difference between men&#8217;s and women&#8217;s median gross hourly earnings, excluding overtime, as a percentage of men&#8217;s. It varied sharply by age: Ages 22 to 29: 0.9% Thirties: 3.9% Forties: 9.1% Fifties: 12.5% Ages 60 and over: 12.6% The Office for National Statistics, Gender pay gap in the UK: 2025 (released 23 October 2025) says the gap increases considerably around the age of 40. It adds that this &#8220;may be a result of the impact of motherhood&#8221;. That is the profile the motherhood penalty claim predicts: a gap near zero before parenthood that widens afterwards. The figures therefore illustrate the claim rather than confirm it. They compare all women with all men at each age, so they cannot separate mothers from other women or show that fathers are unaffected. The profile does not by itself identify the cause. Negative attitudes about different genders Children often favor their own gender. This attitude can show up as discriminatory behavior toward other genders. Adults may reinforce this gender-segregated behavior. It can become a problem once children need to function in gender-integrated settings such as school. Children who cannot relate well to other genders may find their differences reinforced rather than reduced. Both boys and girls tend to associate positive characteristics with their own gender at first. Does this hold steady with age? One influential study tested this directly, alongside whether any such belief already shapes children’s own interests.

This risks an overly deterministic picture, in which workers and consumers are passively shaped by rationalised structures. The field has its own answer. De-McDonaldization and individual pushback, covered earlier, make the case already. Farmers&#8217; markets, Costco&#8217;s wage and benefits model, and Merton&#8217;s typology of deviant adaptation all show this. The typology classifies how people respond when cultural goals and legitimate means clash. Standardisation pressure coexists with real, organised resistance. 3. US-Centrism and Generalisability Geography is the last major challenge. The thesis was built from one American case, McDonald&#8217;s itself, and then generalised outward. Critics question how directly it transfers to societies with different retail histories, labour markets, and consumer cultures. Ritzer and Miles&#8217;s own digital-age reassessment implicitly concedes the point. It re-tests the thesis against three genuinely global companies, McDonald&#8217;s, Amazon, and Wal-Mart, rather than assuming the original American case generalises automatically. On balance, the evidence supports McDonaldization as a durable, still-productive diagnostic lens, strengthened rather than undermined by its own originator&#8217;s willingness to keep revising and re-testing it. The strongest caveats concern its falsifiability, and its tendency, applied uncritically, to understate both local variation and people&#8217;s real capacity to resist or reshape rationalised systems. Comparison with Rival Theories McDonaldization does not stand alone as an account of how standardisation spreads. Two other frameworks cover similar ground, and a comparison sharpens what each one uniquely explains. Theory Core claim Relation to McDonaldization Glocalization (Robertson, 1995) Global forms are adapted and given local meaning as they spread. Complementary: McDonaldization describes the rationalised template, glocalization the local meaning around it. Complementary: Braverman explains why firms deskill, McDonaldization the form deskilling takes across many settings. Weber&#8217;s rationalisation thesis Formal rationality increasingly dominates modern society. Extended: Ritzer makes it a four-part checklist and names concrete costs, but critics say this loses Weber&#8217;s nuance. 1. Glocalization (Robertson, 1995) The most direct rival to McDonaldization&#8217;s homogenisation story is Roland Robertson&#8217;s (1995) glocalization . It describes how globally circulating forms are routinely adapted and given locally specific meaning as they spread. Both happen at once, not homogenisation alone. A McDonald&#8217;s outlet in Delhi or Tokyo is not simply a franchise stamped onto an indifferent local culture. Menus, service norms, and the social meaning of eating there are all reshaped by the culture that receives them. In India that means vegetarian menu items; in parts of Asia, the restaurant becomes a clean, air-conditioned social space for young people. Most readings treat them as complementary, not competing. McDonaldization describes the underlying, rationalised production template, while glocalization describes what happens to the cultural meaning wrapped around it once it lands somewhere specific. 2. Labour Process Theory and Deskilling (Braverman, 1974) McDonaldization&#8217;s account of &#8220;McJobs&#8221; draws directly on Harry Braverman&#8217;s earlier labour process theory . Braverman argued that capitalist management systematically strips skill and judgement out of jobs. It separates the planning of work from its repetitive execution. The goal is cheaper labour and tighter control. Ritzer&#8217;s contribution is to extend this deskilling analysis from factory production into services and consumption, and to fold Weber&#8217;s rationalisation thesis explicitly into the account. The two theories complement each other. Braverman explains why capitalist firms have an economic incentive to deskill work. McDonaldization describes the specific organisational form that deskilling has taken, across a far wider range of settings than Braverman himself studied. Braverman traced this dynamic through Taylorist &#8220;scientific management,&#8221; well before the modern fast-food industry even existed. 3. Weber&#8217;s Original Rationalisation Thesis Because McDonaldization presents itself as an extension of Weber, a natural question is whether it adds anything beyond restating Weber in fast-food language. Ritzer turns Weber&#8217;s abstract account into a checklist. That checklist applies directly to a specific institution, far more so than Weber&#8217;s own writing on bureaucracy allows. The &#8220;irrationality of rationality&#8221; also pushes further than Weber&#8217;s iron-cage metaphor, naming concrete costs: dehumanisation, homogenisation, environmental waste, poor-quality standardised goods. Critics reply that this comes at a cost. Operationalising Weber this way loses some of his nuance. It flattens his distinction between formal and substantive rationality. It also loses his sense that rationalisation interacts with religious and cultural values, such as the Protestant ethic, rather than operating as a purely technical, culture-free process.

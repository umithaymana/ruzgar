Attitude Adjustment Another powerful way to reduce dissonance is by adjusting one’s attitudes or beliefs to better align with behavior. Attitudes bend to fit actions. This includes “spreading apart the alternatives”: increasing the attractiveness of the chosen behavior while decreasing the appeal of the rejected options or conflicting beliefs. For example, someone might convince themselves that “living for today” and enjoying pleasures like smoking is more important than worrying about long-term health consequences. This reduces the perceived importance of the dissonant cognition, such as “smoking is harmful to my health.” Evidence Check: Does the Theory Hold Up? Verdict: Mixed or contested. Writing an essay against your own view (a counter-attitudinal essay) shifts attitudes, relative to writing a neutral essay. The classic claim that the shift depends on free choice was not reproduced. The test was the largest and only preregistered one. Whether that reflects a flaw in the classic paradigm, the choice manipulation or the theory&#8217;s account of why attitudes shift remains disputed. Free Choice and Attitude Change Level of evidence: Experiment; failed to replicate. The theory holds that dissonance arises only when people feel they freely chose their behaviour. Without choice, there is no dissonance and no attitude change. The most direct test was a Registered Report, a multilab replication of Croyle and Cooper&#8217;s (1983) Experiment 1 (Vaidis et al., 2024). Its design was approved before any data were collected. Thirty-nine laboratories in 19 countries and 14 languages recruited 4,898 students. They wrote a counter-attitudinal essay under high choice, a counter-attitudinal essay under low choice, or a neutral essay under high choice. After the registered exclusions, 3,822 participants (38 labs) entered the post-essay analysis and 2,724 (37 labs) the attitude-change analysis. The results were: Manipulation check: Participants felt freer to decline the essay under high choice (M = 6.50) than under low choice (M = 4.44), d = 0.77, 95% CI [0.69, 0.84]. Effect of choice: Post-essay attitude under high choice (M = 2.60) against low choice (M = 2.66) gave d = -0.03, 95% CI [-0.10, 0.04]. Attitude change: The same choice contrast gave d = 0.017, 95% CI [-0.071, 0.11]. Essay versus neutral: Counter-attitudinal essays exceeded neutral essays on post-essay attitude (d = 0.26, 95% CI [0.18, 0.34]) and attitude change (d = 0.31, 95% CI [0.22, 0.41]). The earlier evidence looked far stronger, but it was thin. A commentary by members of the replication team audited 104 counter-attitudinal-essay studies (Sleegers et al., 2024). Original experiment: By the replication report&#8217;s account, about ten participants per cell and d = 2.40, 95% CI [1.40, 3.37] (Vaidis et al., 2024). Earlier meta-analyses: Two meta-analyses of the paradigm give d = 0.81 and d = 0.51 (Vaidis et al., 2024). Audited studies: A mean of 16.66 participants per condition (median 13.50, range 4.25 to 70), no preregistered study, and only two that shared data. Power: Sleegers et al. (2024) calculate that 86 per cell are needed for 80% power at d = 0.43. The replication manipulated choice but not incentive. So it does not test the choice-by-incentive interaction of Linder et al. (1967). Cooper (2019) describes that study as a balanced replication in which less-leads-to-more appeared under high decision freedom and not when participants were forced. How Far Can the Evidence Be Trusted? Vaidis et al. (2024) is the most trustworthy work here. Its strengths and weaknesses are both specific: Strong design: Preregistered, with an effect-size-based power plan and a manipulation check that passed. Precise null: The choice interval (d from -0.10 to 0.04 on post-essay attitude) excludes an effect as large as d = 0.20, the effect it was powered for. Constructive replication: It used students and changed the issue, languages and procedure relative to Croyle and Cooper (1983), so it is not an exact rerun. Exclusions: One lab was dropped for attrition above the preregistered limit, and one control condition in one lab was discarded for a materials error. Disputed manipulation: Perceived choice under low choice averaged near the scale midpoint. Commentators argued this left participants moderately free (as reported by Sleegers et al., 2024). Not independent: All ten authors of the commentary also wrote the replication, so the audit and the replication count as one research programme. What This Means for Free Choice Festinger&#8217;s account, as stated above, predicts more attitude change under high choice than under low choice. A self-persuasion account says otherwise. People come to believe the arguments they generate, so attitudes change in both conditions. Equal change is what the replication found. The registered rerun therefore cuts against the choice condition in this paradigm and fits the rival. The advantage of the counter-attitudinal essay over the neutral one shows only that writing it moves attitudes. Both accounts predict that. The result is contested, not refuted. According to Cooper (2019), a co-author of the New Look revision, dissonance requires personal responsibility for a foreseeable aversive consequence. He wrote before the replication. The replication authors replied that nine audited studies reported higher perceived choice in their low-choice conditions than the replication did. Those studies were still read as supporting the theory. If contextual or methodological conditions limit when the paradigm works, they add, the theory needs to state them (as reported by Sleegers et al., 2024). One paradigm, one rerun and one choice manipulation have been tested at scale. The original positive results came from samples too small to estimate an effect reliably. The claim that choice is necessary is not supported as tested. Does Dissonance Drive the Attitude Shift? Level of evidence: Experiment; partly replicated. People who act against their attitudes feel aversive tension. The theory says they reduce it by changing their attitude to match their behaviour. The registered replication reproduced the attitude shift against a neutral-essay control (Vaidis et al., 2024). The effects were d = 0.26 on post-essay attitude (3,822 participants) and d = 0.31 on attitude change (2,724 participants). Exploratory mood-checklist items added a second finding. Both counter-attitudinal groups reported more discomfort than neutral-essay participants, with means of 2.13 for high choice and 2.16 for low choice on &#8220;uncomfortable&#8221;. They also reported more conflict (2.61 and 2.66). Neutral-essay participants scored 1.87 and 1.89 (Vaidis et al., 2024). Within the high-choice counter-attitudinal group, more reported discomfort and conflict went with a less favourable post-essay attitude. The study found no mediating role for either. The authors ran these affect analyses after the primary tests failed. The study was not designed to test the affect process. How Far Can the Evidence Be Trusted? Exploratory: The affect results rest on two single-item ratings, so they are weaker than the primary contrasts. The authors caution against reading them as support or contradiction. Contested reading: The literature disagrees on whether affect should relate positively, negatively or not at all to attitude change (Vaidis et al., 2024). Self-persuasion: The control does not separate dissonance from self-persuasion. The authors cannot rule it out, and it does not require arousal or conflict (Vaidis et al., 2024). Better-powered: Croyle and Cooper (1983) had about ten participants per cell, so the multilab study is the better-powered estimate of the same shift (Vaidis et al., 2024). What This Means for the Mechanism Dissonance reduction predicts attitude change toward the behaviour, with added discomfort. Self-persuasion predicts the attitude change without it. The reproduced shift and the raised discomfort ratings are what both would produce. So the result is compatible with dissonance but does not isolate it. The negative link between discomfort and attitude cuts against a simple arousal-then-change sequence. Being exploratory, it cannot settle the question. The mechanism, as opposed to the shift, is not established by this evidence. Critical Evaluation Decades of research on cognitive dissonance have produced strong support for the theory, alongside several genuine challenges. Before looking at each challenge in detail, here is a quick summary of the main criticisms: Hard to measure: Dissonance cannot be directly observed, so the concept remains somewhat subjective. Ambiguous term: It is unclear whether &#8220;dissonance&#8221; is a perception, a feeling, or simply guilt. Individual differences: Some people show strong dissonance effects, while others tolerate inconsistency with little discomfort. Low ecological validity: Many classic studies use artificial tasks and rely heavily on student samples. Cultural variation: The classic paradigms may not trigger the same dissonance in every culture. Strengths: A Testable Theory With Surprising Predictions The theory&#8217;s main strength is that it can be tested in controlled experiments. It also makes counterintuitive, precisely specified predictions. The less-leads-to-more effect overturned the common-sense prediction that bigger rewards produce more attitude change. Few theories in social psychology have made so sharp a call. The theory has broad applications too. The same mechanism illuminates smoking, consumer choice, group initiation, health behaviour and religious commitment. It shows that people pursue consistency between attitudes and behaviours, even through irrational means.

High schools are also becoming McDonaldized. Efficiency in Administration: To cut costs amid reduced government funding, public colleges and universities are responding to a consumer orientation. Physical Standardization: Student unions are being transformed into mini-malls with fast-food restaurants, video games, and ATMs. Accessibility and Predictability: The &#8220;McUniversity&#8221; may have a central campus but also convenient satellite locations in community colleges, high schools, businesses, and malls . Parking lots will be adjacent to these satellites to make access easy. Standardized Curriculum and Delivery: Course content, requirements, and materials are highly standardized to make courses alike across satellite locations, leading to a loss of the flavour individual professors bring to their classes. Dehumanization of Instruction (Control/Efficiency): The process is dehumanized by relying on part-time instructors who come and go quickly, preventing students from forming deep relationships with more permanent faculty members. Often, courses are delivered remotely by professors televised from distant places , meaning there may be no teacher physically present. High School Rationalization (Application): High schools are also becoming McDonaldized by applying the concepts of efficiency, calculability, predictability, and technology. Hartley (1995) supplies the fullest evidence for this comparison, tracing 1990s reforms in British and American higher education through all four McDonaldization dimensions. It is a documentary analysis, not a study with an independent measure of &#8220;how McDonaldized&#8221; an institution has become. It cannot establish causation on its own. Parker and Jary (1995) reached the same verdict independently. Studying the same period of UK higher-education reform, they argued that audit culture and market-style management subordinate academic judgement to managerial control. The Workplace and Labour (McJobs) The rationalization of labour is a key result of McDonaldization, relating closely to Marxist concepts of alienation : Routinized Tasks (&#8220;McJobs&#8221;): The establishment of routinized roles and tasks in service industries has resulted in the &#8220;discouragement&#8221; of the work ethic. Working at a fast-food restaurant is often referred to dismissively as having a &#8220;McJob&#8221;. Deskilling and Lack of Initiative: McJobs are often unskilled, low-paid, part-time jobs. Workers are trained to perform simple tasks in a predictable manner, doing each action in exactly the same way, with little scope for using their initiative. Scripted Interaction (Control): Workers are often restricted in what they can say on the job. Every interaction with customers is tightly scripted (e.g., &#8220;May I help you?&#8221;, &#8220;Would you like a dessert to go with your meal?&#8221;, &#8220;Have a nice day!&#8221;), as workers are &#8220;no longer trusted to say the right thing&#8221;. Dehumanization: The robotic nature of these tasks and low pay dehumanizes the workers and strips them of incentives for doing quality work. This relates to the &#8220;waning of emotion&#8221; experienced in organizations where interactions are superficial and scripted. Stigma: Fast-food jobs are stigmatized, particularly because they are typically held by people of low social status , such as minorities, teenagers, immigrants, and the elderly. These jobs often demand displays of deference, forcing workers to violate &#8220;macho&#8221; behaviour codes central to some youth cultures. 3. Consumption, Services, and Everyday Life The principles extend to general service industries and daily consumption habits, mainly through efficiency and calculability: The fast-food model is applied to numerous other services requiring efficiency, such as: losing weight (e.g., prepackaged diet meals), lubricating cars (Jiffy Lube), getting new glasses or contacts , and completing income-tax forms . Shopping Malls consolidate many stores, food, and entertainment options into a single location to maximize convenience (efficiency). Catalogues, television shopping networks, and e-commerce provide further efficiency in shopping. Video rentals, pay-per-view cable options, and picture-in-picture TV sets make movie watching more efficient. Computer-graded tests allow faster exam grading (efficiency). Listening to abridged books-on-tape allows for recreational reading while minimizing &#8220;wasted&#8221; time listening to &#8220;insignificant&#8221; parts (efficiency). Health clubs use various exercise machines designed to maximize workout time, often providing radios and televisions to maximize entertainment or news gathering simultaneously. Package tours bus large numbers of people between sites to see as much as possible in the allotted time (efficiency). The widespread use of microwave ovens and the associated development of microwave foods and TV dinners (made possible by large freezers) also prioritize efficiency. 4. Fast-Food and Retail Industry (The Core Model) The McDonald&#8217;s model is the clearest illustration of all four principles. Beyond the examples above, it adds some further detail: Efficiency: Food preparation uses an assembly-line structure, with pre-sliced buns and redesigned meat shipping materials. Work also shifts to customers through self-service drink centers. Calculability: Quantity becomes equivalent to quality. Retailers sell goods by the pound so customers can measure value, and employees use timecards to calculate hours and overtime pay. Predictability: A Big Mac in New York is virtually identical to one in Chicago or Los Angeles. This brings &#8220;great comfort in knowing that McDonald&#8217;s offers no surprises&#8221;. Drugstore chains such as Rite Aid and Walgreens show the same uniformity. Control: Nonhuman technology, such as automated drink machines, directs unskilled cooks. Lines, limited menus, and uncomfortable seats subtly push customers to eat quickly and leave. 5. Healthcare McDonaldization principles show up in healthcare too. Standardised clinical protocols and care pathways pursue predictability across providers, much like a franchise recipe. The drive to see more patients in less time pursues efficiency, and billing codes turn clinical encounters into calculable, reimbursable units. Control matters too. Computerised prescribing and triage algorithms exert it over clinicians and patients alike, echoing the fast-food front counter. Ritzer and Walczak (1988) applied this logic directly to American medicine. They argued that health-policy, insurance, and organisational changes were pushing physicians away from independent judgement and toward externally imposed formal rules. This eroded what had set the profession apart from bureaucrats. The appeal is genuine: consistent, auditable care reduces certain errors. But the same costs recur here as elsewhere. Clinicians have less room for their own judgement, and patients get a more transactional experience. 6. Technology and AI-Driven Standardisation The pattern keeps extending. McDonaldization has gone digital. The paradigm case was once a fast-food chain (Ritzer, 1983). Its logic has since spread from physical retail and service settings into digital and AI-mediated ones. Automated customer-service chatbots, algorithmically generated product recommendations, and content-moderation systems now apply a single standardised rule set. No human workforce could match that scale (Ritzer &#038; Miles, 2019). These interfaces are arguably more rationalised than their bricks-and-mortar predecessors. This is precisely because a frictionless, &#8220;natural&#8221;-feeling interface makes the underlying standardisation harder for a user to notice or resist (Ritzer &#038; Miles, 2019). 7. Dating Apps and Digital Intimacy Mobile dating apps carry the same logic into romance itself. De Ridder (2021) interviewed ten young adults in London about apps such as Tinder, Bumble, and OkCupid. It used an &#8220;existential media analysis&#8221;, not Ritzer&#8217;s framework. Its central finding is a &#8220;mathematical mind-set&#8221; toward dating, a term drawn from Illouz (2012). Dating becomes a commercialised, rationalised process built on predictability, controllability, and convenience. Chance encounters fade. In McDonaldization terms, match percentages, swipe logics, and algorithmic recommendations stand in for calculability and control. A historically unpredictable process becomes a quantified one. Interviewees did not experience this simply as a loss.

Premack (1971) defined exemplars as aspects of words, sentences, questions, and using language to teach language. Others were concepts of class, such as color, shape, and size, and the logical connector if-then. He also taught the copula. It is the word or phrase that links a subject to its complement, such as “is” in “The cat is fluffy.” Trainers gave the chimpanzees different fruits depending on the part of speech each plastic word represented. Two tests then checked whether the association had formed. The researchers found that the chimpanzee knew which word went with each fruit. Next, the researchers changed the fruit donors. Each new donor required a new second word. For example, with Mary present, the chimpanzee had to signal “Mary apple” to receive an apple. With Randy present, it had to signal “Randy apple,” not “Mary apple” or “apple Randy.” Eventually, the chimpanzee could build longer sentences. It constructed “Mary give apple Sarah,” which would result in the chimpanzee getting the apple. It also constructed “Mary give apple Gussie,” which resulted in another chimpanzee getting the apple. Premack (1971) then tested a further hypothesis. It was that the acquisition of language is the mapping of existing knowledge. The researchers placed two items in front of the chimpanzee and tried to teach the difference between the words “same” and “different.” The chimpanzee largely learned it. It could tell items that were the same from those that were different. Premack (1971) was also able to introduce such concepts as color, shape, and size through this reinforcement. Yes-no questions followed. Asked “Is X different from X?”, the chimpanzee was conditioned to answer no. As evidence for reinforcement, the study is compelling: the contingencies clearly drove learning. As evidence about language, it is much weaker. Later comparative psychologists argued that trained symbol use of this kind may reflect conditioned discrimination rather than genuine syntax. Weight Loss Researchers have tested the Premack Principle in a number of clinical applications. In one such study, Horan and Johnson (1971) recruited 96 female undergraduate volunteers. They assigned them randomly to a control group or one of three experimental groups. The experimental groups had three half-hour counseling interviews. Participants identified a specific highly probable behavior (such as “sitting down on a particular chair”). They also thought about a negative-positive pair of associations linked to being overweight (such as “a shortened life span” and “clothes fitting better”). In the Premack condition, the frequent behavior served as a cue for the weight-related thoughts. Such a covert response is called a coverant. Those who used the principle lost significantly more weight than the control group. They also outdid those who only thought of the pair throughout the day (Horan and Johnson, 1971). Supporting Research Reversibility of Reinforcement in Rats Premack (1962) showed that reinforcement can be reversed. Aim: To show that whether drinking reinforces running, or running reinforces drinking, depends only on which activity is more probable at the time. Method: Premack (1962) gave rats a drinking tube and an activity wheel. Some rats were deprived of water, and others were kept from the wheel. He then arranged contingencies in both directions. Results: Thirsty rats ran more to gain access to water. Rats with water but deprived of the wheel drank more to gain access to running. Conclusion: Reinforcement is reversible and relative. Whichever response is currently more probable reinforces the other. The rats acted as their own controls, so no fixed property of water or the wheel can explain the result. The weakness is that deprivation set the probabilities. Deprivation itself may therefore have caused the effect, and the study cannot separate the two. He controlled rats’ baseline rates of drinking (how often they lick a drinkometer) and running (number of wheel rotations). Longer periods of drinking reinforced longer periods of running, and vice versa. In free choice, the rats ranked the activities as follows, from most to least preferred (Allison, 2019): 16% sucrose drink 32% sucrose drink Running with the 18-gram weight 64% sucrose drink Running with the 80-gram weight Aim: To test whether the amount of lever pressing an activity supports rises with that activity’s rank in the rats’ free-choice preferences. Method: Premack varied the sugar strength of the drinking solution (64%, 32%, and 16%) and the weight on the running wheel (18 or 80 grams). Five groups of rats then pressed a lever to gain one of these activities. Results: Lever pressing tracked the ranking. Rats earning a brief run in the heavily weighted wheel pressed about 20 times per 10-minute session, against about 37 for a 16% drink (Allison, 2019). Conclusion: Reinforcing value rises with an activity’s rank in the preference hierarchy, as the principle predicts. The principle also applies to punishment. Premack claims that punishment occurs when a more preferred response is followed by a requirement to perform a less preferred one. For example, a rat drinking the 16% sucrose solution would be punished if it were then forced to run on a heavily weighted wheel. Premack established in other studies that such a punishment system decreases the time the animal spends on the preferred response. So any response can reinforce a weaker response. It can also punish a stronger one (Allison, 2019). Researchers have since criticized Premack’s methodology. He set each activity’s probability through deprivation, so deprivation may itself have caused the effects. He also never measured deprivation independently (Klatt and Morris, 2001). Premack’s study of first-graders Premack (1959) observed first-grade students who had to choose between candy-dispensing machines and pinball machines. He established each child’s preference, and the children were identified as players or eaters. He then separated them into four groups. Each group faced a contingency between the candy-dispensing machine and the pinball response, to test whether the children’s behavior could be modified. Premack predicted, in line with the principle, that eating should be a reinforcer for the eaters and playing a reinforcer for the players, but not vice versa (Allison, 2019). Learning success differed between the two types of children. Only those in groups 1a and 2b became more likely to engage in their non-preferred behavior than before. The other two groups responded on the first machine as often as they had in the pre-learning session (Allison, 2019). The children’s own hierarchy, not the activity itself, decided what reinforced. The same two activities could each reinforce the other. A limitation is that probability was measured as time spent, which blurs how often a response is chosen with how long it lasts. Critical Evaluation The Premack Principle has had wide appeal in fields such as education (Allison, 2019). Even so, academics have supplanted it with alternate theories because of several apparently inconsistent predictions. Often, the Premack Principle predicts learning that experimental data contradict. For example, Timberlake and Allison (1974) noted that a weaker response can sometimes reinforce a stronger one. A stronger response will also sometimes fail to reinforce a weaker one. The principle also ignores the schedule ratio. This is the relative rate at which each response must be performed to gain access to the other. It can decide whether a contingency increases or decreases behavior, whatever the probability ranking (Mazur, 1975). Lastly, some events are not responses at all. Even so, they can moderate the behaviors that follow. Shocks, for example, can punish behavior, but they are not instrumental responses that animals engage in (Staddon and Ettinger, 1989). Response Deprivation Theory In response to these objections, Timberlake and Allison (1974) developed the response deprivation hypothesis. A response becomes a reinforcer when a contingency restricts access to it below its usual (baseline) level. Response deprivation is an element of establishing operations. According to the hypothesis, a weak response can become a reinforcer of a stronger response through deprivation. Many of Premack’s studies involved deprivation. For example, in the pinball and candy-dispensing experiment, eaters in one group were deprived of eating candy by having to play first. To consider another example of the response deprivation hypothesis, researchers can deprive a rat of the ability to run. Even if running is not the rat’s preferred response, the rat will then increase its rate of running above baseline when it is able to run again. The hypothesis modifies the Premack principle. It generalizes that an animal will perform a non-deprived or less-deprived activity in order to be able to do a more-deprived activity. Timberlake and Allison’s (1974) approach rests on equilibrium.

Parents dressing their baby girl in pink and buying her dolls, while dressing their baby boy in blue and buying him toy trucks is an example of gender socialization. Key Takeaways Definition: Gender socialization is how a culture’s gender-related rules, norms, and expectations are learned, from early childhood onward. Early Start: Children are aware of their own gender by around age three, before they recognize other social groups. Sex vs. Gender: Sex refers to anatomical differences between men and women; gender is the socially constructed overlay of masculinity and femininity built on top of it. Key Agents: Parents, peers, schools, and media each shape gender-typed behavior, mainly through the toys, activities, and language offered to a child. Competing Theories: Social learning, cognitive-developmental, gender schema, and psychoanalytic theories each explain gender socialization differently, and each has documented limitations. Modern Evidence: A large meta-analysis found no reliable effect of parental gender or sexual orientation on children’s own gender-typed development (Fedewa et al., 2015). Biology: Early hormone exposure appears to make a modest contribution to average differences in children’s play, working alongside social input (Hines, 2011; Pasterski et al., 2015). Lifelong Process: Gender socialization does not end in childhood; new expectations continue to be learned and renegotiated throughout life. Agents of Gender Socialization Those who teach gender norms are agents of socialisation . By extension, we also talk about agencies of socialisation, such as the family, the education system, and the media. Gender socialization is a form of primary socialization which is the process by which children and infants learn the norms and behaviors associated with their gender. It is thought to occur within the family, peer groups, mass media and school curriculum (Bhattacharjee, 2021). Parent socialization Parents are often the first agents of gender socialization children experience. The process can begin even before birth. Parental language reflects this too. Girls may be called &#8220;pretty&#8221; or &#8220;delicate,&#8221; boys &#8220;strong&#8221; and &#8220;boisterous.&#8221; Children learn from this language how they should be according to their gender. Expectations often diverge by gender as well. Girls may be encouraged toward cooking, shopping, and cleaning. Boys may be encouraged toward sports and manual repair work. Children are especially attentive to same-gender models (Bussey &amp; Bandura, 1999). A child who observes a same-sex parent behaving in gender-typical ways is, on this account, more likely to adopt the same behaviour. Parents may also handle children differently by gender without meaning to. Girls may be encouraged to play quietly. Boys may be encouraged to play rougher. Even parents who consciously try not to reinforce gender norms may pass on their own gendered upbringing anyway (Statham, 1986). This happens inadvertently, without the parent intending it. How much do parents really differ in how they treat sons and daughters based on gender alone? The clearest evidence that adults react differently to a baby purely because of its labelled sex comes from a classic study. Aim: to test whether adults describe and treat an infant differently based on its labelled sex, holding the infant itself constant. Method: mothers interacted with the same six-month-old infant, introduced to groups either as a girl (&#8220;Beth&#8221;) or a boy (&#8220;Adam&#8221;) (Will, Self, &amp; Datan, 1976). Results: when presented as &#8220;Beth,&#8221; the baby was smiled at more, offered dolls, and described as sweet and soft-crying. The same baby presented as &#8220;Adam&#8221; was offered trains and described in masculine terms instead. Conclusion: because only the label varied, differences in adult behaviour could only reflect gender expectations, not any real difference in the infant. Parental gender socialization can begin before a baby shows gender-typed behaviour. The design’s strength is its control: using one infant across conditions rules out differences between real boys and girls. It is also a small, dated, single-setting study. No large modern replication exists, and it cannot show how consequential such early differential handling proves over a child’s later development. To see how much parents differ overall, the best evidence is the largest synthesis available. Aim: to establish how large and consistent parents’ differential treatment of boys and girls really is, across the whole research literature. Method: a meta-analysis, which pools the results of many studies into one combined analysis. It covered more than 100 studies of how parents treat sons and daughters (Lytton &amp; Romney, 1991). Results: parents did encourage gender-typed activity, steering sons and daughters toward different toys and chores. This was the largest, most consistent effect; most other differences, such as discipline and warmth, were small. Conclusion: parents are a real but narrower source of gender-typed socialization than often assumed. The effect concentrates in the toys, clothes, and activities offered to a child. As a meta-analysis of over a hundred studies, it deserves more weight than any single observational study. It is not a controlled causal test, though, because most of the studies it pools were correlational. Peer socialization Children can also be socialized by their peers. This happens in ways that differ by gender. By around age three, children often prefer playing with peers of the same gender (Wharton, 2005).

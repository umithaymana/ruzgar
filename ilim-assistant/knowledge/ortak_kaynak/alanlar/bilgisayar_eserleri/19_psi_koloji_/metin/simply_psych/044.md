For this reason, TERFs often reduce gender down to biological sex differences and do not support the rights of all those who identify as being a woman. What Are The Goals Of Radical Feminism? Structural change Radical feminists aim to dismantle the entire system of patriarchy. They reject adjusting it through legal or social efforts, which they claim do not go far enough. Their reason is that women’s oppression is systemic. It is produced by how society functions and is found in all institutions. They believe that institutions including the government and religion are centered historically in patriarchal power and thus need to be dismantled. They also criticize motherhood, marriage, the nuclear family , and sexuality, questioning how much culture is based on patriarchal assumptions. They would like to see changes in how these other institutions function. Bodily autonomy Radical feminists emphasize the theme of the body, specifically on the reappropriation of the body by women, as well as on the freedom of choice. They want to reclaim their bodies and choose to be able to do what they want with their bodies. They have argued for reproductive rights for women which would give them the freedom to make choices about whether they want to give birth. This also includes having access to safe abortions, birth control, and getting sterilized if this is what a woman wants to do. End violence against women Radical feminists aim to expose the disproportionate violence that women face at the hands of men. Rape and sexual abuse, they argue, express patriarchal power. They must be stopped. Through dismantling the patriarchy and having justice for victims of violence on the basis of sex, radical feminists believe there will be less instances of this violence. Many also argue that pornography and other types of sex work are harmful and encourage violence and domination of men over women and should be stopped. They believe that sex work falls under the patriarchal oppression of women and is exploitative, although some radical feminists disagree with this position. Women-centered strategies A main part of radical feminism is that they want strategies to be put in place to help women. These include: Shelters: Refuges for abused women. Sex education: Better teaching to raise awareness of consent. Women-only institutions: Women-centered social institutions and organizations that separate women from men who may cause them harm. For instance, they may be against having gender neutral public bathrooms as this increases women’s risk of being abused by a man. This is also where TERFs can be critical of transgender people as they do not want them in women-only spaces since they do not see a transwoman as a woman. Consciousness-raising A distinctive radical feminist practice, especially prominent in the second wave, was the small, women-only consciousness-raising group. Women shared experiences of sexism, marriage, and sexuality. The aim was to reveal these experiences as instances of a shared political condition, not individual misfortune. The practice rests on one slogan: “the personal is political.” Experiences normally treated as private, such as dissatisfaction in a marriage or fear of walking home at night, are produced by wider structures of male power. The groups worked as education and support. In the radical feminist view they were also a research method, because patterns across many women’s accounts counted as data about how patriarchy is structured. The Family Radical feminists recognize that there is a disproportionate amount of violence against women, including domestic abuse. In the 1970’s radical feminists labored to reform the public’s response to crimes such as rape and domestic violence. Rape victims were often blamed for their victimization. Radical feminists helped change that, and there is now more justice for victims of gender-based violence. Official figures bear out the claim of disproportion in direction. An estimated 9.1% of females and 6.5% of males aged 16 and over in England and Wales experienced domestic abuse in the last year (survey year ending March 2025). The overall figure for people aged 16 and over was 7.8% ( Office for National Statistics, Domestic abuse in England and Wales overview: November 2025 , released 26 November 2025). The female rate is about 1.4 times the male rate, a gap of 2.6 percentage points. The gap is modest. Even so, 6.5% of males is a large group. The figures illustrate the claim of disproportion. They do not choose between radical feminism and critics who dispute patriarchy as the single cause. The survey cannot show coercive control as a system, perpetrators’ motives, or whether abuse served to maintain male power. Radical feminists are highly critical of the traditional nuclear family, viewing it as a key institution that preserves male power. Exploitation : They argue that the nuclear family exploits and oppresses women. Within the family, men benefit from women&#8217;s unpaid domestic labor (housework and childcare) and &#8220;emotional labor&#8221; (providing emotional support), often giving little in return. Male Dominance : Radical feminists see the family as a patriarchal unit where men control decision-making and resources. They contend that traditional family values reinforce this hierarchy. Divorce and Lone Parenthood : Consequently, radical feminists often view divorce and lone parenthood positively, seeing them as opportunities for women to escape male domination and gain independence. Crime, Deviance, and the Law Radical feminist perspectives have significantly influenced the study of criminology and deviance. Patriarchy as a Cause of Crime : They argue that women&#8217;s crime often originates in their experiences of patriarchy and exploitation. For example, crimes like shoplifting or prostitution may be survival strategies for women with few economic resources. Violence Against Women : Radical feminists place a strong emphasis on crimes committed against women, such as rape and domestic violence, viewing them as extreme extensions of gender inequality and tools used by men to maintain power. They reject the idea that these are isolated incidents, framing them instead as systemic issues. Prostitution and Pornography : Radical feminists often view prostitution not as a choice but as a form of sexual slavery that institutionalizes women&#8217;s dependence on men. They also argue that pornography promotes violence against women and reinforces patriarchal cultural myths. The Legal System : They criticize the criminal justice system and the law as being patriarchal institutions that protect male power. For instance, they argue that laws regarding prostitution disproportionately punish women while ignoring male customers. Key Study: Brownmiller (1975) on Rape as Social Control The single most influential radical feminist analysis of a specific crime is Susan Brownmiller&#8217;s treatment of rape. It repays reading in full. The argument is load-bearing for the whole &#8220;crime as patriarchal control&#8221; claim made throughout this article. Aim: Brownmiller challenged the then-dominant view of rape as an isolated act of individual deviance or overwhelming desire. She argued it is a social and historical institution that targets all women as a group, not one man. Method: A wide-ranging historical, legal, and cultural analysis, not an empirical survey, spanning war, law, slavery, and criminal statistics. It examined how legal codes and social attitudes treated rape, and how its threat shaped women&#8217;s freedom of movement. Results: Brownmiller argued rape is driven by the desire for power and dominance, not sexual desire. Legal systems historically treated it as a property crime against the men who &#8220;owned&#8221; the woman, not a crime against her. Conclusion: Rape is a tool of intimidation, not the act of a few disturbed individuals. In her words, it is &#8220;a conscious process of intimidation by which all men keep all women in a state of fear.&#8221; This makes it a paradigm case for systemic, group-level control. The analysis was groundbreaking. It shifted public and legal discourse away from rape as uncontrollable lust and toward rape as power. It directly influenced law reform, including the eventual criminalisation of rape within marriage. Its central limitation is methodological. As a historical and textual synthesis rather than a systematic empirical study, its claims about universal male intent are difficult to test directly. Criminologists outside the radical feminist tradition emphasise other factors, including individual psychopathology, alcohol, opportunity, and specific subcultures of violence. Critics also argue that treating rape as an expression of all men&#8217;s collective interest risks overlooking the very unequal distribution of actual offending among men. Official figures show the sex pattern the theory predicts. In the survey year ending March 2026, an estimated 2.1% of people aged 16 and over in England and Wales experienced sexual victimisation, including attempts, in the last year. That was 2.8% of women and 1.4% of men ( Office for National Statistics, Crime in England and Wales: year ending March 2026 , released 23 July 2026). The women’s rate is twice the men’s. The measure includes non-contact offences such as indecent exposure. Critics contest the explanation, not the sex pattern. The ratio therefore does not discriminate between the theories. A one-year rate also cannot show whether violence is pervasive over a lifetime. The survey does not record fear, changes in women’s behaviour, or the identity and number of offenders.

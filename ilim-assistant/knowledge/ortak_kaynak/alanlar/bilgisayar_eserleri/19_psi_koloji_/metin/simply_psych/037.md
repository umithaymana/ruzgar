Policy and Moral Criticisms 1. The Problem of Crime Displacement A key critique of RAT’s practical applications — especially Situational Crime Prevention (SCP) — is crime displacement. Moving, Not Reducing Crime: Opportunity-reduction measures (like better locks or more CCTV) may simply push crime elsewhere — to a different place, time, or target — instead of preventing it altogether. Proponents’ Response: Supporters argue displacement is often partial, not total. Rational offenders must re-evaluate costs and risks before acting, and many will decide not to offend at all. Hot spots policing evidence agrees: where measured, benefits spread to nearby areas more often than crime was displaced (Braga et al., 2019). 2. Issues of Morality and Fairness RAT’s practical, utilitarian focus — its “what works” attitude — has raised ethical and political concerns. Ignoring Root Causes: By avoiding discussions of inequality, deprivation, or discrimination, RAT sidesteps the social conditions that generate both offenders and victims. Blaming the Victim: The emphasis on individual “guardianship” can imply that victims are partly responsible for their victimization — for example, by failing to lock doors or avoid risky environments. Policy Misuse: Critics warn that RAT can justify exclusionary or punitive policies, such as “zero tolerance” policing or surveillance-heavy strategies that disproportionately affect marginalized groups. Unequal Protection: Privately funded security — alarms, guard dogs, gated communities — concentrates among wealthier households. Poorer neighborhoods, less able to harden themselves, can end up absorbing a larger share of any displaced crime. Comparison With Rival Theories Criminological theories divide over where the cause of crime lies: the situation, the offender’s psychology, learned behavior, community structure, or the wider economic order. Comparing RAT with each shows what it explains and what it leaves out. Rational Choice Theory: Two Halves of One Picture RAT’s closest relative is rational choice theory . It develops the offender’s side of the cost-benefit picture. That means the bounded, situationally cued reasoning by which a motivated offender decides whether to act once an opportunity exists. RAT supplies the other half. It describes how that opportunity comes to exist, through the convergence of everyday routines. Both theories treat the immediate situation, not the offender’s deeper character, as the practical lever for preventing crime. That is why situational crime prevention blends them. The two are complementary parts of one opportunity-and-decision framework, not competitors. Rational choice theory asks how an offender decides. RAT asks how the chance to offend arises. Neither answers the other’s question. Together they explain why changing a situation can change behavior without changing anyone’s character. Social Disorganization Theory: Structure Versus Situation Social disorganization theory is a long-run, neighborhood-level account. It explains differences in crime rates through the breakdown of community-level informal control. Weak neighborhood ties, high residential turnover, and concentrated poverty are structural conditions. They operate whatever happens on any single day. RAT instead explains the individual criminal event, through the convergence of offender, target, and guardian at one moment. The theories are not mutually exclusive. A socially disorganized neighborhood plausibly has chronically weaker guardianship and closer proximity to motivated offenders. That is exactly the confound raised against Cohen, Kluegel, and Land’s (1981) study above. They differ in level of analysis. One asks why a neighborhood has a high crime rate over years. The other asks why this offense happened here, now. Differential Association Theory: Learning Versus Opportunity Differential association theory explains crime as behavior learned through close contact with people who model and endorse offending. It answers the question RAT deliberately sets aside: where a motivated offender’s motivation, skills, and techniques come from. A teenager who joins a peer group that treats shoplifting as normal, for example, learns how to do it and why it is acceptable. The theory says little about the situation itself. That setting is what turns a disposition into an actual offense. The two theories can be read as sequential stages of one causal chain. Differential association explains how someone becomes a likely offender. RAT explains how that likely offender meets and acts on a specific opportunity. Each is strong where the other is silent. Deterrence and Broken Windows: Other Environment-Focused Theories Deterrence theory shares RAT’s focus on levers that policy can change. Its mechanism is the offender’s anticipation of punishment. That means the certainty, severity, and speed of a sanction. RAT’s “increase the risks” strategy is one application of deterrence logic. It sits inside RAT’s wider opportunity-reduction framework. Deterrence is a partial, punishment-focused special case, not a rival. Broken windows theory also treats the environment as a lever. Its route is social signaling. Visible disorder, such as graffiti or litter, is said to signal that informal control has broken down. Onlookers then become more willing to offend. RAT’s mechanism is more concrete. It runs through the presence or absence of a guardian and the suitability of a target, whatever the environment signals about norms. In practice the two often prescribe the same thing. Better lighting and well-kept public spaces both harden targets and reduce signs of disorder. Their causal stories about why that works still differ. Radical Criminology and Positivism: Where Is the Cause? Radical criminology criticizes RAT for treating economic and structural conditions as fixed background. Those conditions supply motivated offenders and spread guardianship unevenly. On this view, who can afford alarms and gated communities is no accident. It is a product of the same capitalist inequality that radical criminologists treat as crime’s deeper cause. The older positivist tradition sits at the other pole. Biological theories of crime , such as Lombroso’s born criminal, located the cause inside the offender’s fixed biological makeup. RAT stands with the classical and rational choice traditions on the free-will side of criminology’s oldest divide. Like lifestyle-exposure theory, it places the explanation entirely outside the offender, in the situation. It makes no claim about offenders’ internal traits.

The claim that it hardly matters is much less secure. It comes from a between-study comparison that later reviewers describe as confounded. Slice-length studies that came afterwards give a more mixed picture. Do Brief Samples Predict Outcomes? Level of evidence: Meta-analysis or systematic review; not yet replicated. The claim is simple. Ratings of expressive behaviour sampled for under five minutes predict independent outcomes. Examples are a teacher&#8217;s evaluations, a clinical outcome or a judgement of deception. It should hold across laboratory and field settings. Ambady and Rosenthal (1992) found a mean effect of r = .39 across 38 results. Accuracy did not vary significantly between laboratory behaviour and more naturally occurring behaviour. Nor did it vary across clinical, social or deception research. Murphy and Hall (2021) credit the paper with showing that thin slices can predict other variables. The later work Murphy and Hall review asks a different question: how well a slice represents the whole interaction. It is not a rerun of the 1992 synthesis. The evidence supports the claim as an average, not as a rule for any single snap judgement. An r = .39 is a moderate association. The criteria were often other people&#8217;s judgements, such as evaluations by students, supervisors or clinicians. A correlation can therefore partly reflect shared charisma. A rival account in which perceivers merely share a stereotype would predict the same agreement. The claim is real but moderate, and its explanation is open. The design is the top rung of the evidence ladder. Even so, the abstract reports no publication-bias test and no bias-corrected estimate. Murphy and Hall (2021) also note that correlations with outcomes speak to predictive validity, not accuracy. Does a Longer Exposure Improve Accuracy? Level of evidence: Meta-analysis or systematic review; not yet replicated. The claim is that judgements from slices under 30 seconds are about as accurate as those from slices of several minutes. So more behaviour does not mean more accuracy (Ambady &amp; Rosenthal, 1992). The 1992 meta-analysis reported the same. Predictions based on observations under half a minute did not differ significantly from those based on four and five minutes. A later meta-analysis (Hall et al., 2008), as summarised by Murphy and Hall (2021), covered exposures from under 1 second to 45 minutes. It pooled interpersonal accuracy studies and found no evident trend between slice length and judgement accuracy. Within single studies the picture is more varied. Carney et al. (2007) compared slices of 5, 20, 45, 60 and 300 seconds for Big Five judgements. Accuracy rose significantly with length for extraversion and conscientiousness, and marginally for agreeableness. Neuroticism and openness showed no linear trend. Accuracy was above chance at 5 seconds for every trait except agreeableness. The gain from 60 to 300 seconds was not substantive. Ambady et al. (1999) found that accuracy of judging sexual orientation was significantly greater for 10-second than for 1-second clips. Ambady and Rosenthal (1993) compared 2, 5 and 10-second excerpts of teacher behaviour. Correlations were stronger for longer slices but did not differ significantly from the shorter ones. The claim holds in a weaker form. Both meta-analyses are between-study comparisons, and Murphy and Hall (2021) describe both as confounded. Slice length in the 1992 data was confounded with other study variables, such as sample characteristics. The later meta-analysis varied widely in the construct judged. So very short slices are informative, but length is not irrelevant, and the answer depends on the trait. More exposure buys comparatively little, not nothing. The most direct evidence is Carney et al. (2007), because it varies length within one set of targets. We know its figures only through Murphy and Hall&#8217;s review, and it covers Big Five traits only. Critical Evaluation The meta-analytic finding above is well supported, but it is not the whole story. Later research has sharpened when a thin slice can be trusted, and some of the strongest cautions concern the same pooled accuracy score the 1992 meta-analysis reports. The case for thin-slicing rests on more than one design. A classroom study (Ambady &amp; Rosenthal, 1993) and a marital-conflict study (Carrère &amp; Gottman, 1999) point the same way as the meta-analysis. A large pre-registered cross-cultural study has also probed the face-based variant (Jones et al., 2021). Few effects in social psychology can show that range of independent designs. Agreement between observers is not the same as being right. Two perceivers can share an identical judgement and still both be wrong, if they are reading the same superficial cue rather than something real about the person. A related problem is criterion contamination: the outcome a study predicts is not always independent of what the thin slice measures. A supervisor&#8217;s rating, for example, is itself a human judgement, so it can share the very biases the thin-slice raters are being tested against. Contemporary Research The clearest test of how far these rapid judgements generalise comes from a large multi-laboratory replication led by Jones and colleagues (Jones et al., 2021), run through the Psychological Science Accelerator. Aim: To test whether the influential two-dimensional model of face evaluation, built mainly on Western samples, replicates and generalises across world regions. Method: A pre-registered study across dozens of laboratories collected face-trait ratings from more than 11,000 participants in 41 countries, using the original study&#8217;s modelling. Results: The original two-dimensional structure held up well in the region it was built on, but fit only partially in other world regions. Conclusion: Snap face judgements are structured and reproducible, but their underlying architecture is shaped by culture rather than being universal. The result does not overturn the case for thin-slicing built on dynamic behaviour. Jones and colleagues tested static faces, not the expressive movement, gesture, and voice that carry most of the signal in Ambady and Rosenthal&#8217;s own studies. It is not a culture-free read of character. The broader lesson is the same one attribution research has long made about brief behaviour. Treating a single thin slice as proof of someone&#8217;s stable character risks the same mistake the fundamental attribution error describes: mistaking a brief sample of behaviour for an enduring trait. Most thin-slicing research also draws on Western, often North American, samples, so caution about cultural generalisation likely extends beyond faces to the behavioural thin slices this article covers. When Snap Judgements Mislead Face-based impressions show how thin-slicing can fail. Todorov, Mandisodza, Goren and Hall (2005) studied rapid competence judgements of political candidates. Each judgement came from a one-second glance at a photograph, with no information about policy or record. Even so, the judgements predicted the winners of roughly 70% of U.S. Senate races. The judgement was influential. But there is no evidence it was valid, because facial competence does not predict actual competence in office. Thin-slicing here reveals a bias in voters, not a hidden truth about candidates. Todorov, Olivola, Dotsch and Mende-Siedlecki (2015) reached a broader conclusion. Face-based impressions show strong consensus but contain, at best, only &#8220;small kernels of truth.&#8221; Most are overgeneralisations: the mind reads cues for emotion, age or fitness as if they signalled stable character. So high agreement is not evidence of accuracy. How Thin Can a Slice Be? A second strand of recent work asks how thin a slice can be before the measurement becomes unreliable. Murphy, Hall and Schmid Mast (2015) coded six nonverbal behaviours across repeated slices of the same longer interactions: gaze, gestures, nods, self-touch, smiles and postural shifts. They then asked how well one slice predicted another. Reliability was uneven. Gaze and nods agreed strongly between slices, while idiosyncratic behaviours such as self-touch were noisier. A slice of roughly 1.5 minutes was generally needed before a behaviour&#8217;s true level was adequately represented. Murphy et al. (2019) asked whether trimming a slice costs predictive validity, not just reliability. The answer was a qualified yes. In their five studies, one-minute slices lost some predictive power compared with full five-minute codings. Combining the first two one-minute segments recovered nearly all of it for five of the six behaviours. Murphy and Hall&#8217;s (2021) review separates three questions that earlier research tended to run together. Does a slice represent the whole interaction? Does it predict an outside outcome? Does judgement accuracy change with slice length? These properties need not move together, so a single pooled correlation cannot tell them apart. Ambady and Rosenthal&#8217;s finding on exposure length is better read as a claim about judgement accuracy specifically than as a fixed law. Target, Perceiver and Situation The newest methodological work stops pooling everything into one accuracy score. Zero-acquaintance studies have begun to model how much of a judgement comes from the target, the perceiver and the situation (Jiang et al., 2023). This answers a long-standing complaint. Most studies watch perceivers judge targets in a single context, then generalise as if the result were context-free. Funder&#8217;s (1995) model, described earlier, points the same way.

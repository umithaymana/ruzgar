Cross-Cultural Doubt: Malinowski (1929) questioned whether the family triangle Freud treated as universal takes the same shape outside the patriarchal nuclear family. Karen Horney&#8217;s Critique of Penis Envy One of the earliest critics of Freud&#8217;s Electra complex was Karen Horney . She believed that penis envy, the concept underlying the Electra complex, was both inaccurate and demeaning to women. Instead, Horney traced female psychiatric disturbance to the male-dominated culture that produced Freudian theory. She countered with a concept of her own: womb envy. Womb envy suggested that men envy women&#8217;s ability to bear children (Vanacore, 2020). Horney&#8217;s early-20th-century contemporaries, mostly male psychoanalysts, largely dismissed her. Freud saw this objection coming. A footnote to Female Sexuality anticipates it. Feminist and women analysts, he wrote, would blame the male analyst&#8217;s own &#8220;masculinity complex.&#8221; He called the charge a &#8220;knife that cuts both ways&#8221; (Freud, 1931). Her break with drive theory led to her 1941 disqualification as a training analyst at the New York Psychoanalytic Institute. She resigned and founded her own association (Vanacore, 2020). She argued that penis envy, where it appeared, was a defence against women&#8217;s real social subordination, not their true nature. Freud answered her in print. Clara Thompson: Envy of Privilege, Not Anatomy Clara Thompson pushed the cultural argument further than Horney did. In &#8220;Penis Envy&#8221; in Women (1943), she argued that penis envy is really envy of men&#8217;s social position (Thompson, 1943). It reflects the freedoms and status a patriarchal society hands to men, not to women. On this view, the &#8220;biological&#8221; story built into the Electra complex dissolves into a social fact. Society, not anatomy, produced the pattern Freud described. Thompson&#8217;s reading anticipates a later distinction between sex, the biological category, and gender , the social role built on it. A complex that reflects gender inequality should fade as that inequality does, which a truly universal stage of development could not do. Like Horney&#8217;s, her critique shows how a weak theory can still spark useful debate. Nancy Chodorow: Feminine Identity Without Rupture Nancy Chodorow inverted the theory&#8217;s central asymmetry in The Reproduction of Mothering (1978). Because mothers do most of the early caregiving, she argued, a girl&#8217;s route to gender identity is continuous. She becomes feminine through her unbroken relationship with her mother, with no anatomical crisis required (Chodorow, 1978). On this account, the boy faces the real rupture. He must define his masculinity negatively, by separating from the mother he was first attached to. Feminine identification is not, on this reading, a consolation prize for a failed masculinity, as the penis-envy account implies. It is the default outcome of an unbroken bond. Chodorow&#8217;s relational account reshaped how later clinicians think about mother-daughter and father-daughter relationships. What are the two systems of the superego? What are Object Relations? What is Theory of Neurosis? Criticism by Sigmund Freud The Electra Complex is not originally a Freudian concept. Freud&#8217;s stages of psychosexual development were largely based on his interactions with his male patients. Freud did write about girls, under the name feminine Oedipus complex, but he described a different process from the boy&#8217;s (Freud, 1925). Freud, however, denied any true symmetry between the sexes: &#8220;It is only in the male child that we find the fateful combination of love for the one parent and simultaneous hatred for the other as a rival&#8221; (Freud, 1931). Freud&#8217;s reasoning rested on a discovery that surprised him. A girl&#8217;s first and longest attachment, he found, is to her mother, not her father. In the boy, castration anxiety destroys the Oedipus complex. In the girl, Freud argued, the castration complex creates it instead (Freud, 1931). This asymmetry explains why Freud called the girl&#8217;s Oedipal turn a secondary formation, not a starting point. She reaches it only by an indirect path out of the castration complex (Freud, 1931). Contemporary Research Modern developmental science has now tested the Electra complex&#8217;s core assumptions directly. The results do not support the theory. Aim: To test whether young children&#8217;s gender identity tracks their sex assigned at birth, as the Electra complex requires, or their own asserted identity. Method: Gülgöz and colleagues (2019) studied 317 transgender children aged 3 to 12, 189 cisgender siblings and 316 matched controls, measuring gender identity and gender-typed preferences. Results: Transgender children identified with their current gender, and showed matching gender-typed preferences, as strongly as cisgender children did. The three groups&#8217; patterns were essentially indistinguishable. Conclusion: Early sex assignment and rearing do not always determine how a child later identifies or expresses gender (Gülgöz et al., 2019). Where identity and rearing diverge, identity wins. This finding is robust. The study is large for such a rare population and includes a sibling comparison that holds family environment constant. It is correlational, though, and the families involved had already chosen early transition for their children. The theory&#8217;s timing is also wrong. Children typically label their own gender and show gender-typed preferences by their third year of life. That is well before the phallic stage in which the Electra complex is supposed to forge feminine identity. The theory&#8217;s other testable claim has also failed. Freud predicted that girls form weaker consciences than boys. Hoffman&#8217;s (1975) analysis of moral development found the opposite: where sex differences appeared, girls showed more guilt after wrongdoing and relied less on outside supervision. Kochanska et al. (2002) tested this directly. They observed 106 children. At 22, 33 and 45 months, each was led to believe they had damaged a valuable object. Girls showed more guilt at the last two assessments. Guilt was higher in fearful children and lower when mothers relied on power-assertive discipline. On this evidence, conscience grows from temperament and the parent-child relationship, not from phallic-stage rivalry. Examples of the Electra Complex Today the term has almost no clinical use. No current diagnostic system or evidence-based therapy is built around detecting or resolving an Electra complex. Modern psychodynamic practice approaches father-daughter dynamics through attachment and relational frameworks instead (Bowlby, 1973; Chodorow, 1978). The examples below show where the term survives. LSD-Assisted Psychotherapy Cases (Kafkalides, 1996) Despite wide discrediting for its lack of evidence, some neo-Freudians still use the Oedipus and Electra Complexes to describe their patients&#8217; experiences. One example is Athanassio Kafkalides&#8217;s 1996 paper on LSD-assisted psychotherapy. Researchers began using LSD as a psychiatric drug in the 1960s, before its use was later criminalized. Kafkalides believed his patients&#8217; fixations traced back to the mother and a desire to return to the womb. For both patients, he believed, sexuality was a way of approaching this desire as closely as possible (Kafkalides, 1996). Florence Rush: The Freudian Cover-Up The feminist author Florence Rush argued that Freud&#8217;s Oedipus Complex, and Jung&#8217;s counterpart the Electra Complex, functioned as a cover-up. Both, she said, concealed reports of sexual abuse and incest by patients&#8217; paternal figures (Rush, 1996). Rush traced the reversal in four steps: The Link: Freud initially drew a causal link between sexual abuse and his female patients&#8217; neurosis, since many reported such abuse (Rush, 1996). The Asymmetry: His male patients never complained of maternal seduction. Rush argued that Freud therefore treated the abuse reports as female-specific fantasy: the Oedipus complex in women. The Discomfort: His own theory implied that sexual abuse of women was extremely widespread, and Rush believed Freud grew uncomfortable. The Recasting: Women&#8217;s accounts of sexual assault became, in his theory, unresolved Oedipal complexes and fantasies of sex with their fathers. Rush calls this the Freudian cover-up (1996). Freud&#8217;s own words show the reversal. In 1933 he recalled: &#8220;almost all my women patients told me that they had been seduced by their father.&#8221; In the end, he said, &#8220;these reports were untrue.&#8221; Hysterical symptoms, he concluded, come from phantasies, not real events (Freud, 1933).

Organisations and the Workplace Liberal feminist writers such as Rosabeth Moss Kanter (1977) argued that women&#8217;s disadvantage in corporations was a problem of unequal access to existing power structures. This was soluble, in principle, by promoting more women into them. Political theorist Kathy Ferguson took a different, radical feminist line in The Feminist Case Against Bureaucracy (1984). She argued that bureaucratic organisation itself is a patriarchal form. Bureaucracy structures &#8220;feminine&#8221; subordinate roles, such as compliance, deference, and emotional labour, into its lower rungs regardless of who occupies them. Simply placing more women in management does not remove this underlying gendered logic, Ferguson argued. Only dismantling bureaucratic domination itself would work. This dispute is a direct instance of the wider disagreement between radical and liberal feminism. Is placing more women in institutions enough, or must the institutions themselves be restructured? Criminal Justice Reform and Activism Radical feminist campaigning fed directly into criminal-justice reform from the 1980s onward. Three reforms stand out: Police training: Specialist training for handling rape complainants. Rape suites: Dedicated rooms designed to be less intimidating than a standard police interview room. Marital rape: The eventual criminalisation of rape within marriage. The last change followed centuries of English law holding that a husband could not be guilty of raping his wife. The reasoning: she had irrevocably consented to intercourse for the duration of the marriage. Legal reform was not the whole story. Activism mattered too. Radical feminist ideas generated collective action well beyond the second wave. &#8220;Take Back the Night&#8221; marches reclaimed women&#8217;s freedom to occupy public space at night. Later demonstrations against victim-blaming drew on this same analysis of rape as systemic, not incidental. Support followed. Rape crisis centres, domestic violence shelters, and women&#8217;s health collectives were founded directly by radical feminist activists in the 1970s, as alternatives to patriarchal institutions. Many remain part of the social service landscape in most Western countries today. This is one of the tradition&#8217;s most concretely institutionalised legacies. Criticisms Critiques of radical feminism differ depending on the theoretical standpoint from which they originate. Radical feminists identify patriarchy as the fundamental cause of women&#8217;s oppression and the primary social division. Sociologists and theorists from Marxist, functionalist, multicultural, and poststructuralist perspectives challenge this view. Seven main criticisms follow, in this order: Essentialism and race: A universal “sisterhood” centres white, middle-class women and sidelines women of colour. Capitalism and class: Marxist and socialist feminists say it stresses patriarchy at the expense of economic factors. The family: Functionalists and New Right thinkers say it ignores the positive sides of family life. Criminology: It focuses on male offending against women and ignores female criminality. Men and masculinity: It implies all men share the same power and oppress all women. Poststructuralism: Categories like “woman” and “man” are constructed, not fixed or essential. Universal patriarchy: Treating patriarchy as universal risks biological reductionism. Essentialism and the Neglect of Race and Ethnicity A primary criticism levelled against radical feminism is that it tends to be essentialist and ethnocentric. Radical feminism posits a universal &#8220;sisterhood&#8221; based on shared gender oppression. Critics argue this focuses on the experiences of white, middle-class, heterosexual women. It ignores the distinct experiences of women of color. Multicultural Feminism: Critics raise a narrower charge too. Radical feminism, they argue, fails to account for the intersection of race, class, and gender. Multicultural feminists reject a purely patriarchy-based account. Race, class, and gender interact, they argue, to create a &#8220;triple burden&#8221; for women of color. Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989) argued that the experiences of African American women are shaped by forces that oppress them simultaneously, not one after another. Their status cannot be understood by simply adding racism to sexism, because the oppression is multiplicative . Postcolonial Critique: Postcolonial theorists raise a related objection. Chandra Talpade Mohanty (1988) criticised Western feminists for constructing a monolithic &#8220;Third World Woman&#8221;, portrayed as uniformly poor, victimised, and traditional against a &#8220;liberated&#8221; Western woman. This framing obscures the specific cultural and historical contexts of women in the Global South. It also overlooks their agency as political actors in their own right. Neglect of Capitalism and Class Marxist and socialist feminists criticise radical feminism for overemphasizing patriarchy at the expense of economic factors. Capitalism vs. Patriarchy: While radical feminists view patriarchy as the root of oppression, socialist feminists argue that women&#8217;s oppression results from dual roles as paid and unpaid workers within a capitalist economy (Hartmann, 1979). They argue that radical feminism ignores how capitalism benefits from women&#8217;s unpaid domestic labour and low wages (Delphy, 1984). Economic Determinism: From a traditional Marxist perspective, radical feminism is criticised for failing to recognise that the roots of conflict are often economic (class-based) rather than purely patriarchal. Marxists argue that ending capitalism is the necessary step to achieve gender equality, a point radical feminists dispute by arguing that patriarchy exists even in non-capitalist societies. Criticisms Regarding the Family Radical feminists view the nuclear family as a key site of oppression where men exploit women’s labour and dominate decision-making. This view faces opposition from Functionalists and New Right thinkers. Ignoring Positive Aspects: Critics argue that radical feminists ignore the positive aspects of family life and the satisfaction many women derive from their roles as wives and mothers. Functionalists maintain that the family provides essential emotional support and stability for adults (the &#8220;warm bath&#8221; theory) and that the division of labour is functional rather than purely exploitative. Devaluing Choice: Critics suggest that by characterizing traditional roles solely as oppressive, radical feminists may devalue the choices of women who prioritize family and domestic life. Some research indicates that women may find satisfaction in &#8220;self-sacrifice&#8221; for their families, which radical feminists dismiss as &#8220;false pleasure&#8221; or the result of oppression. Criminology and Victimhood In the field of criminology, radical feminist perspectives have been critiqued for their characterisation of female behaviour and the legal system. Gender-Blindness in Reverse: While seeking to highlight crimes against women, radical feminist criminology has been criticised for focusing excessively on male criminality against women (such as rape and domestic violence) while ignoring female criminality. Essentialism in Crime: Critics argue that some radical feminist approaches imply that men are naturally aggressive and women are naturally passive victims. This biological determinism is challenged by evidence that women can also be violent and that social conditions (poverty, racism) play a massive role in victimization and offending for both sexes. Prostitution: Radical feminists often view prostitution as a form of sexual slavery and institutionalised dependence on men. This view is criticised by those who argue that for some women, sex work may be a rational economic choice or survival strategy within a capitalist system, rather than solely an expression of patriarchal dominance. Views on Men and Masculinity Radical feminism is often perceived as portraying men solely as the enemy. Universal Male Oppression: Critics argue that radical feminism implies all men share the same power and oppress all women. This overlooks the fact that many men (e.g., working-class men or men of color) are also oppressed by the capitalist or colonial systems and do not possess the same patriarchal &#8220;dividend&#8221; or power as elite white men. Crisis of Masculinity: Some sociologists argue that the rise of feminism and the decline of traditional male jobs have led to a &#8220;crisis of masculinity,&#8221; leaving men insecure and uncertain of their role, which radical feminism fails to adequately address. Poststructuralist and Postmodernist Critiques Poststructuralists challenge the very categories radical feminists rely on. Fixed Identities: Poststructuralists and postmodernists argue that categories like &#8220;woman&#8221; and &#8220;man&#8221; are not fixed or essential truths but are socially and linguistically constructed. They criticise radical feminism for relying on essentialist notions of &#8220;womanhood&#8221; that do not account for the fluidity of identity and the differences among women. They suggest that there is no single &#8220;truth&#8221; of female experience. Is Patriarchy Really Universal? A more specific version of the essentialism critique targets the concept of patriarchy itself. Radical feminists have generally treated patriarchy as a phenomenon that has existed across virtually all historical periods and cultures. Critics argue this leaves too little room for genuine historical and cross-cultural variation in how, and how much, women are subordinated.

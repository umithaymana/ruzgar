Critics use them to read fictional characters, though usually detached from Freud&#8217;s ideas about repression and neurosis. One example stands out. Muhammad Shoaib (2014) applied the complex to Shakespeare&#8217;s Twelfth Night . He read Viola and Olivia as driven by a desire to possess their brothers, who served as father figures for them. Both, he argued, represent the phallic and post-Oedipal stages of Freud&#8217;s psychosexual development. Their goal was to replace and imitate their mothers. Each girl first idealized her father figure, then redirected that wish toward emulating her mother by winning an ideal father of her own and having his child. Writers after Freud have used the term knowingly. Sylvia Plath, for instance, described her poem &#8220;Daddy&#8221; as spoken by a girl confronting an unresolved Electra complex in the wake of her father&#8217;s early death (Plath, 1981). Jill Scott&#8217;s Electra after Freud: Myth and Culture (2005) traces how the Electra myth, sidelined by Freud himself, became a resource for twentieth-century literature&#8217;s alternatives to the Oedipal template. History and Overview The Electra Complex is the female equivalent of the Oedipus Complex , a term in neo-Freudian psychology . It describes a daughter developing love for her father, alongside jealousy of and blame toward her mother for supposedly depriving her of a penis. Origins in Greek Myth The name has a literary source. The Electra Complex derives from Greek myth, dramatised by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides. Electra was the daughter of King Agamemnon and Clytemnestra. When her father was murdered, she persuaded her brother Orestes to help her kill her mother and her mother&#8217;s lover, Aegisthus, in revenge. Jung chose the myth because it captured a daughter&#8217;s loyalty to her father and hostility to her mother in one figure. He later explored Greek myth&#8217;s psychological patterns further with the mythologist Karl Kerenyi (Jung &amp; Kerenyi, 1963). The myth fits the theory only loosely, though. Electra is an adult grieving a real murder, not a small child living out a fantasy. Critics have long argued the analogy was too loose. The label, they say, always implied more symmetry than the theory delivers. Jung&#8217;s Term and Freud&#8217;s Rejection Carl Jung introduced the term in his Fordham University lectures, delivered in 1912 and published in 1913. He argued that a daughter develops &#8220;a specific liking for the father, with a correspondingly jealous attitude towards the mother&#8221; (Jung, 1913). Freud, however, never used the label. Freud spoke instead of the feminine Oedipus complex, his own term for the girl&#8217;s version of the family drama. Freud disagreed with Jung&#8217;s choice of name. In a 1920 footnote, he rejected the label bluntly: &#8220;I do not see any advance or gain in the introduction of the term &#8216;Electra complex&#8217;, and do not advocate its use&#8221; (Freud, 1920). In his 1931 paper Female Sexuality , Freud went further. He argued that the term wrongly stresses an analogy between the sexes. Only in boys, he wrote, do we find the combination of loving one parent while resenting the other as a rival (Freud, 1931). Why Freud and Jung Disagreed The quarrel over the name reflects the wider split between Freud and Jung . Jung&#8217;s 1913 lectures were his public break with Freud. In them he treated libido, the mind&#8217;s driving energy, as general psychic energy rather than a specifically sexual drive (Jung, 1913). Jung stopped treating the complex as a literal childhood wish. He noted that it appears even in people raised by foster parents. He took this as proof that it is &#8220;much less a reality than a purely regressive fantasy formation&#8221; (Jung, 1913). For Jung, an adult failing at a present-day task regresses. The libido flows backwards and revives childhood family imagery, which then looks like a buried infantile desire. This reverses Freud&#8217;s causal arrow: For Freud: The childhood complex is the root cause, and adult conflict only triggers it. For Jung: Adult conflict is the cause, and the childhood material is a costume it wears. Freud treated these revisions as an abandonment of the foundations of psychoanalysis (Freud, 1914). That explains the names. Jung&#8217;s less sexual libido made the boy&#8217;s and girl&#8217;s family dramas parallel, so a matching pair of names suited him. Freud&#8217;s account ran through anatomy, with castration anxiety destroying the boy&#8217;s complex and creating the girl&#8217;s, so symmetry was the very error (Freud, 1931).

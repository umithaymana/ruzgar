The two payment groups reasoned differently: $1 group: Insufficient external justification (no good outside reason for lying) created strong dissonance, so participants changed their attitude and rated the boring tasks as enjoyable. $20 group: The large payment gave sufficient external justification, so participants felt little dissonance and kept their original negative view of the tasks. Related Findings: Mild Threats and Over-Justification Aronson and Carlsmith (1963) found the same logic in children. Children were told not to play with an attractive toy, under either a mild or a severe threat. Those who obeyed the mild threat felt more dissonance, because a weak deterrent is harder to justify. They devalued the toy more, and for longer (Freedman, 1965). The mirror image is the over-justification effect. It occurs when an unneeded reward for an enjoyable activity undermines the original interest. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973) tested it on nursery children. They promised the children a &#8220;good-player&#8221; award for drawing with felt-tip pens, an activity they already enjoyed. These children later played with the pens less than children rewarded unexpectedly or not at all. The salient reward supplied an external justification that crowded out their original intrinsic motivation . Too little justification pushes people to internalise a counter-attitudinal act. Too much can undermine interest they already had. 2. Effort Justification Effort justification occurs when people put a lot of time, energy, or effort into something disappointing or unpleasant. To avoid the discomfort of feeling their effort was wasted, they convince themselves the outcome was worthwhile. Why it happens: If we spend a great deal of effort on something and then evaluate it negatively, this creates dissonance. To reduce this, we reshape our perception, emphasizing positive aspects or finding new value in the experience. Classic Study &#8211; Aronson and Mills (1959): Aim: To test whether a more severe initiation into a group increases how much a person later comes to like that group. Method: Sixty-three female college students volunteered for what they believed was a group discussion on the psychology of sex. They were assigned to a control, mild-embarrassment or severe-embarrassment initiation (described below). Results: All participants rated how interesting and valuable the same deliberately dull taped discussion about sex in animals was. The severe-embarrassment group rated it far more positively than the mild or control groups did. Conclusion: Greater effort or discomfort during initiation increases how much a person values the group afterwards. Inflating the group&#8217;s worth resolves the dissonance of having suffered for it. The three initiation groups were: Control group: Joined with no initiation at all. Mild-embarrassment group: First read aloud milder words, such as &#8220;prostitute&#8221; and &#8220;virgin.&#8221; Severe-embarrassment group: First read aloud obscene words and explicit sexual passages to a male experimenter. 3. Decision Making Making a decision between two or more attractive options often creates dissonance because each choice has both positive and negative aspects. Why dissonance occurs: After making a choice, people feel uneasy about the benefits they gave up from the other options. To reduce this discomfort, they focus on the positives of their choice and downplay the negatives, helping them feel confident and satisfied with their decision. This process is known as spreading of alternatives . Classic Study &#8211; Brehm (1956): Aim: To test whether choosing between similarly attractive options changes how much people like each option afterwards. Method: Female participants rated household appliances on an eight-point scale, then chose one of two items to keep. The items were ½ to 1½ points apart (a hard choice) or three points apart (an easy choice). Results: After the hard choice, participants liked the chosen item more and the rejected item less. This is post-decisional spreading of the alternatives. Conclusion: Harder decisions arouse more dissonance. People reduce it by stressing the chosen option&#8217;s strengths and the rejected option&#8217;s weaknesses. Field Study &#8211; Knox and Inkster (1968): Aim: To test whether post-decisional dissonance appears after a real, irrevocable choice outside the laboratory. Method: Researchers approached bettors at a racetrack, either queuing to place a $2 bet or just after placing it. All were asked how confident they were that their horse would win. Results: Bettors who had already placed the bet were markedly more confident, although nothing about the horses&#8217; chances had changed. Conclusion: The bet made the decision irrevocable. Dissonance could then be reduced only by becoming more certain of the choice, so bettors inflated their horse&#8217;s chances. The study&#8217;s strength is high ecological validity: real gamblers, real money and a consequential choice. Its limitation is that the two groups were different people sampled at different moments, so it lacks random assignment. Dissonance theory also predicts selective exposure. After a decision, people seek out information that fits their choice and avoid information that would reopen the conflict. The evidence is stronger for two related processes. Selective attention means dwelling on consistent information already present. Selective interpretation means reading ambiguous information as consistent with existing beliefs. Additional Causes of Cognitive Dissonance Three further situations commonly trigger dissonance: Contradictory beliefs: Holding opposing beliefs simultaneously (e.g., believing in honesty but telling a lie) causes tension. Behavior conflicting with values: Acting against personal morals or values can trigger dissonance and guilt. New information: Encountering facts that challenge deeply held beliefs may cause dissonance, leading to denial or attitude change. Dissonance Requires Free Choice Across every cause above, dissonance only arises when a person feels personally responsible for the behavior. No choice, no dissonance. If someone believes they had no real choice, the tension and the pressure to change simply don&#8217;t appear. Freedman (1963) showed dissonance theory and reinforcement theory are not rivals. They apply to different situations: dissonance predicts voluntary behavior, reinforcement predicts involuntary behavior. Commitment matters too. The person must feel publicly and irrevocably bound to the behaviour before dissonance takes hold (Brehm &amp; Cohen, 1962). Decision freedom is also decisive. Linder, Cooper and Jones (1967) had participants write a counter-attitudinal essay for a large or small fee, under high or low choice. When choice was high, a larger fee produced less attitude change than a small one. The less-leads-to-more pattern appeared only in this free-choice condition. When choice was low, the ordinary pattern returned: more money produced more change. This choice requirement has not held up cleanly, however. A large preregistered replication of a related essay-writing experiment found no extra attitude change under high choice (Vaidis et al., 2024). It did not test Linder and colleagues&#8217; choice-by-incentive pattern. The Evidence Check above weighs what it does and does not show. How To Reduce Cognitive Dissonance Changing attitudes or behaviors can be difficult. Sometimes, people hold onto dissonance or engage in avoidance, denial, or other defense mechanisms that may lead to irrational or inconsistent behaviors. Festinger (1957) saw people as rationalising, not rational, creatures. They try to appear rational, both to others and to themselves. He set out four logical routes for lowering dissonance. People can change the behaviour, reject the dissonant belief, add new consonant beliefs, or reduce the importance of the clash. The four strategies below map onto these routes. 1. Behavior Change When the dissonance involves a behavior that conflicts with a belief, the most direct way to reduce it is to change or eliminate the behavior. For example, a smoker who believes smoking is harmful may quit smoking to resolve the conflict. However, this can be very challenging because well-learned behaviors or habits are hard to break. People often try mental strategies first. 2. Rationalization Rationalization involves creating explanations or excuses to make conflicting behavior or beliefs seem acceptable. Instead of changing behavior, the individual alters how they think about the situation. For example, a smoker might say, “I only smoke occasionally,” or “It helps me relax,” which lessens the conflict without quitting. 3. Justification through New Information People often seek or emphasize new information that outweighs the conflicting belief. For instance, a smoker may focus on studies suggesting that “research has not definitively proven that smoking causes lung cancer.” This lets them reduce the dissonance by questioning the strength of the original belief.

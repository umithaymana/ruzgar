Conclusion: a stereotype linking brilliance to men emerges around the start of formal schooling. It already measurably narrows girls’ own interest in activities marketed as being for exceptionally clever children. Pairing a belief measure with a matching behavioural measure is unusually strong evidence that the stereotype has real motivational consequences. It is, however, one research team’s work with American children. The age at which the shift appears, and its size, would benefit from independent replication in other settings. Girls can develop negative feelings about their own gender. Their sense of their own abilities can suffer too. Strict societal ideas about gender can also make life harder for people who are non-binary, transgender, or who otherwise do not conform to their assigned birth gender. It can be difficult for these individuals to be their true selves in a society that treats gender as a strict binary. Limitations for men Gender socialization limits men as well as women. It can restrict them in several ways: Career pressure: Boys and men can feel pressure to succeed and dominate in their careers. Falling short of that can feel like failure. Affection and anger: Men and boys may avoid affectionate expression with peers, treating anger as the one acceptable emotional outlet. Hidden vulnerability: Vulnerable emotions and crying often get coded as feminine, so boys and men avoid showing them in front of others. Bullying: A boy who shows typically &#8220;feminine&#8221; traits risks being bullied. He learns not to display them again. Mental health: Suppressing vulnerable emotion can damage men’s mental health and make them less likely to seek help when struggling. Violence: Socialization toward aggression is also linked to higher rates of violence, including against women (Marcus, 2018). Gender Stereotypes and Sexism Gender stereotyping ascribes attributes, characteristics, or roles to people simply because of their gender. People are thought to be socialized into holding these stereotypes. Societies often have fixed ideas about each gender. When children absorb these ideas, they can hold stereotypes about other genders or feel pressure to conform to their own. Stereotypes appear early. In one study of Spanish children aged four to nine, gender bias grew stronger with age (Solbes-Canales et al., 2020). The pressure falls unevenly. Female stereotypes tend to be less restrictive than male ones, and females are generally less scrutinized for not adhering to them (Wharton, 2005). Stereotypes also feed sexism. The persistence of traditional gender roles exacerbates gender inequalities, encourages rigid behaviors, and maintains unbalanced power relations in favor of men (Marcus, 2018). Where society treats women as weaker or less competent at the same work, this can lead to discriminatory behavior toward women. Researchers trace the roots of prejudice, discrimination, and violence against women back to widespread gender stereotyping (McCarthy et al., 2018). Real-World Applications Researchers and practitioners have applied this evidence in several settings: Education: Teacher training targets unintentional gender-differentiated praise and discipline. The brilliance stereotype (Bian et al., 2017) has shaped how subjects seen as needing raw genius are taught to girls. Toys and retail: Labels alone can shape toy preferences (Martin et al., 1995; Weisgram et al., 2014). Some retailers and campaigns have dropped gender-segregated toy aisles in response. Parenting: Parents mainly transmit gender through toys, clothes, and activities (Lytton &amp; Romney, 1991). Guidance targets these channels, but old patterns resist even sustained effort (Statham, 1986). Family law: Children&#8217;s gender-typed development does not detectably depend on parental gender or sexual orientation (Fedewa et al., 2015). The finding has been cited in family-law and child-welfare contexts. Media: Content analysis, first used on children&#8217;s books (Weitzman et al., 1972), now tracks female characters in film, television, and video games. It also informs media-literacy guidance. Clinical practice: Knowing how early expectations spread helps clinicians. It separates ordinary variation in gender-typed interests from persistent incongruence between gender identity and assigned sex (see gender dysphoria ). Individuals can start with awareness. They can notice how their assigned gender shaped them, then ask whether a decision reflects that socialization or their own choice. Parents and educators can act too, so that children are not restricted by gender. Options include gender-neutral language, a wide range of toys and activities, and playful settings where children of different genders interact. Challenging stereotypes helps. Children learn they need not stay confined to traditional gender roles when adults discuss them rather than stress gender differences. Background Gender socialization often begins very early for most people. It is thought that children know their gender by age 3 (Stockard, 2006). That is long before they recognize any other type of group they belong to. It helps to distinguish sex from gender. Sex refers to the anatomical and reproductive differences between men and women (Kretchmar, 2011). Gender , by contrast, is a social construct, believed to exist on a continuum, involving ideas about masculinity and femininity. The terms are recent. Money (1994) dates the first printed use of &#8220;gender role&#8221; to 1955, when &#8220;gender&#8221; still belonged almost exclusively to grammarians. &#8220;Gender identity&#8221; emerged in the mid-1960s. The central idea of gender socialization is that societies have their own ideas of what gender is. People and cultures throughout the world recognize different gender groups. Each group has assigned roles and responsibilities. Through gender socialization, people develop their own beliefs about gender and ultimately form their own gender identity. People may not be consciously aware of the gender norm decisions that they make. Gender roles and norms are often built into the script of what a man/woman should do or be. Gender socialization does not end in childhood. People keep learning and renegotiating expectations about gender throughout life.

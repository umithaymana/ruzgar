Key Takeaways Jung&#8217;s Term: The Electra complex, first proposed by Carl Jung, is the female equivalent of the Oedipus complex. Phallic Stage: Like the Oedipal complex, the Electra complex arises in Freud&#8217;s phallic stage (roughly ages three to six), when a child&#8217;s pleasure centers on the genitals. Turning From Mother: During this stage, a female child supposedly pushes away from her mother and becomes jealous of and displays possessive affection toward her father. Penis Envy: According to Freud, penis envy drives this shift: a young girl realizes she lacks a penis and turns resentment toward her mother. Unresolved Complex: Freud thought an unresolved complex could feed neurosis, though by 1940 he judged it did &#8220;little harm&#8221; to a woman (Freud, 1940). Little Evidence: The Electra Complex has been criticized by modern psychology and is widely considered an antiquated concept because there is little evidence to support it. Key Critiques: Critics have questioned Freud&#8217;s methodology in his case studies and argued that penis envy and the Electra Complex were invented as cover-ups for sexual trauma. Lasting Influence: Nonetheless, the Electra Complex still holds a place in modern consciousness, cited in everything from case studies of drug use to psychoanalytic literary theory. The Electra complex is a psychoanalytic theory proposed by Carl Jung that describes a girl&#8217;s unconscious sexual desire for her father and feelings of jealousy and resentment towards her mother. In This Article: Toggle How Does the Electra Complex Work? Carl Jung , who coined the term, described the Electra complex as part of a broader process of family attachment and eventual detachment. He saw this process as largely non-sexual in origin, reaching its true crisis at adolescence rather than early childhood (Jung, 1913). Freud, however, is credited with the well-known account. In Freud&#8217;s version, a young girl&#8217;s intense early bond with her mother turns to jealousy once she discovers she lacks a penis. She then desires what her father&#8217;s anatomy symbolizes, and may become possessive of his attention or hostile when she does not get it. Freud described the girl&#8217;s path in four steps: Mother Attachment: The girl&#8217;s first love is her mother, a bond that can last beyond age four and ends in hate (Freud, 1931, 1933). Penis Envy: She notices the anatomical difference, reads it as her own deficiency, and blames her mother (Freud, 1925). Baby Wish: She swaps the wish for a penis for a wish for a child, and takes her father as her love object (Freud, 1925). Identification: She identifies with her mother, taking in her traits and morality, though Freud doubted this works reliably (Freud, 1925). In Freudian theory , an important part of the developmental process is learning to identify with one&#8217;s same-sex parent. Attraction to one&#8217;s father Freudian theory holds that between three and six or seven years of age, a child begins to want to possess and grow closer to the opposite-sex parent. A boy may push his father away while kissing and hugging his mother. A young girl, similarly, may insist on following her father into the bathroom. The complex, Freud said, fades slowly. Its effects can persist into adult life (Freud, 1925). Defense mechanisms do most of the work: repression and identification. Resolution Freud also suggested that a young girl discovers she has no penis. This discovery, he argued, produces penis envy: resentment toward her mother for her own lack of male anatomy. Eventually, this resentment gives way to identification. The daughter incorporates many of her mother&#8217;s personality characteristics into her ego, the part of the mind responsible for sorting out what is real. This process also lets the daughter incorporate her mother&#8217;s morality into her superego, the part of the mind Freud believed controlled the id&#8217;s impulses (Honig, 2000). In Freud&#8217;s view, this identification lets the child accept her gender role, understand her sexuality, and develop a sense of morality. Freud was hesitant about how reliably it works, though.

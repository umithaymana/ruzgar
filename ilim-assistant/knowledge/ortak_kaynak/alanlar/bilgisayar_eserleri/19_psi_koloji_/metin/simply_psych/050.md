Children come to understand what they believe is acceptable and appropriate for each gender. Gender schemas make new situations more predictable. Children build more elaborate schemas as their gender identity and understanding of gender roles develop. The theory suggests these schemas are internalized so that males and masculinity are seen as the norm, valued more highly than females and femininity (Wharton, 2005). A closely related schematic processing model is more specific. Children first sort information into &#8220;same-gender&#8221; and &#8220;other-gender&#8221; categories (Martin &amp; Halverson, 1981). They then build a detailed schema only for their own gender. The other gender’s schema stays cruder. This helps explain why children’s stereotypes about their own gender are often more nuanced than their beliefs about the opposite gender. Two central claims of the theory have been tested directly. First, a basic gender schema forms very early. Second, it actively organizes children’s play. Aim: to establish when children’s gender schemas begin to organize their behaviour, and what part environmental input plays in building them. Method: children’s emerging understanding of gender categories and gender-typed activity was assessed. It was then related to their later play and to their gendered environment, such as parental attitudes and toys (Fagot &amp; Leinbach, 1989). Results: children who could label gender categories and sort activities by gender showed more gender-typed play later. The content and speed of each child’s schema varied with the input they had received. Conclusion: schemas are neither purely internal nor a passive mirror of the environment. They develop through the child’s own categorizing and the gendered information around them. The study turns an abstract idea, the gender schema, into measurable labelling and sorting behaviour. But it is correlational, so it cannot separate cause from effect. Children with gender-typed interests may also draw more gender-typed responses from parents. One limitation is the problem of individual differences. The theory does not explain why children who share much the same environment respond differently to gender-appropriate behavior. A related question is whether this process is specific to gender at all. A classic experiment tested it. Aim: to test whether sorting children into a meaningless social category is enough to produce biased attitudes like those seen for gender and race. Method: elementary-school children were randomly assigned to wear blue or yellow t-shirts. In some classrooms, teachers used the colour groups, for example lining children up by colour; in others they did not (Bigler, Jones, &amp; Lobliner, 1997). Results: where teachers used the groups, children showed clear in-group favouritism and biased attitudes toward the other colour group, resembling patterns seen for gender and race. Conclusion: stereotyping may need nothing unique to gender: it can arise whenever adults make any category salient. Sorting children less by gender might, in principle, reduce it. Using a meaningless category isolates the categorizing process. But a t-shirt group is a far weaker category than gender, which children meet from birth. The study shows a plausible mechanism, not gender stereotyping’s only cause. Psychoanalytic theory The psychoanalytic theory of gender socialization is different from the learning theories above. It was not outlined until the 1970s, by Nancy Chodorow, building on the work of Sigmund Freud . The theory suggests some aspects of gender identity come from unconscious psychological processes , not from conscious modeling or schemas. A key factor is the mother’s role as primary caregiver. Because children spend more early time with mothers, Chodorow argues their first identification is with the feminine. Girls’ development is therefore comparatively simple: they have already identified with the mother. Boys, however, must first reject that feminine identification. They then construct masculinity as &#8220;not feminine.&#8221; In this process, boys also learn to devalue femininity. Criticisms of psychoanalytic theory for gender socialization are that the theory is hard to empirically verify. It also reinforces gender stereotypes, places too much emphasis on the unconscious, and does not explain how children who were not raised by mothers develop their gender identity. Biological and Hormonal Perspectives Biological accounts ask a different question: not how culture teaches gender, but whether some gender-typed behavior has roots that predate any teaching. They propose that prenatal hormone exposure and brain organization shape average differences in play and interests. Culture then builds on this foundation (Hines, 2011). Sociologist R. W. Connell reviewed a century of research on the physiological mechanisms a strong biological-determinist position would need. He concluded that none linking supposed innate predispositions to the complexity of gendered behavior had been convincingly demonstrated (Connell, 1987). More recent hormonal research has reopened part of that question. Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia The clearest natural experiment comes from children with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH). This condition exposes female fetuses to unusually high levels of androgens, the male-typical hormones, before birth. Girls with CAH consistently show more male-typical play in childhood than their unaffected female relatives. These girls are otherwise raised and socialized as girls. The finding is therefore hard to explain by socialization alone, and it is among the most cited evidence for a biological contribution to gender-typed play. Ordinary Hormone Variation in Boys Does ordinary variation in early hormones, not just the extreme case of CAH, predict gender-typed behavior? A study using a physical marker of androgen exposure tested this. Aim: to test whether the normal androgen surge in male infants in the first months of life (&#8220;mini-puberty&#8221;) predicts later sex-typed play in typically developing boys. Method: researchers measured penile growth from birth to three months, a marker of androgen exposure, in 81 boys. Parents later completed a questionnaire on the boys’ sex-typed play at ages three to four (Pasterski et al., 2015). Results: greater penile growth in early infancy predicted more male-typical play. This held after accounting for prenatal androgen exposure, which also predicted play independently. Conclusion: ordinary variation in the postnatal androgen surge, not just extreme prenatal exposure, appears to contribute independently to how strongly boys later show sex-typed play. Using a physical growth marker avoids hormone measures that are hard to obtain and validate in infancy. But the design is correlational, the sample is modest, and the finding awaits independent replication. How Biology and Socialization Fit Together Wider reviews agree. Early hormone exposure is linked to average differences in gender-typed play and interests. It is one influence among several, not a determinant that overrides social input (Hines, 2011). Hormonal and social influences are not rivals. They look more like parts of one story. Early androgen exposure may nudge the starting point of a child’s play preferences, particularly for boys. Parents, peers, media, and children’s own thinking then reinforce, elaborate, or sometimes counteract it. Few studies have modelled both together. Critical Evaluation Most gender-development research is correlational, not experimental. Cause and effect are therefore often unclear: a child who already prefers gender-typed toys may draw more gender-typed encouragement from parents, rather than the encouragement creating the preference. These measurement tools are not neutral either. Older questionnaires decide in advance which traits count as &#8220;masculine&#8221; or &#8220;feminine.&#8221; They can build in the very assumptions under test. They have faced exactly this criticism (Bem, 1974). Treating &#8220;male&#8221; and &#8220;female&#8221; as simple, fixed starting categories is itself a live methodological caution, not a neutral assumption (Connell, 1987). More recent hormonal and neurological research has reopened part of this question, showing that early biology can also play a modest, contributing role (Hines, 2011). A newer, more radical social constructionist perspective goes further still. It questions not just how gender is learned, but whether the underlying sex categories are as fixed as the socialization model assumes. West and Zimmerman called their account &#8220;doing gender.&#8221; They reframed gender not as a stable trait a person has, but as something continuously produced through everyday interaction. People are held accountable, moment to moment, for acting in recognizably masculine or feminine ways (West &amp; Zimmerman, 1987). Judith Butler’s related concept of performativity argues that repeated, culturally scripted acts do not just express a pre-existing gender identity but actively constitute it (Butler, 1990). In plain terms, gender is created by acting it out. Gender seems like a stable inner essence only because people perform it so consistently and so rarely interrupt it. On this view, even the line between &#8220;biological&#8221; sex and &#8220;social&#8221; gender is itself a product of culture.

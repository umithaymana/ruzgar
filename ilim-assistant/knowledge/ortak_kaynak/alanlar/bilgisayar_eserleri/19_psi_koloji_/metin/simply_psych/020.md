Questioning the Classic Methods A second strand asks a harder question: do the classic paradigms measure real change at all? Chen and Risen (2010) re-examined Brehm&#8217;s (1956) rate-choose-rate design, in which people rate options, choose between two, then rate them again. They showed the design can produce apparent preference change as a statistical artefact of the choice itself, even when no real dissonance-driven shift has occurred. The choice itself is revealing. Picking between two closely rated options can expose a person&#8217;s pre-existing, slightly stronger preference. The second rating then simply restates that preference as if it were new attitude change. The critique didn&#8217;t disprove the effect. It raised the evidential bar instead: once tighter controls are used, much of the free-choice spreading still survives. It remains a caution against reading attitude change directly off any single classic paradigm. That&#8217;s one reason researchers today weight converging designs more heavily than any lone procedure. A larger test targeted the induced-compliance paradigm itself. A preregistered multilab replication tested it. Thirty-nine laboratories recruited 4,898 students. Writing a counter-attitudinal essay shifted attitudes. The classic advantage of high over low perceived choice did not appear (Vaidis et al., 2024). The replication authors concluded that the evidence behind the paradigm is thin, because earlier studies were small and unregistered (Sleegers et al., 2024). The Evidence Check above weighs the result in full. Dissonance on Someone Else&#8217;s Behalf A further strand asks whether people can feel dissonance for someone else. Norton, Monin, Cooper and Hogg (2003) coined the term vicarious dissonance, blending dissonance theory with social identity theory . Aim: To test whether people can feel dissonance when someone from their own group behaves inconsistently. Method: Students watched a fellow student (in fact a confederate) freely write a statement supporting a tuition-fee increase he privately opposed. The statement might be used to sway university officials. Results: Observers who shared the writer&#8217;s group membership shifted their own attitudes toward supporting the increase, despite making no choice. The more positively they felt about the group, the larger the shift. Conclusion: Vicarious arousal mediated the effect. Observers who would have felt more uncomfortable in the writer&#8217;s position showed more attitude change. Group identification can therefore carry the same aversive arousal that Zanna and Cooper (1974) found in firsthand dissonance. Scaling Up: Dissonance-Based Prevention Programmes The strongest evidence now comes from applied prevention trials, not lab demonstrations. These programmes have at-risk young women voluntarily argue against the thin ideal promoted by media and culture. Arguing against a view they might privately hold arouses dissonance. Participants resolve it by adopting a healthier body attitude. Aim: Stice, Marti, Shaw and Rohde (2019) set out to estimate how well dissonance-based eating-disorder prevention programmes work, and which features predict the largest effects. Method: The team synthesised 56 trials evaluating 68 dissonance-based programmes, covering 7,808 participants, against both minimal-intervention and alternative-intervention controls. Results: The programmes worked. Against minimal-intervention controls, effect sizes reached 0.57 for thin-ideal internalisation, 0.42 for body dissatisfaction, 0.37 for dieting and 0.31 for eating-disorder symptoms. Conclusion: Deliberately induced dissonance produces robust, real-world behaviour change at scale. The moderators identified offer a template for more effective interventions. Effects held up, even against active alternative interventions. The comparator effect sizes were 0.31, 0.18, 0.17 and 0.13 across the same four outcomes. Bigger effects came from programmes with more dissonance-inducing activities, larger in-person groups, and more sessions. One finding cut against the theory&#8217;s own logic. Paid participants showed larger effects too, even though payment should supply an external justification and dampen dissonance. Engagement, not dissonance alone, may explain the anomaly. The moderator analyses are correlational, not experimental, so they cannot prove the dissonance-heightening features directly cause the larger effects. Even so, the pattern holds across dozens of trials and thousands of real participants. Real-World Applications Cognitive dissonance theory isn&#8217;t just a laboratory curiosity. Its logic underpins several deliberate strategies for changing real behaviour outside the lab, from public health to marketing. Advocacy and Attitude Change Getting someone to argue for a position out loud, with little pressure or reward, produces deeper private attitude change than a big incentive ever could. This is the same induced-compliance logic behind the $1/$20 experiment. It still works today. Advocacy campaigns, teachers, and persuaders exploit it by asking people to make the case themselves, rather than simply telling them what to think. A person who freely makes an argument owns it. Someone who is just told what to think never does. The $1/$20 experiment shows the mechanism at its purest. Participants paid too little to justify the lie resolved the dissonance by coming to believe it. A big incentive would have let them shrug it off, changing nothing they believed. Health Behaviour Change (Hypocrisy Induction) Public health researchers use a technique called hypocrisy induction to change habits like unsafe sex, poor sun protection, or wasteful water use. The technique works in three steps: Advocate: People are first asked to publicly advocate the healthy behaviour. Remind: Later, they are reminded of times they failed to practise it themselves. Resolve: What they preached and what they did don&#8217;t match, which creates dissonance. The advocacy can&#8217;t be taken back, so most people bring their behaviour in line with it. Field experiments support the technique. Dickerson, Thibodeau, Aronson and Miller (1992) made patrons of a campus recreation facility feel hypocritical about their showering habits. Those patrons took shorter showers than controls. Stone, Aronson, Crain, Winslow and Fried (1994) used the same procedure for safe sex. More sexually active young adults in the hypocrisy condition bought condoms, and on average they bought more of them than controls did. The technique has one limit, though. It can only close a gap for people who already hold the healthy belief; it cannot create that value from nothing. Marketing and Consumer Behaviour Post-decisional dissonance drives the familiar experience of buyer&#8217;s remorse , and the ways people talk themselves out of it. After an expensive purchase, buyers often reduce dissonance in three ways: Seeking reviews: Buyers look for positive reviews of what they bought. Avoiding comparisons: They steer clear of comparing their choice with the rejected alternative. Talking it up: They praise what they bought. This is the same &#8220;spreading of alternatives&#8221; seen in Brehm&#8217;s experiment, playing out after a real purchase. Marketers exploit this pattern deliberately. Reassurance emails after a purchase, and small effortful commitments like loyalty schemes, both increase the attachment that dissonance-reduction already creates. The same dynamic shapes everyday decisions too, from choosing a university course to picking a phone contract. Once the choice is made and hard to reverse, the mind gets to work justifying it. Brehm found the same spreading of alternatives with household appliances. What is the difference between cognitive dissonance theory and balance theory? Cognitive dissonance theory, proposed by Festinger, focuses on the discomfort felt when holding conflicting beliefs or attitudes, leading individuals to seek consistency. Heider&#8217;s Balance Theory , on the other hand, emphasizes the desire for balanced relations among triads of entities, such as people and their attitudes. When these relations are unbalanced, the theory predicts attitudes will change to restore balance. Both theories address cognitive consistency, but in different contexts.

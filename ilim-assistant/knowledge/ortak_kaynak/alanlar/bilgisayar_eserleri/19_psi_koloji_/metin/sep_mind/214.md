This work may be best viewed as the beginning of a new subfield, that we might call the philosophy of madness (e.g. Feyaerts and Sips 2023, Jeppsson 2022, Kusters 2020, Rashed 2019, Savile-Smith 2023.). 4.3 Network models The medical model is widely believed to borrow the &ldquo;common cause&rdquo; structure of general medicine, in which observed variables (symptoms) indicate the existence of a latent variable that is their cause. The network model (Nuijten et al 2016), however, makes a very different bet. Rather than an underlying cause or latent variable, it attributes the joint occurrence and course of the clustered symptoms to the fact that they are part of the same network: &ldquo;instead of being effects of a common cause, psychiatric symptoms have been argued to cause each other&rdquo; (Borsboom 2017, 5). This can be represented as a network in which symptoms are nodes, and the connections between nodes indicate causal relations, which we can understand using interventionist models of causation. Each symptom is a node, and a change in a node changes the probability distribution of the other symptoms which are connected to the original node. For example, difficulties in sleeping is likely to be strongly connected to fatigue, and both nodes may be connected to depressed mood, indicating that tired, sleep-deprived people are more likely to feel down and feel bad about themselves. (The connections could run in the other direction too, from low mood to difficulty sleeping.) Eventually we see a set of connections stabilizing the network as the symptoms of depression switch each other on and even symptom nodes that are not directly connected influence each other via shared neighbours (Borsboom 2017, 6). The intuitive appeal of the network model relies on the idea that standard conceptions of medical disease, with their separation of underlying condition and visible symptom, make no sense in psychiatry and are implausibly reductive (Borsboom et al 2019, Borsboom 2017). However, it has been argued in response that the (Bringmann and Eronen 2018) network and latent variable models are not so easy to distinguish, and that the choice between them is orthogonal to questions of reductionism. Further, although a disease such as depression cannot function as a part of the network, we can include in the network neurological or genetic processes that are taken to be part of the underlying state, so that the line between networks and traditional models is blurry. Bringmann and Eronen&rsquo;s review of the literature suggests that model choice should not be made on the basis of background pictures of disease, but on the basis of other factors, especially the dynamic relations among variables stressed by network theorist. The philosophical appeal of a conception of mental illness by itself does not legitimate any choice of model.

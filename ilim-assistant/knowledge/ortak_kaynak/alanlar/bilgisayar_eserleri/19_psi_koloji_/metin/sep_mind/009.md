The second example is Robert Rauschenberg&rsquo;s Erased de Kooning drawing (1953), which is just what it says it is: all we see is an empty paper (with hardly visible traces of the erased drawing on it). Again, it is difficult to look at this artwork without trying to discern what drawing might have been there before Rauschenberg erased it. And this involves trying to conjure up mental imagery of the original drawing. Again, these were two classic examples. But there are more. All of Ai Wei Wei&rsquo;s works, for example, rely heavily on our mental imagery. Not all works of conceptual art evokes mental imagery this way. One exception would be Robert Barry&rsquo;s All the things I know , which is nothing but the following sentence written on the gallery wall with simple block letters: &ldquo;All the things I know but of which I am not at the moment thinking &ndash; 1:36 PM; June 15, 1969&rdquo;. It would be difficult to argue that this work has much interest in enticing the viewer&rsquo;s mental imagery. But it is not easy to find an example of a conceptual artwork where mental imagery plays no role.

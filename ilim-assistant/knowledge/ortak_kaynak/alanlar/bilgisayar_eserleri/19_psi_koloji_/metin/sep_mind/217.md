The question of what well-being consists in is of independent interest, but it is of great importance in moral philosophy, especially in the case of utilitarianism, according to which the only moral requirement is that total or overall well-being be maximized. Significant challenges to the very notion have been mounted, in particular by G.E. Moore and T.M. Scanlon. It has become standard to distinguish theories of well-being as either hedonist theories, desire theories, or objective list theories. According to the view known as welfarism, well-being is the only value. Also important in ethics is the question of how a person&rsquo;s moral character and actions relate to their well-being. 1. The Concept Popular use of the term &lsquo;well-being&rsquo; usually relates in particular to health. A doctor&rsquo;s surgery may run a &lsquo;Women&rsquo;s Well-being Clinic&rsquo;, for example. Philosophical use is broader, but related, and amounts to the notion of how well a person&rsquo;s life is going for that person, that is, of which ultimate goods in that life make it a life worth living for that person or from their point of view. A person&rsquo;s well-being is what is &lsquo;good for&rsquo; them. Health, then, might be said to be a constituent of my well-being, but it is not plausibly taken to be all that matters for my well-being. One correlate term worth noting here is &lsquo;self-interest&rsquo;: my self-interest is what is in the interest of myself, and not others. Well-being is a kind of value, sometimes called &lsquo;prudential value&rsquo;, to be distinguished from, for example, aesthetic value or moral value. What marks it out is the notion of &lsquo;good for&rsquo;. The serenity of a Vermeer painting, for instance, is a kind of goodness, but it is not &lsquo;good for&rsquo; the painting. It may be good for us to contemplate such serenity, but contemplating serenity is not the same as the serenity itself. Likewise, my giving money to a development charity may have moral value, that is, be morally good. And the effects of my donation may be good for others. But it remains an open question whether my being morally good is good for me; and, if it is, its being good for me is still conceptually distinct from its being morally good. A great deal of attention has been paid in philosophy to the issue of moral reasons, or &lsquo;normativity&rsquo;, less so to that of prudential normativity (recent exceptions are Dorsey 2021; Fletcher 2021; Brown 2025). Well-being can be assessed across its possessor&rsquo;s life as a whole, or at a particular time. So we might say of someone that, for example, they had a life which was good overall, though at particular times things went quite badly for them and their well-being level was low. For an argument that only the former assessment is appropriate, see Bramble 2018 and a response by Bradley (2021). Another question is whether all that matters for a person is the total amount of well-being in their life, or whether it makes a difference when particular elements of the well-being of that person occur. It might be claimed, for example, that a life which starts badly and improves as time goes on is better than a life which starts well and then goes downhill, even if the total well-being in each life is the same (see Velleman 1991; Dorsey 2015). Well-being is usually discussed in connection with individual human beings (or other individual animals), but there is some discussion of whether it makes sense to speak of a group&rsquo;s having well-being (see Wiland 2022). The philosophical use of the term also tends to encompass the &lsquo;negative&rsquo; aspects of how a person&rsquo;s life goes for them. So we may speak of the well-being of someone who is, and will remain in, the most terrible agony: their well-being is negative, and such that their life is worse for them than no life at all. The same is true of closely allied terms, such as &lsquo;welfare&rsquo;, which covers how a person is faring as a whole, whether well or badly, or &lsquo;happiness&rsquo;, which can be understood&mdash;as it sometimes was by the classical utilitarians from Jeremy Bentham onwards, for example&mdash;to be the balance between good and bad things in a person&rsquo;s life. But note that philosophers also use such terms in the more standard &lsquo;positive&rsquo; way, speaking of &lsquo;ill-being&rsquo;, &lsquo;ill-faring&rsquo;, or, of course, &lsquo;unhappiness&rsquo; to capture the negative aspects of individuals&rsquo; lives. Most philosophical discussion has been of &lsquo;goods&rsquo; rather than &lsquo;bads&rsquo;, but recently more interest has been shown in the latter (e.g. Kagan 2015; Bradford 2021; Bradford (ed.) 2022). &lsquo;Happiness&rsquo; is often used, in ordinary life, to refer to a short-lived state of a person, frequently a feeling of contentment: &lsquo;You look happy today&rsquo;; &lsquo;I&rsquo;m very happy for you&rsquo;. Philosophically, its scope is more often wider, encompassing a whole life. And in philosophy it is possible to speak of the happiness of a person&rsquo;s life, or of their happy life, even if that person was in fact usually pretty miserable. The point is that some good things in their life made it a happy one, even though they lacked contentment. But this usage is uncommon, and may cause confusion. Over the last few decades, so-called &lsquo;positive psychology&rsquo; has hugely increased the attention paid by psychologists and other scientists to the notion of &lsquo;happiness&rsquo;. Such happiness is usually understood in terms of contentment or &lsquo;life-satisfaction&rsquo;, and is measured by means such as self-reports or daily questionnaires. Is positive psychology about well-being? As yet, conceptual distinctions are not sufficiently clear within the discipline. But it is probably fair to say that many of those involved, as researchers or as subjects, are assuming that one&rsquo;s life goes well to the extent that one is contented with it&mdash;that is, that some kind of hedonistic account of well-being is correct. Some positive psychologists, however, explicitly reject hedonistic theories in preference to Aristotelian or &lsquo;eudaimonist&rsquo; accounts of well-being, which are a version of the &lsquo;objective list&rsquo; theory of well-being discussed below. A leader in the field, Martin Seligman, for example, has suggested that, rather than happiness, positive psychology should concern itself with positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning and accomplishment (&lsquo;Perma&rsquo;) (Seligman 2011). It is common for people to speak not only of how well their life is going for them, but of things in their life that they find meaningful, or that give their life meaning. On the question of how meaning, meaningfulness, and the &lsquo;meaning of life&rsquo; relate to well-being, see e.g. Wolf 2010; Woodard 2017; Hammerton 2022. When discussing the notion of what makes life good for the individual living that life, it is preferable to use the term &lsquo;well-being&rsquo; instead of &lsquo;happiness&rsquo;. For we want at least to allow conceptual space for the possibility that, for example, the life of a plant may be &lsquo;good for&rsquo; that plant. And speaking of the happiness of a plant would be stretching language too far. (An alternative here might be &lsquo;flourishing&rsquo;, though this might be taken to bias the analysis of human well-being in the direction of some kind of natural teleology.) In that respect, the Greek word commonly translated &lsquo;happiness&rsquo; ( eudaimonia ) might be thought to be superior. But, in fact, eudaimonia seems to have been restricted not only to conscious beings, but to human beings: non-human animals cannot be eudaim&#333;n . This is because eudaimonia suggests that the gods, or fortune, have favoured one, and the idea that the gods could care about non-humans would not have occurred to most Greeks. It is occasionally claimed that certain ancient ethical theories, such as Aristotle&rsquo;s, result in the collapse of the very notion of well-being. On Aristotle&rsquo;s view, if you are my friend, then my well-being is closely bound up with yours. It might be tempting, then, to say that &lsquo;your&rsquo; well-being is &lsquo;part&rsquo; of mine, in which case the distinction between what is good for me and what is good for others has broken down. But this temptation should be resisted. Your well-being concerns how well your life goes for you, and we can allow that my well-being depends on yours without introducing the confusing notion that my well-being is constituted by yours. There are signs in Aristotelian thought of an expansion of the subject or owner of well-being. A friend is &lsquo;another self&rsquo;, so that what benefits my friend benefits me.

Here the three moments of origination, endurance, and cessation do not correspond to three different entities. Rather, they represent three phases of a single momentary phenomenon and are defined as one single consciousness moment: a dhamma occurs in the first sub-moment, endures in the second sub-moment and perishes in the third one. In this way, the Therav&#257;dins avoided some of the difficulties faced by the Sarv&#257;stiv&#257;da-Vaibh&#257;&#7779;ikas, of how to compress the characteristics of the conditioned into one single indivisible moment and of how to account for their ontological status. The Therav&#257;dins also claimed that only mental phenomena are momentary, whereas material phenomena (e.g., common-sense objects) endure for a stretch of time. The Therav&#257;din commentarial tradition subsequently elaborated on this proposition and produced a unique view of the ratio between material and mental phenomena, asserting that a material phenomenon lasts for sixteen or seventeen consciousness moments ( Kv 620; Vibh-a 25&ndash;28; Vism XX 24&ndash;26; Kim 1999, 79&ndash;80 &amp; &sect;3.1). Despite their different interpretations of the concept of momentariness, the early Buddhist schools all derived this concept from the analysis of impermanence in terms of the dynamics of dharmas qua physical and mental events. The equation of a moment with the duration of these transient events as extremely short occurrences&mdash;even the shortest conceivable&mdash;led to the direct determination of the moment in terms of these occurrences. Yet the doctrine of momentariness spawned a host of problems for the Buddhist schools, particularly with regard to the status of the endurance moment and to the explanation of continuity and conditioning interaction among the dharmas (see section 5 below). If dharmas go through an endurance phase or exist as real entities within the span of any given moment, how can they be momentary? And if experience is an array of strictly momentary dharmas , how can continuity and causal conditioning be possible? One might argue that the conceptual shift from &ldquo;impermanence&rdquo; to &ldquo;endurance&rdquo; is a result of scholastic literalism and testifies to the Abhidharma tendency towards reification and hypostatization of dharmas (Gombrich 1996, 36&ndash;37, 96&ndash;97 &amp; 106&ndash;107). Nevertheless, the object of the doctrine of momentariness is not so much existence in time or the passage of time per se, but rather, in epistemological terms and a somewhat Bergsonian sense, the construction of temporal experience. Instead of a transcendental matrix of order imposed on natural events from without, time is seen as an inherent feature of the operation of dharmas . The doctrine of momentariness analyzes dharmas as they transpire through time: as psycho-physical events that arise and cease in consciousness and, by the dynamics of their rise and fall, construct time. The sequence of the three times is therefore secondary, generated in and by the process of conditioned and conditioning dharmas. In fact, the conceptual shift from the principle of impermanence to the theory of momentariness is a shift in time scales. While the S&#363;tr&#257;nta worldview interprets the three times as referring to past, present, and future lives, the Abhidharma sees them as phases that any conditioned dharma undergoes each and every moment. Impermanence marks dharmas over a period of time, but is also encapsulated in every single consciousness moment ( Vibh-a 7&ndash;8; Sv 991; Vism XIV 191; Collins 1992, 227). 4. Intrinsic nature: between categorization and ontology To preserve the principle of impermanence and explain continuity and causal conditioning in ordinary experience, the Buddhist schools introduced novel interpretations of the nature of dharmas . At the heart of these interpretations is the concept of intrinsic nature (Skt., svabh&#257;va , Pali, sabh&#257;va ) that plays a major role in the systematization of Abhidharma thought, is closely related to the consolidation of the dharma theory, and is regarded as that which gave an impetus to the Abhidharma&rsquo;s growing concern with ontology. The term svabh&#257;va/sabh&#257;va does not feature in the s&#363;tras/suttas and its rare mentions in other Sarv&#257;stiv&#257;da and Therav&#257;da canonical texts offer no account of dharma as defined by a fixed intrinsic nature that verifies its real existence. [ 11 ] This situation changes significantly in the post-canonical literature, in which svabh&#257;va becomes a standard concept extensively used in dharma exegesis. A recurring idea in the exegetical Abhidharma literature from the period of the early vibh&#257;&#7779;&#257; compendia onward is that dharmas are defined by virtue of their svabh&#257;va . For instance, a definition transmitted in the Abhidharmako&#347;abh&#257;&#7779;ya reads: &ldquo; dharma means &lsquo;upholding,&rsquo; [namely], upholding intrinsic nature ( svabh&#257;va ),&rdquo; and the Mah&#257;vibh&#257;&#7779;&#257; states that &ldquo;intrinsic nature is able to uphold its own identity and not lose it [&hellip;] as in the case of unconditioned dharmas that are able to uphold their own identity&rdquo; (Cox 2004A, 558&ndash;559). Similarly, a definition prevalent in Therav&#257;din Abhidhamma commentaries is: &ldquo; dhammas are so called because they bear their intrinsic natures, or because they are borne by causal conditions&rdquo; (e.g., Dhs-a 39-40; Pa&#7789;is-a I 18; Vism-mh&#7789; I 347). The commentaries also regularly equate dhammas with their intrinsic natures, using the terms dhamma and sabh&#257;va interchangeably. For example, the Visuddhimagga proclaims that &ldquo; dhamma means but intrinsic nature&rdquo; ( Vism VIII 246), and the sub-commentary to the Dhammasa&#7749;ga&#7751;i indicates that &ldquo;there is no other thing called dhamma apart from the intrinsic nature borne by it&rdquo; and that &ldquo;the term sabh&#257;va denotes the mere fact of being a dhamma &rdquo; ( Dhs-m&#7789; 28 &amp; 94; see also Karunadasa 2010: Ch. 1). These commentarial definitions of dharmas as carrying their intrinsic natures should not be interpreted ontologically as implying that dharmas are substances having inherent existence. The Pali commentaries, cautions Gethin (2004, 533), &ldquo;are often viewed too much in the light of later controversies about the precise ontological status of dharmas and the Madhyamaka critique of the notion of svabh&#257;va in the sense of &lsquo;inherent existence.&rsquo;&rdquo; In fact, defining dharmas as bearing their intrinsic natures conveys the idea that there is no enduring agent behind them. Adding that dharmas are borne by causal conditions counters the idea of intrinsic natures borne by underlying substances distinct from themselves. Just as dharmas are psycho-physical events that occur dependently on appropriate conditions and qualities, their intrinsic natures arise dependently on other conditions and qualities rather than on a substratum more real than they are ( ibid; Karunadasa 1996, 13&ndash;16; Nyanaponika 1998, 40&ndash;41). We must also note that the context within which dharmas are rendered in terms of their intrinsic natures is that of categorization, where multiple criteria and qualities are applied to create a comprehensive taxonomic system that distinguishes the particular character of any given dharma . Cox (2004A: 559&ndash;561) has shown that in the early period of northern Indian Abhidharma texts, as represented by the &#346;&#257;riputr&#257;bhidharma&#347;&#257;stra and portions of the Mah&#257;vibh&#257;&#7779;&#257; , the concept of intrinsic nature develops within the context of the method of inclusion ( sa&#7747;graha ), that is, the process by which the inclusion of dharmas within a specific category is to be applied. Dharmas are determined ( parini&#7779;panna ) by the intrinsic nature that defines them and hence should not be considered to possess a separately existing intrinsic nature. &ldquo;&lsquo;Determination&rsquo; implies two further features of dharmas [&hellip;] First, just as categories in a well-structured taxonomic schema are distinct and not subject to fluctuation, so also dharmas , as &lsquo;determined,&rsquo; are clearly and unalterably discriminated: they are uniquely individualized and as such are not subject to confusion with other dharmas . [Second,] determination by intrinsic nature undergoes no variation or modification, and hence, dharmas , which are in effect types or categories of intrinsic nature, are established as stable and immutable&rdquo; ( ibid , 562). In the early Sarv&#257;stiv&#257;da exegetical texts, then, svabh&#257;va is used as an atemporal, invariable criterion determining what a dharma is, not necessarily that a dharma exists. The concern here is primarily with what makes categorial types of dharma unique, rather than with the ontological status of dharmas . Nevertheless, from the foregoing categorial theory, the mature Abhidharma drew ontological conclusions with regard to the reality of dharmas . This transition in the conception of dharma coincided with an inherent ambiguity in the term svabh&#257;va , which is grounded logically and etymologically in the term bh&#257;va that came to denote &ldquo;mode of existence&rdquo; ( ibid , 565&ndash;568).

The Concept of Overall Freedom The concept of overall freedom appears to play an important role both in everyday discourse and in contemporary political philosophy. We commonly talk about people&rsquo;s freedom as increasing or decreasing , we say that one society is freer than another, and so on. Yet it is only over the last three decades that philosophers have moved beyond the question of what it is to be free to do or become this or that particular thing, in order to ask, in addition, whether we can make sense of descriptive claims to the effect that one person or society is freer than another, or of liberal normative claims to the effect that freedom should be maximized or that people should enjoy equal freedom or that they each have a right to a certain minimum level of freedom. The literal meaningfulness of such claims depends on the possibility of gauging degrees of overall freedom, sometimes comparatively, sometimes absolutely. Theorists disagree, however, about the importance of the notion of overall freedom. For some libertarian and liberal egalitarian theorists, freedom is valuable as such. This suggests that more freedom is better than less (at least ceteris paribus ), and that freedom is one of those goods that a liberal society ought to distribute in a certain way among individuals. For other liberal theorists, like Ronald Dworkin (1977, 2011) and the later Rawls (1991), freedom is not valuable as such, and all claims about maximal or equal freedom ought to be interpreted not as literal references to a scalar good called &lsquo;liberty&rsquo; but as elliptical references to the adequacy of this or that list of specific liberties, or types of liberties, selected on the basis of values other than liberty itself. Only the first group of theorists finds the notion of overall freedom interesting. The theoretical problems involved in measuring overall freedom include that of how an agent&rsquo;s available actions are to be individuated, counted and weighted, and that of comparing and weighting different constraint types (but not necessarily different constraint sources) such as physical prevention, punishability, threats and manipulation). How are we to make sense of the claim that the number of options available to a person has increased? Should all options count for the same in terms of degrees of freedom, or should they be weighted according to their importance in terms of other values? If the latter, does the notion of overall freedom really add anything of substance to the idea that people should be granted those specific freedoms that are valuable? Should the degree of variety among options also count? And how are we to compare the unfreedom created by the physical impossibility of an action with, say, the unfreedom created by the difficulty or costliness or punishability of an action? It is only by comparing these different kinds of actions and constraints that we shall be in a position to compare individuals&rsquo; overall degrees of freedom. These problems have been addressed, with differing degrees of optimism, not only by mainstream political philosophers (Steiner 1983; Carter 1999; Kramer 2003; Garnett 2016; C&ocirc;t&eacute; 2020; Carter and Steiner 2022) but also by theorists working at the interface between philosophy and economics, including social choice theorists interested in finding a freedom-based alternative to the standard utilitarian or welfarist framework that has tended to dominate their discipline (e.g. Pattanaik and Xu 1990, 1998; Hees 2000; Sen 2002; Sugden 1998, 2003, 2006; Bavetta 2004; Bavetta and Navarra 2012, 2014). A particularly important line of research within this social choice approach concerns the relevance of diversity of available options for the measurement of freedom (See e.g. Hees 2004; Sher 2021; Gravel et al. 2025; C&ocirc;t&eacute; 2025). MacCallum&rsquo;s framework is particularly well suited to the clarification of such issues. For this reason, theorists working on the measurement of freedom tend not to refer a great deal to the distinction between positive and negative freedom. Most of them are concerned with the notion of freedom understood as the availability of options. This notion of freedom is unequivocally negative in Berlin&rsquo;s sense at least where two conditions are met: first, the source of unfreedom is limited to the actions of other agents, so that natural or self-inflicted obstacles are not seen as decreasing an agent&rsquo;s overall freedom; second, the actions one is free or unfree to perform are weighted in some value-neutral way, so that one is not seen as freer simply because the options available to one are more valuable or conducive to one&rsquo;s self-realization. Of the above-mentioned authors, only Steiner embraces both conditions explicitly. Sen rejects both of them, despite not endorsing anything like positive freedom in Berlin&rsquo;s sense. Many social choice theorists seem to treat freedom as a dyadic relation, either assuming that freedom is identical to ability or remaining agnostic about the interpretation of MacCallum&rsquo;s constraint variable. 8. Is the Distinction Still Useful? We began with a simple distinction between two concepts of liberty, and have progressed from this to the recognition that liberty might be conceptualized in any number of ways, depending on how one interprets the three variables of agent, constraints, and purposes. Negative liberty is the absence of constraints, but so is positive liberty. Positive liberty is the possibility of realizing certain ends, but so is negative liberty. Despite the utility of MacCallum&rsquo;s triadic formula and its strong influence on analytic philosophers, however, Berlin&rsquo;s distinction has remained an important point of reference in contemporary debates. Is this continued use of the distinction philosophically well-founded? Let us consider some possible reasons for this persistence, while also noting their conceptual limits in light of the above discussion. A first reason for the persistence of the distinction might be thought to lie in the difference between what Charles Taylor, in his defense of positive liberty, called an opportunity concept and an exercise concept of freedom (C. Taylor 1979). If one thinks of freedom as an opportunity concept, then one is thinking of possibilities open to an agent, what the agent can do or, as Berlin put it, &lsquo;how many doors are open.&rsquo; If, by contrast, one thinks of freedom as involving self-direction, which implies the presence of control, one has in mind an exercise-concept of freedom as opposed to an opportunity-concept. If freedom is an exercise concept, it consists not merely in the possibility of doing certain things, but in actually doing certain things in certain ways&mdash;for example, in realizing one&rsquo;s true self or in acting on the basis of rational and well-informed decisions. MacCallum&rsquo;s single concept might be criticized as failing to capture this exercise concept of freedom, for it seems to cover only cases in which certain opportunities are open or closed for the agent. However, this defense of the positive-negative distinction, as coinciding with the distinction between exercise- and opportunity-concepts of freedom, has been challenged by Eric Nelson (2005). As Nelson points out, most of the theorists that are traditionally located in the positive camp, such as Green or Bosanquet, do not distinguish between freedom as the absence of constraints and freedom as the doing or becoming of certain things. For these theorists, freedom is the absence of any kind of constraint whatsoever on the realization of one&rsquo;s true self (they adopt a maximally extensive conception of constraints on freedom). The absence of all factors that could prevent the action x is, quite simply, equivalent to the realization of x . In other words, if there really is nothing stopping me from doing x &mdash;if I possess all the means to do x , and I have a desire to do x , and no desire, irrational or otherwise, not to do x &mdash;then I do x . If this argument is correct, then MacCallum&rsquo;s triadic relation accommodates the idea of freedom as an exercise concept. A second reason for the persistence of the distinction might be thought to lie in the centrality of the relation between freedom and ability. Many people seem to persist in the view that if freedom is about being able to do things, then freedom should be understood as a positive rather than a negative concept. Two limits of this view should be evident from the above discussion. First, freedom as ability fails to capture much of what Berlin had in mind in speaking of positive liberty. Second, some conceptions that are classified as negative by their proponents, such as Kramer&rsquo;s trivalent conception (2003) or Garnett&rsquo;s bivalent ability-based conception (2007; 2022a), identify freedom with ability. To these two limits can be added a third: Christman&rsquo;s conception of liberty, which is unequivocally positive, is in some respects ability-independent (2025). A third reason might lie in the importance of the distinction between morally-neutral conceptions and morally committed ones, where the former are classed as negative and the latter as positive: for many theorists in Berlin&rsquo;s negative camp, a person can be free to perform an action whether it is good or evil, valuable or disvaluable, permissible or impermissible, and they can be unfree to act irrespective of whether the prevention in question is justified or not. Most positive conceptions, by contrast, take the value or permissibility of actions and/or restrictions to be relevant for ascriptions of freedom and unfreedom.

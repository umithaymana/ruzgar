Some theorists answer that it does not render me unfree (Oppenheim 1985; Steiner 1994; Kramer 2003; Shnayderman 2013). Others argue that certain omissions, especially those for which agents can be held responsible, or those involving a failure to intervene where obstacles are easily removable, do count as sources of unfreedom (Kristj&aacute;nsson 1996; Miller 1985; Christman 2025). Among those who agree that only the conduct of others generates un freedom, there is further disagreement about what we might call the conditions of freedom . If I suffer from a natural or self-inflicted inability to do something, I am not unfree to do it, if unfreedom is understood as a social relation. But should we say that I therefore remain free to do it? Or should we instead say that the inability entails my lack of freedom to do it without entailing my un freedom to do it? The former position implies a &lsquo;bivalent&rsquo; conception of freedom, according to which a person is necessarily either free or unfree to do something (Steiner 2001; Carter and Steiner 2022). The latter position implies a &lsquo;trivalent&rsquo; conception, according to which there are some things that a person is neither free nor unfree to do. Kramer (2003) endorses a trivalent conception whereby freedom is identified with ability, and unfreedom with the prevention by others of outcomes that the agent would otherwise be able to bring about. The unfreedom to do x is a social relation; the freedom to do x is not. Some theorists go even further, arguing that we should not only identify freedom with ability, but we should also identify unfreedom with inability, thereby denying any distinction between unfreedoms and mere inabilities (Crocker 1980; Cohen 2011, pp. 193&ndash;97; Sen 1992; Van Parijs 1995; Garnett 2022a; Rainbolt 2024). The main motivation for this bivalent ability-based conception of freedom is that the distinction between unfreedom and mere inability lacks a principled justification and may reflect ideological bias, implying that mere inabilities are somehow less important than social unfreedoms. Bivalent freedom-as-ability is sometimes referred to as a kind of positive freedom&mdash;somewhat confusingly, given that it is very different from what Berlin meant by positive freedom. It is generally assumed in defenses of the so-called capability approach to justice and human development (Sen 1985, 1988, 1992; Nussbaum, 2006, 2011). The question of sources has important implications for how we assess economic constraints like recession, poverty and unemployment. Do such conditions merely render people unable to act, or do they also render them unfree? Libertarians and egalitarians have often provided contrasting answers to this question by appealing to different conceptions of the sources of constraints. One way of denying that such economic constraints produce unfreedom is to adopt an even more restrictive view of its sources, counting only those obstacles that are intentionally brought about by other persons. In this case, impersonal economic forces, being brought about unintentionally, can restrict people&rsquo;s options without making them any less free. This view has been adopted by several market-oriented libertarians, including, most famously, Friedrich von Hayek (1960, 1982), according to whom freedom is the absence of coercion, understood as subjection to the arbitrary will of another. (Notice the somewhat surprising similarity between this conception of freedom and the republican conception discussed earlier, in section 4). This narrow conception of a constraint is contested not only by those adopting the notion of freedom as ability, but also by those adopting a broader conception of the sources of social unfreedom. Notice, moreover, that economic hardship can be understood as creating unfreedom on both the causal view and the moral responsibility view of social freedom (see Cohen 2011 cited at the end of section 3). But why would one want to distinguish, in the first place, between unfreedom and mere inability? The mere claim that freedom is a social relation looks more like a stipulation than a justification. An answer might be found by examining more closely our ideas about the value of freedom. Freedom might be said to have &lsquo;ability-independent&rsquo; value: even when we are unable to act, the fact that our inability is not due to the conduct of others can itself be something that matters morally (Morriss 2012; Intropi 2022; Huseynli 2025). One reason for thinking this might be that abstaining from constraining others is part of what respectful conduct amounts to. It would explain why individuals may object to limits imposed by the law or the conduct of other individuals even where they already lacked the relevant abilities. The idea of freedom&rsquo;s ability-independent value supports the idea that debates about freedom cannot be reduced to those about ability or capability, since the normative significance of social freedom extends beyond what persons can do and become, to how they stand in relation to others . 6.2 Distinguishing Constraint Sources and Constraint Types Constraint types and constraint sources are often not distinguished carefully. For example, those who interpret freedom as a social relation are often said to count only those obstacles that are external to the agent. But the term &lsquo;external&rsquo; is ambiguous in this context, for it might refer either to the location of the obstacle itself, or to the location of its source (this ambiguity was present in the brief summary in section 1). Obstacles that count as internal in terms of their location include psychological phenomena such as ignorance, irrational desires, illusions and phobias. Such obstacles can arise in various ways: for example, they might have a genetic origin, or they might be brought about intentionally by others, as in the case of brainwashing or manipulation. In the first case we have an internal constraint brought about by features of the agent herself, and in this sense internally ; in the second, an internal constraint intentionally imposed by another agent, and in this sense externally . The two dimensions of type and source are therefore logically independent: it is theoretically possible to combine a narrow view of what counts as a source of a constraint with a broad view of what types of obstacle count as unfreedom-generating constraints, or vice versa . As a result, it is not clear that theorists who are normally placed in the negative camp need deny the existence of constraints on freedom that are located internally (see Kramer 2003; Garnett 2007). To illustrate the independence of the two dimensions of type and source, let us compare the case of Hayek, cited in the previous subsection, with that of the unorthodox libertarian Hillel Steiner (1974&ndash;5, 1994). On the one hand, Steiner has a much broader view than Hayek of the possible sources of constraints on freedom: he does not limit the set of such sources to intentional actions of other persons, but extends it to cover all actions of other agents, believing that any limitation of such social sources can only be an arbitrary stipulation, usually arising from some ideological bias. On the other hand, Steiner has an even narrower view than Hayek about what type of obstacle counts as a constraint on freedom: for Steiner, an agent counts as unfree to do something only if it is physically impossible for her to do that thing. Any extension of the constraint variable to include other types of obstacle, such as the costs anticipated in coercive threats, would, in his view, necessarily involve a reference to the agent&rsquo;s desires, and we have seen (in sec. 2) that for those liberals in the negative camp there is no conceptually necessary relation between an agent&rsquo;s freedom and her desires. Consider the coercive threat &lsquo;Your money or your life!&rsquo;. This does not make it impossible for you to refuse to hand over your money, only much less desirable for you to do so. If you decide not to hand over the money, you will suffer the cost of being killed. That will count as a restriction of your freedom, because it will render physically impossible a great number of actions on your part. But it is not the issuing of the threat that creates this unfreedom, and you are not unfree until the sanction (described in the threat) is carried out. For this reason, Steiner excludes threats&mdash;and with them all other kinds of imposed costs&mdash;from the set of obstacles that count as freedom-restricting. This conception of freedom derives from Hobbes ( Leviathan , chs. 14 and 21), and its defenders often call it the pure negative conception (M. Taylor 1982; Steiner 1994; Carter and Kramer 2008; cf. Flathman 1987) to distinguish it from those impure negative conceptions that make at least minimal references to the agent&rsquo;s beliefs, desires or values (Oppenheim 1961, 1981; Benn and Weinstein 1971; Miller 1983). Steiner&rsquo;s account of the relation between freedom and coercive threats might be thought to have counterintuitive implications. Many laws that are normally thought to restrict negative freedom do not physically prevent people from doing what is prohibited, but deter them from doing so by threatening punishment. Are we to say, then, that these laws do not restrict the negative freedom of those who obey them? A solution to this problem may consist in saying that although a law against doing some action, x , does not remove the freedom to do x , it nevertheless renders physically impossible certain combinations of actions that include doing x and doing what would be precluded by the punishment.

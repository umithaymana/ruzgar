As both a legal and as a philosophical matter, the question &ldquo;if the defendant had not been negligent, would the plaintiff have suffered the same injury?&rdquo; seems to admit of no single answer, but rather to depend on which of the untaken precautions forms the relevant contrast case (Gilboa 2019). 4.8 Emerging Issues: Public Nuisance and the Tort Liability of Public Authorities As a common-law subject, most of tort law is the result of decisions made by judges adjudicating specific disputes. Broader changes in social life and legal structures give rise to new disputes and generate novel doctrines. Some of these doctrinal developments give new urgency to familiar philosophical questions, in particular, the question of whether tort law is simply a matter of liability rules or whether it is a matter of interpersonal wrongdoing. Other developments raise novel philosophical questions. This is particularly clear in the interaction of tort law with what are widely perceived as broad social problems, including the opioid crisis, climate change, and worries about corruption and official misconduct. Litigants concerned to convince courts to grant a remedy unsurprisingly adopt the strategy of drawing on existing doctrines. This has led to a renewed interest in old tort of public nuisance and both old and novel torts involving the conduct of public officials. The other torts discussed in this entry all involve as private requirements that one person to avoid interfering with another. Both public nuisance and many torts involving public officials depend on requirements from public law that private persons avoid interfering with public provision, or that public officials do their jobs as assigned. They therefore raise general issues of how a public requirement crystallizes into a private claim. The tort of public nuisance protects the plaintiff&rsquo;s right to use something as a member of the public. Early cases involved blockage of public highways or navigable waterways, which are supposed to be open to all members of the public and have rules of access requiring that users not interfere with the ability of others to use them. The standard remedy for a public nuisance is an injunction, requiring the defendant to abate the nuisance, although damages are awarded for losses incurred. The early cases gave rise to distinctive doctrinal structures, the most significant of which is the requirement that the plaintiff must show &ldquo;special damage.&rdquo; If someone blocks a road, all members of the public are prevented from passing on it, but only the person who finds their passage blocked is entitled to sue. By the nineteenth century, industrialization and urbanization led to new concerns about pollution and to an incremental expansion of common rights to include the right to fish in tidal waters, to draw portable water from a river, or to be free of threats to public health. In recent decades, scholars, governments and activists have brought actions for public nuisance to deal with social and environmental issues. Cities have brought suits against manufacturers of products that cause social problems including opioids and guns, demanding either damages for expenses incurred or that the source of the nuisance be abated. Both activists and governments have brought common-law actions against major releasers of greenhouse gases. These cases have been unsuccessful in the United States, but one was recently successful in New Zealand, and others are pending in other common law jurisdictions. They raise doctrinal questions that raise philosophical ones: virtually everyone is affected by climate change; does the requirement of special damage require that the plaintiff&rsquo;s injury be unique, or only that a factual loss be suffered? Climate change is the result of massive numbers of greenhouse gas emitters, not all of whom are before the court; in what sense does the defendant cause the specific detriment of which the plaintiff complains? More generally, what is the right way to conceptualize common rights? Do they extend to something on which everyone depends, but nobody uses in the way in which people use a road or waterway? (Neyers 2017; Bullock 2022;) These issues are attracting increasing attention from philosophers and legal scholars. (Kendrick 2023, Goldberg 2024; Ripstein 2024) Another area of litigation giving rise to both doctrinal development and philosophical questions is the tort liability of public officials. At common law, the Crown or sovereign was immune from tort liability; by the middle of the twentieth century common-law jurisdictions had waived this immunity, raising questions of whether official action was subject to the same rules as the action of private persons. Other claims against public authorities involve the exercise of official powers such as issuing licenses, supervising regulated industries and investigating and prosecuting crimes. Public officials are often thought to owe duties to the government or to the public in general, raising questions of the conditions under which failure to do their jobs gives rise to a private wrong. Prominent recent examples include cases in which someone claims that an official violated their constitutional rights; cases in which a plaintiff complains of an injury sustained because an official knowingly acted outside their powers, for example by refusing to shut down an insolvent bank or investigate a shooting involving police (Chamberlain 2012) or refused deport a dangerous criminal because he is a police informant, or brought criminal charges against someone as a way of advancing some private purpose (Law-Smith 2024). Other cases involve tort liability for injuries inflicted in wartime. (Abraham 2024). They raise philosophical questions about the status of intention, because they require that the official be aware of the impropriety of their action, thus making them different from more familiar tort doctrines that look at what a reasonable or ordinary person would know rather than what the actual person did know. They also raise broader issues about the nature of the rule of law, the powers of public officials, and the role of private law in managing the conduct of public officials.

We next examine a related question as to whether search engine companies in general have some special social/moral responsibilities, in light of their important societal roles. 4. Moral Accountability and Social-Responsibility Issues for Search Engine Companies Thus far, issues from the perspectives of business ethics and professional responsibility have not been directly addressed in this entry; our main focus in Section 3 has been on the kinds of moral impacts that search engines have for ordinary users, especially with respect to privacy, surveillance, and freedom. In Section 3.1 , however, we hinted that search engine companies (SECs) might be held morally accountable for their practices involving bias in the ranking of search results. And in concluding Section 3.4 , we briefly entertained the notion that major SECs might be held accountable for policies and practices that either favor or directly support censorship. In this section, we consider whether SECs have any special social responsibilities and moral obligations because of their &ldquo;privileged place&rdquo; in our society. We begin by briefly describing a key societal role that search engines play in providing access to information and knowledge. Elgesem (2008: 241) notes that search engines have an important societal role as &ldquo;contributors to the public use of reason &rdquo;, and Noble (2018: 148) argues that search results can actually &ldquo;structure knowledge&rdquo; for the societies within which they operate. As we already noted in Section 3 , Hinman (2005, 2008) and Diaz (2008) view search engines as &ldquo;gatekeepers&rdquo; of knowledge on the Web. Taddeo and Floridi (2016: 1575), who also describe the important &ldquo;gatekeeping function&rdquo; that SECs like Google now have because of their &ldquo;centrality&rdquo; in information societies, worry about the lack of consensus thus far with regard to which &ldquo;principles should shape&hellip;[an SEC&rsquo;s]&hellip;moral responsibilities and practices&rdquo;. The authors argue that we need an &ldquo;ethical framework&rdquo; both to &ldquo;define&rdquo; an SEC&rsquo;s responsibilities and &ldquo;provide the fundamental sharable principles&rdquo; necessary to guide an SEC&rsquo;s conduct &ldquo;within the multicultural and international context in which they operate&rdquo;. But Taddeo and Floridi also believe that this problem applies to other kinds of major &ldquo;online service providers&rdquo;, which the authors refer to as &ldquo;OSPs&rdquo;; they include Meta (formerly Facebook) and X (formerly Twitter) as well as Google under that category. However, we limit our focus on moral-responsibility issues in this section to SECs per se. Does the central role that major SECs like Google and Bing now play as &ldquo;gatekeepers&rdquo; of knowledge in our information society entail some special responsibilities for them? Hinman (2005: 21) lists four reasons why these companies should shoulder significant social responsibility. First, he notes that search engines &ldquo;play an absolutely crucial role&rdquo; in accessing information and that without them, the Web would &ldquo;simply be inaccessible to us&rdquo; and thus &ldquo;almost useless&rdquo;. Second, he points out that access to information is &ldquo;crucial for responsible citizenship&rdquo;, also noting that &ldquo;citizens in a democracy cannot make informed decisions without access to accurate information&rdquo;. Third, Hinman notes that search engines have become &ldquo;central to education&rdquo;, and he points out that students now search on Google and other major search engines more frequently than they visit libraries. Fourth, he points out that major search engines are owned by private corporations&mdash;i.e., by businesses that are mainly, and &ldquo;quite properly&rdquo;, interested in making a profit. Regarding Hinman&rsquo;s fourth point, it would seem that conflicts can easily arise because of an SEC&rsquo;s mission to be profitable and its broader societal role in providing access to information in a manner that is fair, accurate, and unbiased. 4.1 Commerce-Related Conflicts for SECs Consider one kind of conflict involving bias and profit for SECs. Nicas (2011: 1) points out that while many SECs were initially &ldquo;content to simply produce search results&rdquo;, some are now very much involved in diverse markets, &ldquo;offering everything from online music to local coupons to mobile phones&rdquo;. Nicas also describes a bias-related concern affecting this trend by noting that when Google entered into the online travel business, it &ldquo;began placing its new flight-search service atop general search results&rdquo;&mdash;i.e., above those of other major players in the online travel business such as Orbitz and Expedia. Because Google also engages in these kinds of practices in EU countries, where Google holds over 90% market share (Bianchi 2024), it has and continues to face charges of anti-trust violations. In Europe, Google has been formally charged with &ldquo;systematically favoring its own comparison shopping product&rdquo; by prominently displaying that product/service in its search returns, &ldquo;irrespective of its merits&rdquo; and at the expense of its competitors (Chappell 2015). If the accounts provided by Nicas, Chappell, and others are correct, it would seem that there are good reasons to be concerned about commercial conflicts involving major SECs such as Google. A relatively new bias-related conflict for SECs has recently surfaced in connection with &ldquo;voice search&rdquo;, which is becoming increasingly popular with users of mobile devices. Barysevich (2016) notes that voice search is the &ldquo;fastest growing type of search&rdquo;, pointing to statistics in the U.S.showing that 55% of teenagers and approximately 40% of adults use this mode of search on a daily basis. Why are users&rsquo; searches trending in the direction of voice versus (&ldquo;traditional&rdquo;) text? Hawkins (2017 [ Other Internet Resources ]) offers two reasons: (a) voice search is faster than text, since most people also tend to speak much more quickly than they type; and (b) voice search is hands-free, which is convenient for people using mobile devices. Given Google&rsquo;s preeminence&mdash;and some might even say its &ldquo;dominance&rdquo;&mdash;in the online &ldquo;search industry&rdquo; to date, one might assume that Google has also been the lead player in the emerging area of voice search. But Hawkins points out that Bing has had an edge over Google in the voice-search market thus far. He attributes this to the fact that Apple&rsquo;s Siri, Microsoft&rsquo;s Cortana, and Amazon&rsquo;s Alexa&mdash;three of the four major &ldquo;virtual assistants&rdquo; on the market&mdash;use Bing for their searches. Of course, one might well expect that Microsoft&rsquo;s voice-assistant applications would direct its users&rsquo; searches to Bing, and that Google&rsquo;s voice assistants would do likewise in directing users to its search engine. But one could also question whether voice-search users, especially of products/services other than Microsoft&rsquo;s and Google&rsquo;s, should be given an explicit option to set default voice-search requests on their devices to a specific search engine of their choice? Otherwise, companies like Amazon and Apple could be perceived as biased because of their tilting, even if unintentionally, the voice-search market toward Bing (and away from Google)? However, since Google has already cornered so much of the existing search and search-related markets, it might be difficult for us to view that SEC as somehow itself a victim of (search) bias. Some critics have pointed out that conflicts of interest involving SECs are not limited to bias and unfair business practices in the commercial sector, but can also affect the free flow of knowledge in society. For example, Carr (2010: 163) worries that the commercial control that Google and other major SECs now have over the &ldquo;distribution of digital information&rdquo; could ultimately lead to &ldquo;restrictions on the flow of knowledge&rdquo;. And Hinman (2008: 67) believes that the control of knowledge that SECs have is &ldquo;in a very fundamental sense, a public trust, yet it remains firmly ensconced in private hands and behind a veil of corporate secrecy&rdquo;. Much of this secrecy, as we have seen, is closely tied to the proprietary search algorithms that major search engines use, which also raises the question of whether aspects of these algorithms should be more transparent to the general public. But Elgesem (2008: 241) believes that SECs should not be required to disclose information about their proprietary search algorithms, arguing instead that they should be required to (a) make their policies known to users and (b) follow those policies as closely as possible. We conclude this section by noting that SECs continue to face some critical challenges with respect to fulfilling their &ldquo;gatekeeper role&rdquo; in a socially responsible way, while simultaneously protecting both their proprietary algorithms and the interests of their shareholders to whom they also have legal and moral obligations.

Much of the appeal of freedom as non-domination lies in its apparent ability to capture phenomena such as oppression and structural injustice without appealing to internal constraints. It has been applied not only to relations of domination between governments and citizens, but also to relations of domination between employers and workers (Breen and McBride 2015), between husbands and wives (Lovett 2022), and between able-bodied and disabled people (De Wispelaere and Casassas 2014; S&eacute;pulchre 2022). It remains to be seen, however, whether republican freedom is ultimately distinguishable from negative freedom, or whether republicans have instead simply provided good arguments to the effect that negative freedom is best promoted, on balance and over time , through certain kinds of political institutions rather than others. While there is no conceptually necessary connection between negative liberty and democratic government, there may nevertheless be a strong empirical correlation between the two. Many theorists have argued that republican policies are therefore best defended empirically on the basis of negative freedom, rather than by rejecting that concept (Carter 1999, 2008; Kramer 2003, 2008, Goodin and Jackson 2007; Moen 2024). An important premise in such an argument is that the extent of a person&rsquo;s negative freedom is a function not simply of how many single actions are prevented, but of how many different act-combinations are prevented. People who can achieve their goals only by bowing and scraping to their masters must be seen as less free, negatively, than people who can achieve those goals unconditionally. Another important premise is that the extent to which people are negatively free depends, in part, on the probability with which they would be constrained from performing future acts or act-combinations should they attempt to do so. People who are subject to arbitrary power are less free in the negative sense, even if they do not actually suffer interference, because the probability of their suffering constraints is always greater ( ceteris paribus , as a matter of empirical fact) than it would be if they were not subject to that arbitrary power. Only this greater probability, these liberal theorists say, can adequately explain the concerns expressed by republicans about the fear , the sense of exposure , and the precariousness of the dominated (see Bruin 2009; Lang 2012; Shnayderman 2012; Kirby 2016; Carter and Shnayderman 2019). In reply, republicans have insisted that freedom as non-domination is distinct from negative liberty because what matters for an agent&rsquo;s freedom is not the improbability but the impossibility of others interfering. For example, a husband might be kind and generous, and therefore be extremely unlikely ever to deny his wife the same opportunities as he himself enjoys; but the wife is still dominated if the structure of norms in her society is such as to permit husbands to frustrate the choices of their wives in numerous ways. If she lives in such a society, she is still subject to the husband&rsquo;s power whether he likes it or not. Moreover, the wife&rsquo;s subjection to his power will tend to influence how third parties treat her &ndash; for example, in terms of offering employment opportunities. Taken at face value, however, the requirement of impossibility of interference seems over-demanding, as it is never completely impossible for others to constrain me. For example, it is not impossible that I be stabbed by someone as I walk down the street this afternoon. If the mere possibility of the stabbing makes me unfree to walk down the street, then unfreedom is everywhere and the achievement of freedom is impossible. To avoid this worry, republicans have qualified their impossibility requirement: for me to be free to walk down the street, it must be impossible for others to stab me with impunity (Pettit 2008a, 2008b; Skinner 2008). But this qualification is open to objections. Is impunity a purely formal requirement, or should we say that no one can carry out a street stabbing with impunity if, say, at least 70% of such stabbings lead to prosecution? Even if 100% of such stabbings lead to prosecution, there will still be some stabbings. Will they not be sources of unfreedom for the victims? Another problem with the requirement of impossibility is that there will always be some group of individuals who, if they coordinated, could interfere arbitrarily with me. Such coalitions are possible , even if unlikely . Since no one is powerful enough to resist the interference of all such possible coalitions, not only the weak but also the powerful turn out to be dominated by others who might act in concert. Domination is thus pervasive, and republican freedom impossible to achieve (Dowding 2011; Simpson 2017). To avoid this conclusion, republicans have argued that not all potential coalitions possess dominating power. Consider two would-be muggers who might wish to coordinate an attack on me. They form a potential team but, it might be said, they do not dominate me unless they share an awareness of one another&rsquo;s intentions and have a salient strategy for acting together. When such epistemic conditions are met, they constitute a capable team, and only capable teams dominate. Non-domination, while demanding, is therefore achievable (Lovett and Pettit 2018). Yet this reply has something of an ad hoc feel. Moreover, it does not conclusively resolve the problem, since sufficiently large or suitably situated groups will still qualify as capable teams. Too many agents seem to retain the capacity to form dominating coalitions (Simpson 2019). A more recent republican response to the impossibility problem invokes the concept of &lsquo;ignorability&rsquo; (Ingham and Lovett 2019). The idea is that each potentially interfering agent has an actual type, consisting in her actual preferences regarding whether to interfere, and a range of possible types corresponding to preferences she might have in other possible worlds. There are some possible interfering types that can be ignored and therefore need not be considered sources of domination. They can be ignored when the common knowledge of a discrepancy between the actual type and the possible interfering types does not have significant practical consequences. To illustrate: in a traditional patriarchal society where husbands are generally expected or permitted to relate to their wives in coercive or exploitative ways, the news that a particular husband is benevolent will have significant practical consequences: third parties will treat his wife as more autonomous and, say, offer her employment. His possible interfering types are therefore not ignorable, and she is dominated. By contrast, in a liberal society like San Francisco, where husbands are generally expected to be non-interfering, the same news changes nothing significant in anyone&rsquo;s behavior. His possible interfering types are therefore ignorable, and his wife is free. Hence, a possible interfering type is ignorable if and only if its becoming common knowledge that the agent&rsquo;s actual type does not belong to that set of possible interfering types would have no significant practical consequences. By narrowing the range of possible worlds that freedom pertains to, the notion of ignorability is intended to make room for the possibility of republican freedom. Negative liberty theorists have raised two main objections to this move (Jafarov and Huseynli 2022). The first is that the criterion of ignorability is reducible to a threshold of sufficiently low probability. To say that the absence of certain interfering types would have &lsquo;no significant practical consequences&rsquo; is, in effect, to say that interference is already unlikely enough to be disregarded by rational agents. Ignorability therefore collapses into the probabilistic standard that republicans sought to reject. The second objection challenges the claim that ignorability entails non-domination (assuming the republican account of domination). There are cases in which an agent&rsquo;s possible interfering types are ignorable, yet domination persists because the agent retains an insufficiently constrained capacity to interfere. For example, suppose a husband has an unconstrained ability to prevent his wife from leaving the home, but she is terminally ill and in a deep depression and has no desire to leave, and everyone in society is aware of this fact. Since nobody&rsquo;s behavior would change upon learning that the husband is not the type to exercise his power, his possible interfering types are ignorable. Yet she is dominated, since he retains an unconstrained ability to frustrate her choices. The possibility of domination with ignorability suggests that the notion of ignorability excludes cases that republicans ought, out of consistency, to count as unfreedom (for a response, see Lovett and Ingham 2022; and for further discussion, see Inoue 2025; Moen 2025). The jury is still out on whether republicans have successfully carved out a third concept of freedom that is distinct from those of negative and positive liberty. This conceptual uncertainty need not itself cast doubt on the distinctness and attractiveness of republicanism as a set of political prescriptions. Rather, what it leaves open is the question of the ultimate normative bases of those prescriptions: is non-domination something that supervenes on certain configurations of negative freedom and unfreedom, and therefore explainable in terms of such configurations, or is it something truly distinct from those configurations? 5.

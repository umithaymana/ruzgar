How, after all, could they see their disagreement as one about the nature of liberty if they did not think of themselves as in some sense talking about the same thing ? In an influential article, the American legal philosopher Gerald MacCallum (1967) put forward the following answer: there is in fact only one basic concept of freedom, on which both sides in the debate converge . What the so-called negative and positive theorists disagree about is how this single concept of freedom should be interpreted. Indeed, in MacCallum&rsquo;s view, there are a great many different possible interpretations of freedom, and it is only Berlin&rsquo;s artificial dichotomy that has led us to think in terms of there being two. MacCallum defines the basic concept of freedom&mdash;the concept on which everyone agrees&mdash;as follows: a subject, or agent, is free from certain constraints, or preventing conditions, to do or become certain things. Freedom is therefore a triadic relation &mdash;that is, a relation between three things : an agent, certain preventing conditions, and certain doings or becomings of the agent. Any statement about freedom or unfreedom can be translated into a statement of the above form by specifying what is free or unfree, from what it is free or unfree, and what it is free or unfree to do or become . Any claim about the presence or absence of freedom in a given situation will therefore make certain assumptions about what counts as an agent, what counts as a constraint or limitation on freedom, and what counts as a purpose that the agent can be described as either free or unfree to carry out. The definition of freedom as a triadic relation was first put forward in the seminal work of Felix Oppenheim in the 1950s and &rsquo; 60s. Oppenheim saw that an important meaning of &lsquo;freedom&rsquo; in the context of political and social philosophy was as a relation between two agents and a particular (impeded or unimpeded) action. However, Oppenheim&rsquo;s interpretation of freedom was an example of what Berlin would call a negative concept. What MacCallum did was to generalize this triadic structure so that it would cover all possible claims about freedom, whether of the negative or the positive variety. In MacCallum&rsquo;s framework, unlike in Oppenheim&rsquo;s, the interpretation of each of the three variables is left open. In other words, MacCallum&rsquo;s position is a meta-theoretical one: his is a theory about the differences between theorists of freedom. To illustrate MacCallum&rsquo;s point, let us return to the example of the smoker driving to the tobacco shop. In describing this person as either free or unfree, we make assumptions about each of MacCallum&rsquo;s three variables. If we say that the driver is free , what we probably mean is that an agent, consisting in the driver&rsquo;s empirical self, is free from external (physical or legal) obstacles to do whatever he or she might want to do. If, on the other hand, we say that the driver is unfree , what we probably mean is that an agent, consisting in a higher or rational self, is made unfree by internal, psychological constraints to carry out some rational, authentic or virtuous plan. Notice that in both claims there is a negative element and a positive element: each claim about freedom assumes both that freedom is freedom from something (i.e., preventing conditions) and that it is freedom to do or become something. The dichotomy between freedom from and freedom to is therefore a false one, and it is misleading to say that those who see the driver as free employ a negative concept and those who see the driver as unfree employ a positive one. What these two camps differ over is the way in which one should interpret each of the three variables in the triadic freedom-relation. More precisely, what they differ over is the extension to be assigned to each of the variables. Thus, those whom Berlin places in the negative camp typically conceive of the agent as having the same extension as that which it is generally given in ordinary discourse: they tend to think of the agent as an individual human being and as including all of the empirical beliefs and desires of that individual. Those in the so-called positive camp, on the other hand, often depart from the ordinary notion, in one sense imagining the agent as more extensive than in the ordinary notion, and in another sense imagining it as less extensive: they think of the agent as having a greater extension than in ordinary discourse in cases where they identify the agent&rsquo;s true desires and aims with those of some collectivity of which she is a member; and they think of the agent as having a lesser extension than in ordinary discourse in cases where they identify the true agent with only a subset of her empirical beliefs and desires&mdash;i.e., with those that are rational, authentic or virtuous. Secondly, those in Berlin&rsquo;s positive camp tend to take a wider view of what counts as a constraint on freedom than those in his negative camp: the set of relevant obstacles is more extensive for the former than for the latter, since negative theorists tend to count only external obstacles as constraints on freedom, whereas positive theorists also allow that one may be constrained by internal factors, such as irrational desires, fears or ignorance. And thirdly, those in Berlin&rsquo;s positive camp tend to take a narrower view of what counts as a purpose one can be free to fulfill. The set of relevant purposes is less extensive for them than for the negative theorists, for we have seen that they tend to restrict the relevant set of actions or states to those that are rational, authentic or virtuous, whereas those in the negative camp tend to extend this variable so as to cover any action or state the agent might desire. On MacCallum&rsquo;s analysis, then, there is no simple dichotomy between positive and negative liberty; rather, we should recognize that there is a whole range of possible interpretations or conceptions of the single concept of liberty. (The distinction between a concept and its various conceptions comes from Rawls 1971, p.5. A number of authors have applied it to the concept of liberty, explicating that concept by means of MacCallum&rsquo;s triadic formula&mdash;see e.g. Gray 1991.) 6. The Analysis of Constraints To illustrate the range of conceptions of the concept of liberty made available by MacCallum&rsquo;s analysis, let us now take a closer look at his second variable&mdash;that of constraints. These can be classified along two logically independent dimensions: their sources and their types. By constraint-source we mean the factors that bring about a constraint: must an obstacle be attributable to the conduct of other persons in order to count as a constraint on freedom, or can purely natural events and self-inflicted incapacities also render one unfree? By constraint-types we mean classes of constraints identified in terms of their intrinsic properties: are constraints on freedom limited to physical barriers such as walls or locked doors, or can they include mental barriers such as phobias?; and does a constraint on freedom necessarily render an action impossible, or does it suffice that it make the action more difficult or costly? 6.1 Sources of Constraint Most proponents of negative conceptions&mdash;including, as we have seen, Berlin&mdash;hold that freedom is essentially a social relation . They therefore limit the range of obstacles that count as constraints on freedom to those attributable to the conduct of other agents. If I am incapacitated by natural causes&mdash;by a genetic handicap, say, or by being struck by lightning&mdash;I may be rendered unable to do certain things, but I am not, for that reason, rendered unfree to do them. This observation yields a distinction between unfreedoms , understood as inabilities due to the conduct of other persons, and mere inabilities , which are due to nature or to oneself. If you lock me in my house, I am both unable and unfree to leave. But if I am unable to leave because of a naturally caused illness or a snow drift which has blocked the door from the outside, or because I locked the door from the inside and destroyed the key, I am not unfree to leave. There are different ways of cashing out the idea that the source of unfreedom is the conduct of other persons. One possibility is the causal view, according to which I am unfree to do something whenever my inability is causally attributable to another person&rsquo;s action (Oppenheim 1961, 1985; Steiner 1994; Kramer 2003). Another possibility is the moral responsibility view, according to which we must ask whether the agent who created the obstacle can be held morally responsible for my inability (Kristj&aacute;nsson 1996; Miller 1983; Shnayderman 2013; Schmidt 2016). The latter view makes freedom partly dependent on moral judgments, whereas the former treats freedom as an empirical behavioural concept, requiring only knowledge of causal relations, and therefore allows persons with different moral judgements to agree on whether someone is unfree. It has recently been suggested that if moral responsibility is understood broadly, as moral answerability , the two views may converge, since the set of actions for which agents are answerable is arguably coextensive with the set of actions that generate unfreedom under the causality view. On this capacious account, agents can, in principle, be asked to answer for any obstacle their actions causally bring about, where answerability does not entail any specific moral response such as blame or praise (Huseynli 2026).

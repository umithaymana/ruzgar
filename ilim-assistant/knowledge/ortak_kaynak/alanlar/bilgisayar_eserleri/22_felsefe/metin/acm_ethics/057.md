Conclusion Bibliography Academic Tools Other Internet Resources Related Entries 1. Introduction and Overview It may be difficult to imagine today&rsquo;s world without search engines. Which school-aged child has not used a Web search engine to query about some topic or subject? Of course, it is quite possible that many Internet users, both young and old, do not consciously distinguish between the search engines they use and Web browsers that now also typically include search engines as a feature within their user interface. But virtually all Internet users have come to expect and depend on the instantaneous results they receive in response to their various search queries. Search engines function across multiple domains: they are technological systems, user interfaces, economic tools, and societal influencers. Thus, how to define what a &ldquo;search engine&rdquo; is can be difficult. For purposes of this entry, however, the definition of a (Web) search engine, put forth by Halavais (2008: 5&ndash;6), is &ldquo;an information retrieval system that allows for keyword searches of distributed digital text&rdquo;. We note that this definition includes some important technical terms and concepts that, in turn, need defining and further elucidation. Our examination of key technical concepts underlying search engines is intended to provide a useful context for our analysis of the ethical implications. In this sense, Blanke (2005: 34) is correct that an adequate analysis of the ethical aspects of search engines &ldquo;requires knowledge about the technology and its functioning&rdquo;. We begin with a brief sketch of the history and evolution of search engines, from their conception in the pre-Internet era to the development and implementation of contemporary (&ldquo;Web 2.0&rdquo; era) search engines such as Google. Our examination of important historical developments of this technology is intended to address our first question, noted above: &ldquo;What is a search engine?&rdquo; It also provides a backdrop for analyzing our second major question, &ldquo;What are the ethical implications of search engines?&rdquo; where a cluster of ethical concerns involving search engine technology is examined. These include issues ranging from search engine bias and the problem of opacity/non-transparency, to concerns affecting privacy and surveillance, to a set of issues involving censorship and democracy. We also describe some ethical concerns affecting (cyber)security&mdash;at the data, system, and (inter)national levels&mdash;generated by the use of &ldquo;discoverable search engines&rdquo; in the context of the Internet of Things. Additionally, we question whether search engine companies have any special moral obligations, e.g., in light of their &ldquo;privileged&rdquo; placed in society as &ldquo;gatekeepers of the Web&rdquo; (Hinman 2005: 21), for which they should be held accountable. In analyzing this question, we also examine a key role that trust now plays for users who have come to depend on search engines, and the companies that develop them, for access to accurate information. Finally, in the concluding section, we briefly mention some impacts that search engines have for broader philosophical issues (especially in the area of epistemology) that may not be solely or mainly ethical in nature. However, an adequate analysis of these issues is beyond the scope of this entry. 2. Search Engine Development and Evolution: A Short History Because search engines provide Internet users with access to important information by directing them to links to available online resources on a plethora of topics, many are inclined to see search engine technology in a positive light; some might also assume, as Noble (2018) and others note, that this technology is &ldquo;value-neutral&rdquo;. However, search engines can raise a number of ethical controversies. Before examining these controversies, however, we first briefly discuss the history of search engine technology via categories that, for our purposes, reflect five distinct eras: (i) Pre-Internet, (ii) Internet (pre-Web), (iii) early Web, (iv) Web 2.0, and (v) AI-enhanced Web.

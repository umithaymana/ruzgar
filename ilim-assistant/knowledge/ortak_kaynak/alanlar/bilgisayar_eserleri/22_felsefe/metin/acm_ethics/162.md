Each position designates a type of normative relation between a right-subject and a right-object (Raz 1970: 176). &ldquo;Right-subject&rdquo; denotes a right&rsquo;s holder. &ldquo;Right-object&rdquo; denotes the party the right is held over or against. Finally, a right&rsquo;s &ldquo; content &rdquo; denotes the normative component of the right; that is, the specific normative relation obtaining pursuant to the right between the right&rsquo;s subject and its object (Sumner 1987: 24). As normative relations , each one of the four Hohfeldian positions held by right-subjects correlates to a certain type of normative position in the right-object. Altogether, Hohfeld identifies four pairs of correlatives: claim/duty ; privilege/no-claim ; power/liability ; and, immunity/disability . For example, X (right-subject) can hold a contractual claim against Y (right-object) that Y paint X &rsquo;s house, where Y is under a correlative duty to X to paint her house (right-content). Claims and privileges are first-order norms, respectively determinative of what the relevant parties ought to do or may do. Powers and immunities are second-order norms (that is pertaining to other norms), respectively determinative of whether parties are able or unable to change certain norms (Sumner 1987: 27&ndash;29). Some Hohfeldian positions are active while others passive (Lyons 1970; Sumner 1987: 29&ndash;31). Rights that are actively exercised determine what right- holders may do (privilege-right) or can do (power-right). Rights which right-holders enjoy passively, determine what right- objects ought to do (claim-right) or cannot do (immunity-right). 3.1.1 Claim-Right While law deploys the term &ldquo;right&rdquo; to refer to all four Hohfeldian positions, &ldquo;rights&rdquo; are typically most associated with claim-rights, which Hohfeld denotes as right &ldquo;in its strictest sense&rdquo;. A &ldquo;claim&rdquo; is a type of entitlement (Kamm 2002: 476). X has a claim- right against Y with respect to some &phi; if and only if Y is under a duty to X to &phi;. Claim-rights are, therefore, &ldquo;directed&rdquo;&mdash;the claim of the right-subject is directed at the right-object, whose correlative duty is owed to (directed at) the right-subject. Claim-rights correlate either to &ldquo;negative&rdquo; duties not to impede one&rsquo;s &phi;-ing (e.g., the right not to be defamed) or to &ldquo;positive&rdquo; duties to actively further one&rsquo;s &phi;-ing (e.g., the right to receive state welfare benefits) (Fitzgerald [Salmond] 1966: 234&ndash;235). Examples of legal claim-rights are property owners&rsquo; rights against others not to trespass on their land, or promisees&rsquo; contractual rights that promisors do as they promised. 3.1.2 Privilege (or Liberty) That X holds against Y a privilege to &phi; entails that X is not under a duty to Y not to &phi; and, correlatively, that Y does not hold a claim against X not to &phi;. Put differently, a privilege to do something is a directed liberty or permission to do so. Accordingly, the Hohfeldian opposite of a privilege to &phi; is a duty not to &phi;. Similarly to claim-rights, a privilege can be positive&mdash;that is liberty to act&mdash;or negative&mdash;that is liberty to omit from acting&mdash;or both. In the literature, Hohfeld&rsquo;s term &ldquo;privilege&rdquo; is often exchanged for &ldquo;liberty&rdquo;, terms here used interchangeably. Are privileges (or liberties) genuine &ldquo;rights&rdquo;, as Hohfeld posits? In some respects, a liberty is the paradigm of a right (e.g., Hobbes 1651: ch. XIV). Yet, at least in law, there is something misleading in the Hohfeldian notion that standalone privileges (&ldquo;naked privileges&rdquo;) qualify as &ldquo;rights&rdquo;. After all, how does a privilege to &phi; count as a legal right, if the privilege-holder does not also hold some accompanying legal claim or other form of legal insolation against interference with her &phi;-ing? (Hart 1982: 173; Sumner 1987: 35; Raz 1994: 275). In fact, stand-alone liberties to &phi; are compatible with others&rsquo; liberties to interfere with one&rsquo;s &phi;-ing (Hart 1982: 166&ndash;167; Jones 1994: 18&ndash;19). It is not clear, therefore, what stand-alone liberties do for putative right-holders; at least not in legal system in which liberty is the default legal position (that is, unless positively forbidden, &phi;-ing is permitted). Regardless, in practice, legal privileges to &phi; are mostly (even if not necessarily) secured by a measure of legal protection against interference. Firstly, occasionally there are explicit legal claim-rights to &phi; accompanying privileges to &phi;. Secondly, privileges are regularly secured by a &ldquo;protective perimeter&rdquo; of legal prohibitions and claim-rights (other than to &phi;) effectively securing significant aspects of one&rsquo;s freedom to &phi; (Hart 1982: 167 &amp; 171&ndash;173; Steiner 1994: 75&ndash;76; Kramer 1998: 11&ndash;12). For example, even if one does not have a specific legal claim-right to &ldquo;walk on a public street&rdquo;, one&rsquo;s privilege to do so is largely secured by various legal prohibitions on conduct that could interfere with one&rsquo;s enjoying that privilege, such as crimes and torts of assault, privacy, harassment, battery, and false imprisonment. Moreover, normally liberty-rights to &phi; are considered as more than mere &ldquo;lack of duties&rdquo; to &phi;. Liberties are thought of as &ldquo;full-liberties&rdquo;&mdash;that is, liberties to &phi; or to not &phi;&mdash;rather than as &ldquo;half-liberties&rdquo;&mdash;that is, liberties to &phi; without the liberty to not &phi;. Accordingly, &ldquo;mandatory rights&rdquo;, such as children&rsquo;s liberty to attend school or a conscript&rsquo;s liberty to enlist in the army (that is, a lack of duty not to attend or not to enlist) may be labeled &ldquo;half-rights&rdquo; or &ldquo;half-liberties&rdquo;, given that while they involve liberty to attend school or to enlist, they do not provide liberty not to do so (Feinberg 1980: 157, 237; See also Hart 1982: 166&ndash;167). 3.1.3 Power Loosely, a normative power is the ability to alter (through volitional action) the normative landscape; and in law, legal power is the legally recognized ability to change or create legal positions, relations, and norms (e.g., Hohfeld 1919: 50&ndash;57). The opposite of holding a power is normative disability. Correlative to X &rsquo;s power over Y to alter an aspect of Y &rsquo;s normative position is Y &rsquo;s liability to X &rsquo;s respective power. While &ldquo;public powers&rdquo; are vested in the state or persons in their capacity as an agent or officer of the state, &ldquo;private powers&rdquo; are vested in private persons (or corporations) ((Fitzgerald [Salmond] 1966: 229&ndash;230). For example, parliament holds a legal power over the citizenry to enact laws that bind them, and citizens are correlatively liable to that legislative power; prosecutors hold the power to prosecute criminal defendants; and, in private law, owning property typically includes the power to gift or bequeath title to others; estate law empowers people to create wills; contract law provides power to enter into contractual obligations; and agency law empowers to appoint others to take legal actions in one&rsquo;s stead. But are powers indeed legal rights in themselves, as is Hohfeld&rsquo;s view? Arguably not. At least not when divorced of privilege to exercise or to refrain from exercising that power. For instance, under the market overt rule, a thief seemingly holds a legal power to sell stolen goods but it would be odd to describe the thief as holding a legal &ldquo;right&rdquo; to do so (Raz 1972: 82). Similarly, if a judge is under a duty to rule in a certain way on a certain issue, it would be odd to describe her as holding a &ldquo;right&rdquo; to rule in that way, even if she holds the power to do so. In any case, Hohfeld&rsquo;s account of legal power as ability to alter legal relations via volitional conduct (Hohfeld 1919: 50&ndash;51), seems too broad. For example, the commission of a tort or crime entails changes to legal positions (mostly one&rsquo;s own) and typically involve volitional conduct, yet do not appear exercises of legal power (MacCormick 1981 [2008: 98&ndash;100]). Refining Hohfeld&rsquo;s account, some offer narrower conceptions of legal power (Halpin 1996; 142&ndash;147). For instance, emphasizing acting with intent to thereby alter a legal position, as a condition for exercising a power to do so (Fitzgerald [Salmond] 1966: 229; MacCormick 1972: 63; 1981 [2008: 97&ndash;98]). Although, arguably this refinement is also too broad (e.g., homeless persons intentionally committing crimes as a means of exchanging the street for prison; or, a person moving house, thereby incurring obligations not to cause a nuisance to her new neighbors) as well as too narrow (e.g., unintentionally entering a contract). Another approach is that &phi;-ing only counts as an exercise of a legal power if &phi;-ing brings about a certain legal change L and law&rsquo;s reasons for acknowledging &phi;-ing as resulting in L is that people are reasonably expected to &phi; only for the purpose of effecting L (Raz 1972: 80&ndash;81; 1975: [1999: 98&ndash;104]. For example, forming a contract by signing it (for a critical assessment of Raz&rsquo; view see Halpin 1997: 61&ndash;65). 3.1.4 Immunity X holds an immunity against Y in respect of some aspect of X &rsquo;s normative position, if Y is disabled (lacks power) to alter that position. Correlative to X &rsquo;s immunity is Y &rsquo;s respective disability. The Hohfeldian opposite of an immunity is liability (vulnerability to power).

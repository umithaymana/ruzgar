This concerns legal requirements for consent for data processing by third parties, voluntary disclosure of various forms of information on for example social networking services, as well as the choice between alternative technological services that provide different levels of privacy protection. A key question is to what extent individuals are capable of dealing with this responsibility. It has been noted that there is a discrepancy between the attitudes towards privacy and the actual privacy behaviour, the so-called &ldquo;privacy paradox&rdquo; (Kokolakis 2017; Gerber, Gerber &amp; Volkamer 2018). That is, while individuals claim to care about privacy, it seems to play a relatively minor role when making technology choices or disclosure decisions. This could be due to the prevalence of perceived benefits of disclosure, inattention during the actual decision making, or possibly &ldquo;giving up&rdquo; on trying to protect privacy (privacy fatigue / nihilism; Choi, Park &amp; Jung 2018; Hoffmann, Lutz &amp; Ranzini 2016; Van Ooijen, Segijn &amp; Opree 2022). In addition, the design of interfaces may be intentionally crafted to stimulate individuals to reveal as much information as possible, as this is in the interest of the online services. These so-called &ldquo;dark patterns&rdquo; (Waldman 2020) interfere with the ability of individuals to incorporate the value of privacy in their decisions. Dark patterns are a form of &lsquo;hostile cognitive scaffolding&rsquo; where the online environment is designed to serve the interests of others than the user (Timms &amp; Spurrett 2023). If this is indeed the case, the question is whether the choice architecture imposed by the socio-technical systems in place succeeds in enabling individuals to deal with the responsibility assigned to them, and if not, what should be done about this, and who is responsible for creating the appropriate conditions. What seems to be clear is that choice overload or overchoice (Waldman 2020) is not a condition that is helpful in taking responsibility. The question of whether the choice architecture imposed by the socio-technical systems in place enables individuals to deal with the responsibility assigned to them also ties in with the question of whether technology-related privacy is an individual problem in the first place. Many of the harms could be argued to be related to comparison of individuals, rather than learning sensitive data about one particular individual, making privacy a common good (Regan 2015). 4.3 Privacy and other values Within the relation between privacy and information technology, we thus see that (a) our understanding of privacy may be changed by technological developments, and (b) the understanding of privacy matters for the associated allocation of responsibility. Hence, there is a key role for societal dynamics in relation to privacy and technology. But such dynamics are not limited to a single value. Thus, finally, it is appropriate to note that not all social effects of information technology concern privacy (Pieters 2017). Examples include the effects of social network sites on friendship, related to values such as engagement and happiness, and the verifiability of results of electronic elections, related to the value of fairness. Therefore, value-sensitive design approaches and impact assessments of information technology should not focus on privacy alone, since information technology affects many other values as well.

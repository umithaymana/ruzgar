This led to significant improvements in various domains, including natural language processing (NLP), image recognition, and generative systems. Search engines leveraged these new technologies to enhance efficiency, functionality, and user experiences. For example, in 2019, Google began incorporating BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), a novel natural language processing (NLP) model, into its search engine architecture (Nayak 2019 [ Other Internet Resources ]). BERT was designed to improve search results by enabling the search engine to better understand the nuances and context of search queries, ideally leading to more relevant and accurate responses. Microsoft similarly incorporated advanced AI and NLP features into its search products (Baer 2019 [Other Internet Resources]). By 2021, Google released the Multitask Unified Model (MUM), a new AI model 1000-times more powerful than BERT that could handle increasingly complex search queries, understand information across different formats (text, images, video), and utilize knowledge across 75 different languages simulaneously (Nayak 2021 [Other Internet Resources]). Advances in NLP have also increased the usefulness of AI-driven voice assistants like Apple&rsquo;s Siri, Google Assistant, and Amazon&rsquo;s Alexa. Such developements have helped provide an improved understanding of spoken language, dialects and colloquialisms, and also enabling back-and-forth interactions where users can ask follow-up questions and progressively refine their search query (Podesta 2024). Search engines have also increasingly integrated machine learning to enhance image recognition capabilities, leveraging various techniques and advancements to improve the accuracy and efficiency of visual search and image labeling. And since the rapid emergence of user-friendly artificial intelligence systems designed to create new content based on user prompts&mdash;such as ChatGPT or text generation or DALL-E for image generation (Chui et al. 2023 [ Other Internet Resources ])&mdash;search engine providers have been quick to incorporate these new technologies to enhance their capabilities and user experience. Microsoft quickly moved to integrate ChatGPT-like technology from OpenAI into Bing, transforming the search engine into a conversational AI platform that enhances user interaction and information retrieval (Mehdi 2023). Google soon followed with its custom Gemini model to power AI-driven search overviews, providing users with AI-generated overviews to certain queries with the option for users to explore more natively within the search results (Reid 2024). As we noted earlier, many of us now depend on them to direct us to information that affects nearly all facets of our day-to-day lives&mdash;information about work, travel, recreation, entertainment, finances, politics, news, sports, music, education, and so forth. However, as we also noted earlier, the use of search engines has generated a cluster of ethical concerns (Nagenborg 2005). In Section 3, we organize these concerns into five broad categories: (i) search-engine bias and the problem of opacity/non-transparency, (ii) personal privacy and informed consent, (iii) monitoring and surveillance, (iv) censorship and democracy, (v) (cyber)security issues, &ldquo;discoverable search engines&rdquo;, and the Internet of Things. A different, but also related, cluster of ethical issues &mdash; viz, questions concerning moral accountability and social responsibilities for search engine companies &mdash; are examined in Section 4 . 3.1 Search Engine Bias and the Problem of Opacity/Nontransparency What is search-engine bias, and why is it controversial? In reviewing the literature on this topic, it would seem that the phrase &ldquo;search-engine bias&rdquo; has been used to describe at least three distinct, albeit sometimes overlapping, concerns: (1) search-engine technology is not neutral, but instead has embedded features in its design that favor some values over others; (2) major search engines systematically favor some sites (and some kinds of sites) over others in the lists of results they return in response to user search queries; and (3) search algorithms do not use objective criteria in generating their lists of results for search queries. 3.1.1 The Non-Neutrality of Search Engines While some users may assume that search engines are &ldquo;neutral&rdquo; or value-free, critics argue that search engine technology, as well as computer technology in general, is value-laden and thus biased because of the kinds of features typically included in their design. For example, Brey (1998, 2004) and others (see, for instance, Friedman &amp; Nissenbaum 1996) have argued that computer technology has certain built-in features that tend to favor some values over others. Brey worried that some of these technological features have embedded values that are &ldquo;morally opaque&rdquo;. Because the values embedded in these features are not always apparent to the technical experts who develop computer systems, Brey argues that a methodological framework, which expands upon what he calls the &ldquo;standard&rdquo; applied-ethics model typically used in &ldquo;mainstream computer ethics&rdquo;, is needed to identify or disclose the &ldquo;hidden&rdquo; values at the design stage. He refers to this model as &ldquo;disclosive computer ethics&rdquo; (Brey 2004: 55&ndash;56). Identifying the human values embedded in technological design and development has been the main objective of a movement called Value Sensitive Design or VSD , which Friedman, Kahn, and Borning (2008: 70) define as a theoretically grounded approach to the design of technology that accounts for human values in a principled and comprehensive manner throughout the design process. Friedman et al. illustrate their model using the example of Internet cookies&mdash;i.e., small text files that a Web browser places on a user&rsquo;s computer system for the purposes of tracking and recording that user&rsquo;s activities on a Web site. In particular, they examine the design of cookies in connection with the informed-consent process vis-&agrave;-vis Web browsers. They further argue that embedded features in the design of cookies challenge the value of informed consent and that this value is important because it protects other values such as privacy, autonomy, and trust. Cookies technology is not only embedded in the design of contemporary Web browsers, it is also used by major search engine companies to acquire information about users. In so far as these companies place cookies on users&rsquo; computer systems, without first getting their consent, they also seem to contribute to, and perhaps even exacerbate, at least one kind of technology-related bias&mdash;i.e., one that threatens values such as privacy and autonomy, while favoring values associated with surveillance and monitoring.

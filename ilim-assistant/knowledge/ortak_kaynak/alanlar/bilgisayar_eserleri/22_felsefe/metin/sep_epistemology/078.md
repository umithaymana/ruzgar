[ 1 ] The epistemic closure debates concern whether sets of certain beliefs&mdash;primarily those that are known, justified, or warranted&mdash;are closed under relations that, in one way or another, involve valid deductive implication. Most&mdash;but by no means all&mdash;of the discussion has centered on knowledge closure . As we&rsquo;ll characterize it here (see &sect;1.1 below), knowledge closure is the claim that, if you know \(P\) and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s deductively implied by \(P\), then you also know \(Q\). [ 2 ] And how could you not? After all, as you&rsquo;re well aware, \(Q\) has to be true if \(P\) is; and you know that \(P\) is true. So of course you know that \(Q\) is true as well! The intuition expressed by the &ldquo;how could you not?&rdquo; exclamation above is a powerful one, as even closure deniers concede. Some advocates, indeed, view closure as an unassailable epistemological axiom, so that any theory implying closure failure is refuted thereby. [ 3 ] Admittedly, a stance as dismissive of closure denial as this is not as widely endorsed nowadays. But the force of the intuition that closure is obviously true has been, and remains, the most influential consideration in its favor, as a result of which closure&rsquo;s endorsement is widely regarded as the default position. Consequently, most of the arguments for closure are really arguments against closure denial, ones that purport to show that the supposed gains that accrue to such denial can be achieved without renouncing closure. Closure deniers, meanwhile, argue that such denial is the linchpin for the resolution of a variety of epistemological quandaries, and that their stance avoids untenable commitments that are forced on the closure advocate. Closure is central to a wide variety of epistemological issues. These include the nature of knowledge and of justification, skepticism, sensitivity and safety, transmission and transmission failure, fallibilism, contextualism and pragmatic encroachment, bootstrapping and circularity, evidence and probability, and the lottery and preface paradoxes, among others. 1. Defining Closure and Closure Denial 1.1 Defining Closure According to the simplest version of closure&mdash;appearing primarily in studies of epistemic logic&mdash;if you know \(P\), and \(P\) implies \(Q\), then you know \(Q\). If, however, you have no idea that \(P\) does imply \(Q\), you wouldn&rsquo;t be expected to nevertheless inevitably know \(Q\), even if you do know \(P\). What might be called the classic version says that, if you know both \(P\) and that \(P\) implies \(Q\), then you know \(Q\). But it&rsquo;s consistent with the antecedent that you don&rsquo;t even believe \(Q\). And, even if you do, you might not believe it because it follows from \(P\) but instead from, say, wishful thinking. A recent, now-popular alternative forgoes reference to your knowing that \(P\) implies \(Q\): If you know \(P\) and competently deduce \(Q\) from \(P\), thereby coming to believe \(Q\), while retaining your knowledge of \(P\) throughout, then you know \(Q\) (Hawthorne 2004, 34). Call this the dynamic version. The &ldquo;retaining your knowledge throughout&rdquo; clause is due to the fact that this is a diachronic principle: your deduction of \(Q\) from \(P\) takes up an interval of time, at the end of which you come to believe \(Q\). Since you could lose your knowledge of \(P\) during that interval, the additional clause is required. This contrasts with the classic version, which is synchronic: you know \(P\), that \(P\) implies \(Q\), and \(Q\) at the same time. The dynamic version represents the expansion of knowledge by inference, which its advocates view as the core of the intuition behind closure. [ 4 ] There are, however, reasons to prefer a synchronic version. A competent deduction could involve a number of inferential steps or just one. If it involves a number of them, then closure will need to apply to each intermediate step. But then we might as well just limit the principle to one-step inferences. But we shouldn&rsquo;t treat a one-step competent deduction as requiring that you end up believing \(Q\); closure is sometimes appealed to in order to explain retraction phenomena wherein, realizing that \(P\) implies \(Q\), you retract your belief in \(P\) rather than coming to believe \(Q\). But then a one-step competent deduction just seems to involve your recognition, at a time, that \(P\) implies \(Q\) (Alspector-Kelly 2019, 9&ndash;10). It&rsquo;s also consistent with the antecedent of the dynamic version that, at some point during the interval taken up by the competent deduction, you lose your recognition that \(P\) implies \(Q\), and so arrive at your belief in \(Q\) without any grip on its inferential relation to \(P\). You could, then, believe \(Q\) at that point from wishful thinking, and so not know it. So you need to retain throughout, and so into the moment that you believe \(Q\), not only your knowledge of \(P\), but also your recognition that \(Q\) follows from \(P\). But then we might as well just characterize closure as concerning your epistemic state at that moment from the outset. [ 5 ] We&rsquo;ll work with the following synchronic version: SPC (single-premise closure): necessarily, if you know \(P\), and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\), then you know \(Q\). [ 6 ] The phrase &ldquo;because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\)&rdquo; does not mean merely that you know that \(P\) implies \(Q\) as per the classic version. You could know this solely by testimony, for example, without any comprehension of the inferential relation. [ 7 ] Rather, you understand, or &ldquo;see&rdquo;, that \(P\) implies \(Q\), which understanding somehow grounds your inferential dispositions, and which dispositions are manifested by your believing \(Q\). We won&rsquo;t attempt to finesse such understanding further here. [ 8 ] Your believing \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\) is compatible with belief overdetermination: you could believe \(Q\) for other reasons as well. You might, for example, have already believed, before arriving at the department meeting, that the minimum of ten faculty members required for a quorum are present because a colleague, already there, told you as much by phone. Upon arriving and counting twelve faculty members, you then infer that a quorum is indeed present, and believe it for that reason as well. The &ldquo;necessarily&rdquo; operator is needed. If SPC were a material conditional, then it would be true if there just happen not to be cases wherein the antecedent is true and the consequent false, but where that combination is entirely possible. It&rsquo;s also likely stronger than a subjunctive conditional, which still permits possible scenarios in which the antecedent is true and the consequent false (see note 11). But it&rsquo;s not logical necessity: the consequent isn&rsquo;t a logical consequence of the antecedent. So it&rsquo;s intended as either a metaphysical necessity, reflecting the essential characteristics of knowledge, or a conceptual necessity, reflecting the contours of the concept of knowledge. We won&rsquo;t try to determine which here. SPC concerns inferences with only one premise. The multi-premise version runs as follows: MPC: necessarily, if you know each of \(P, R, S,\ldots\), and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P, R, S,\ldots\), then you know \(Q\). Much of the discussion below concerns SPC because what&rsquo;s said of it will apply to MPC as well and, as we&rsquo;ll see in &sect;4 , there are concerns about MPC that, prima facie at least, don&rsquo;t apply to SPC. 1.2 Closure Denial Closure deniers don&rsquo;t deny that sometimes&mdash;indeed, most of the time&mdash;when SPC&rsquo;s antecedent is satisfied, so is its consequent. But they deny that this is an exceptionless generalization: there are, the closure denier insists, cases&mdash;possible ones, if not actual&mdash;wherein the antecedent is satisfied but the consequent not. However, there aren&rsquo;t any closure deniers&mdash;at least, no sane ones&mdash;in the sense of someone who thinks that no closure principle is true. Some feature \(F\) will systematically distinguish cases wherein SPC succeeds from those wherein it fails. So some version of the following closure principle is true: if you know \(P,\) believe \(Q\) because it follows from \(P,\) and \(F\) is realized , then you know \(Q\).

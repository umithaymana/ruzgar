[ 64 ] 4. Multi-Premise Closure The discussion to this point has entirely concerned SPC. This is because there are issues that only affect MPC (or so it seems at first glance). Recall from &sect;2.8 that, on the probabilistic threshold conception, you&rsquo;re justified in believing \(P\) when the probability of \(P\) on your evidence is above some high threshold, and know \(P\) when your belief, in addition to being sufficiently probable, is also true and not a Gettier case. Since the probability of your beliefs given your evidence is rarely 1, that threshold is standardly understood to be less than 1 in order to avoid skepticism. But if the probability of two or more premises is, while above that threshold, less than 1, the probability of their conjunction can drop below that threshold. And that allows the probability of the conclusion to also fall below that threshold, since its probability need only be at least as high as the probability of that conjunction. So MPC can fail on the threshold view. This doesn&rsquo;t affect SPC, since if \(P\) implies \(Q\) then \(p(Q) \ge p(P)\). A classic illustration is the lottery paradox (Kyburg 1961). It&rsquo;s highly probable, of each ticket in a typical lottery, that it will lose. Suppose that&mdash;like many real lotteries&mdash;although it&rsquo;s probable that there&rsquo;s a winning ticket in a particular lottery, it&rsquo;s not guaranteed; and, as it turns out, there isn&rsquo;t a winner this time around. If you believe, of each ticket, that it will lose, those beliefs will all be true. And they don&rsquo;t exhibit any of the characteristics of Gettier cases. So, on the threshold view, you know, of each ticket, that it will lose. Those propositions together imply that all the tickets will lose (equivalently, that the conjunction of the many &ldquo;ticket x will lose&rdquo; propositions is true). [ 65 ] But you don&rsquo;t know the latter, since it&rsquo;s improbable. MPC fails. One can deny, as many do, that you know, of each losing ticket, that that ticket will lose, however probable its losing might be. Perhaps, for example, knowing \(P\) requires, not only that \(P\) is probable on your evidence, but also that your belief in \(P\) is safe, which, as per &sect;2.8 above, these beliefs are not. But suppose that you do in fact know every one of the thousands of individual claims you&rsquo;ve made in your book, each of which is, then, both safe and probable on your evidence. Those claims together imply their conjunction. However, as you modestly concede, it&rsquo;s likely that at least one claim in the book is wrong. That conjunction is therefore improbable. On the view we&rsquo;re considering, then, you don&rsquo;t know it, and MPC fails. [ 66 ] Preserving MPC in this case requires, not that high probability is not sufficient for knowledge, but that it isn&rsquo;t necessary: your belief can count as known even if its probability on your evidence is very low. Even those who don&rsquo;t endorse high probability as sufficient for knowledge might balk at the idea that you can know \(P\) despite \(P\)&rsquo;s being highly improbable on your evidence (but see Williamson 2009a and 2014, and Smith 2016a and 2025). A compromise is to endorse SPC and reject MPC. But one problem with doing so is that the intuitions supporting SPC seem to equally support MPC. A purported cost of closure denial discussed in &sect;2.5 above is that closure deniers are forced to reject highly intuitive closure principles like addition closure, simplification closure, or equivalence closure. But many simple multi-premise closure principles&mdash;employing conjunction introduction, modus ponens, modus tollens, disjunctive syllogism, etc.&mdash;are equally highly intuitive, and are all susceptible to the risk-accrual problem. Moreover, &ldquo;I know \(P\) and I know \(Q\), but I don&rsquo;t know \(P \amp Q\)&rdquo; (for example) seems as abominable as any single-premise version. Another concern with the compromise is that our inferential dispositions aren&rsquo;t infallible: you could believe \(Q\) because, you think, it follows from \(P\) when, in fact, it doesn&rsquo;t. So the risk that the conclusion could be false is a function, not only of the risk that the premises are false, but also that the inference is faulty. These can combine, so that the risk that the conclusion is false is too high for it to count as known (Lasonen 2008). This can happen even if there is no risk that the premise is false. Suppose that you perform an extremely long sequence of single-premise deductions from a certain mathematical truth. For each step there is a small chance that you&rsquo;ve reasoned incorrectly. Those chances can accumulate so that your putative knowledge of the final conclusion is defeated by that accumulated risk (Lasonen 2008, Schechter 2013, and Brown 2018, chs. 5 &amp; 6; but see Tang 2018 and Montessori 2025). [ 67 ] 5. Justification Closure The discussion to this point has only concerned knowledge closure. But there is also a robust debate concerning whether justification is closed. Consider the following principle: SPJC: If you are justified in believing \(P\), and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\), then you are justified in believing \(Q\). Assuming that justified false beliefs are possible, neither sensitivity nor safety (nor basis-relative versions of them) can be justification, since both are factive. [ 68 ] But much of the discussion from &sect;&sect;3&ndash;4 above applies also to justification. If SPJC is true, is that because transmission of justification succeeds? If so, then the following is also correct: SPJT: if (i) you are justified in believing \(P\), and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\), then (ii) you are justified in believing \(Q\) because of (i). It is, however, as unintuitive that SPJT is true in Dretske cases as it is that SPT is true in those cases. If, instead, SPJT is false in such cases but SPJC is preserved, then much the same questions can be posed concerning the source of justification as were asked about the source of warrant: does your justification for \(P\) suffice on its own as a justification for \(Q\)? Or is justification for \(Q\) a precondition for your justification for \(P\)? If the latter, is the requisite justification for \(Q\) provided by background knowledge? Or is \(Q\) justified by default? In fact, a significant proportion of the literature on these issues expressly concerns either justification or, confusingly, &ldquo;warrant&rdquo;, but where the latter term typically means, not ways of knowing, but either justification or something similar to it (see note 48). The debate concerning knowledge and that concerning justification/warrant are not always carefully distinguished. This is unfortunate, since a view ensuring that justification is closed doesn&rsquo;t thereby ensure that knowledge is closed as well. It is, for example, more plausible that purely statistical information concerning the likelihood of losing the lottery delivers justification for &ldquo;I will lose&rdquo; than that it delivers knowledge. It&rsquo;s also at least somewhat more plausible that non-evidential entitlements can justify cornerstone propositions than that such entitlements deliver knowledge of them. If the justification for your belief is a function of its probability on your evidence, then you would be justified in believing, for each ticket in a lottery, that it will lose&mdash;including the one that will win if there is one, assuming justification isn&rsquo;t factive&mdash;but not that they all will. So, for the same reasons canvassed in &sect;4 , SPJC will succeed but the multi-premise version, MPJC, will fail. And the arguments extending those concerns to SPC still apply, thereby threatening SPJC as well. Other conceptions of justification are not similarly threatened. If justification is knowledge then, if you are justified in believing the premises, you know them. And if \(E = K\), the probability of each premise on your evidence&mdash;on, that is, that very premise&mdash;is 1. And since the premises together imply the conclusion, the probability of the conclusion is also 1. There are other non-probabilistic accounts of justification that preserve both SPJC and MPJC without the radical identification of justification and knowledge. For example, on Martin Smith&rsquo;s normic account, you have a justification for believing \(P\) when \(P\) is normically supported by your evidence (Smith 2010 and 2016a). &ldquo;A body of evidence \(E\) normically supports a proposition \(P\) just in case the circumstance in which \(E\) is true and \(P\) is false requires more explanation than the circumstance in which \(E\) and \(P\) are both true&rdquo; (Smith 2016a, 40; see Goodman and Salow 2023 for a similar view). Although improbable, there&rsquo;s nothing abnormal about your winning the lottery given your statistical evidence. So that evidence doesn&rsquo;t justify your believing that you will lose the lottery (call this case \(A)\).

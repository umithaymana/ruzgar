It&rsquo;s odd that a defense of closure appeals to a condition&mdash;safety&mdash;that makes that inference superfluous. 2.8 Lottery Cases There are also Dretske cases wherein it&rsquo;s doubtful that the nearest not-\(Q\) world is distant. The flagship example is Hawthorne 2004&rsquo;s lottery case. In light of your meager bank balance, you know that you can&rsquo;t afford to go on an African safari next week. That implies that you won&rsquo;t win the lottery this week and so be able to afford such a trip despite that meager balance. But it&rsquo;s widely agreed that you don&rsquo;t know that you&rsquo;ll lose the lottery before the draw, despite its being extremely likely that you will. That agreement puts pressure on probabilist threshold theories of justification and knowledge according to which you are justified in believing \(P\) when \(P\) is sufficiently probable on your evidence&mdash;as is, presumably, your belief that you will lose the lottery given the astronomical odds in its favor&mdash;and know \(P\) when your belief, in addition to being sufficiently probable, is also true and not a Gettier case, both of which are satisfied by your belief that you will lose the lottery. But it doesn&rsquo;t put the same pressure on modal theories, including safety theory. Although improbable, the nearest world in which you win the lottery is very nearby: just a slightly different arrangement of the balls that drop from the cage will do the trick. So the safety theorist can explain that wide agreement: you don&rsquo;t know that you&rsquo;ll lose because you&rsquo;ll still believe that you&rsquo;ll lose in the nearby world in which you win. [ 34 ] But, as per &sect;2.7 above, if you win the lottery in a nearby world, then &ldquo;you can&rsquo;t afford an African safari&rdquo; is unsafe. So you don&rsquo;t know that either, despite intuition to the contrary. The safety theorist is a skeptic to at least this extent: if you have a lottery ticket, then you don&rsquo;t know any proposition that would be false if you&rsquo;ll soon have a lot of money. Perhaps that&rsquo;s an amount of skepticism we can live with. However, there are any number of analogous &ldquo;lottery-esque&rdquo; propositions that make trouble for a much wider swath of ordinary knowledge (Vogel 1990a, Hawthorne 2004). For example: you remember parking your car in parking spot 3b half an hour ago, where it remains. You believe&mdash;and, presumably, know&mdash;that it&rsquo;s there now. This implies that it wasn&rsquo;t stolen in the last half-hour. But you don&rsquo;t seem to know that it wasn&rsquo;t. And you know that the Broncos won by reading that in the newspaper. Their winning implies that that report isn&rsquo;t an erroneous misprint. But you don&rsquo;t seem to know that it isn&rsquo;t. In response, the safety theorist will insist that, for example, if you do know that the car is in 3b, the nearest world in which it is stolen will be distant&mdash;the thief detests that model, say&mdash;and so, by the safety account, you do know that it isn&rsquo;t stolen. This contrasts with the eponymous lottery case wherein the nearest world in which you win is nearby. Were, however, the nearest world in which it&rsquo;s stolen also nearby&mdash;the thief covets that model&mdash;then you indeed don&rsquo;t know that it&rsquo;s in 3b (Pritchard 2007a). But our being informed that the nearest \(\neg Q\) world is distant in such cases doesn&rsquo;t seem to shift our intuition that you don&rsquo;t know \(Q\). (Note that you&rsquo;re not so informed.) In the lottery case, suppose that a ball with one of your ticket&rsquo;s numbers on it&mdash;and so one that would have to drop in order for you to win&mdash;got stuck in the cage&rsquo;s wire frame, so that only if the cage were forcefully shaken would that ball drop. However, since the cage is firmly affixed to the floor, that&rsquo;s not something that would easily happen. The nearest world in which you win is now distant. But, intuitively, you&mdash;knowing nothing about the ball&rsquo;s being stuck&mdash;still don&rsquo;t know that you&rsquo;ll lose. Instead, that intuition seems to hinge on the fact that your only available evidence in lottery cases is statistical: your case (ticket, parked car, report) is one among many, you have reason to believe that it is, or is likely to be, false in a small number of those cases (a ticket will win, a car will probably be stolen, some report will likely contain a misprint), and you have no reason to think that your case is exceptional (Vogel 1990). Such evidence seems not to deliver knowledge, however distant the nearest \(\neg Q\) world might be. Recall also that, on the safety view, \(Q\) propositions in Dretske cases must be vacuously safe if you know the corresponding \(P\) proposition. So your basis for believing \(Q\) shouldn&rsquo;t matter. Since most of our sources of information misfire on occasion, similar cases involving lottery-esque propositions can be constructed for most of our everyday knowledge. The resulting skepticism is very widespread. Such cases are not only a challenge for the safety theorist; they confront any non-skeptical closure-affirming view. Some closure advocates argue that, notwithstanding intuition, you do know that you will lose the lottery, and similarly for other lottery-esque propositions (Cohen 1988, Engel 2021). And some, like the safety theorist, claim that there are crucial differences between the eponymous lottery proposition, which you don&rsquo;t know, and lottery-esque propositions, which, it&rsquo;s claimed, you do know (Nelkin 2000, Smith 2010, 2016a, and 2021). 2.9 Closure Failures for Safety Although the safety account preserves closure in Dretske cases, there are other cases that seem to constitute closure failures on that account. You believe that your Lamborghini is in parking spot 3b because you&rsquo;re obsessively watching it on a closed-circuit TV monitor, and infer from this that it hasn&rsquo;t been stolen. Your belief that it&rsquo;s in spot 3b is safe: since you&rsquo;re watching it on the monitor, there&rsquo;s no nearby world in which you believe that it&rsquo;s in that spot when it isn&rsquo;t. However, there&rsquo;s a nearby world in which your friend asks to borrow the car. You agree, and complacently watch him drive off on the monitor. You still believe that your car wasn&rsquo;t stolen. But it was; your friend is a thief. So your belief that your car is in 3b is safe but your belief that it hasn&rsquo;t been stolen isn&rsquo;t. Closure fails. In response, the safety theorist might claim that knowledge requires, not that your belief is safe, but that your basis is: there&rsquo;s no nearby world in which you believe it on that basis and it&rsquo;s false (Luper 1984, Sosa 1999, Pritchard 2007b). They should do this anyway, since Nozick&rsquo;s grandmother case can be adjusted to count against safety too: just add that, although the grandson is actually well, there&rsquo;s a nearby world in which he&rsquo;s sick. [ 35 ] In the car example above, you believe that your car isn&rsquo;t stolen, not by inferring this from its being in 3b, but because you thought your friend wouldn&rsquo;t steal it. Any nearby world in which you believe it&rsquo;s in 3b by seeing it on the monitor and infer that it&rsquo;s not stolen is a world in which it is in 3b and not stolen; both beliefs are basis-safe. However, suppose there&rsquo;s a nearby world in which the monitor fails. You call your friend, who assures you, while joyriding in your car, that it&rsquo;s still in 3b. You infer from this that it&rsquo;s not stolen; but it is. Your belief that it&rsquo;s in 3b is basis-safe, since there&rsquo;s no nearby world in which the monitor shows that it&rsquo;s there when it isn&rsquo;t. But your belief that it&rsquo;s not stolen, although still inferred from its being in 3b, is not. And closure fails again (Alspector-Kelly 2011; see also Murphy 2005 and Zhao 2022). At this point the safety theorist might claim that, when you infer \(Q\) from \(P\), your basis for believing \(Q\) includes, not only that you inferred it from \(P\), but also whatever your basis was for your belief in \(P\) (Williamson 2009a, 2009b). In the last case above, you actually believe that it&rsquo;s in 3b because you see it on the monitor, whereas in the nearby world you believe this because your friend says so. So, if that claim is right, your basis for believing that it&rsquo;s not stolen in the actual world is not what it is in the nearby world after all. That belief is basis-safe, and closure is preserved once again. Whether this is a plausible and viable way to individuate bases is controversial (see Goldstein and Hawthorne 2024). 3. Closure, Transmission, and Sources of Warrant There is a crucial distinction between closure and transmission , one that has come to play an increasingly important role in the closure debate. [ 36 ] The consequent of SPC doesn&rsquo;t say anything about how you come by your knowledge of Q; it just says that you know it. So it&rsquo;s compatible with SPC that, although you infer \(Q\) from \(P\) that you know, you nevertheless can&rsquo;t learn \(Q\) because you so infer; however, in such cases, you inevitably know it by other means. If so, then, although closure is preserved, the following principle is violated: SPT (Single Premise Transmission): if (i) you know \(P\), and believe \(Q\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\), then (ii) you know \(Q\) because of (i). Your knowing \(Q\) because of (i) is compatible with knowledge overdetermination: you could know \(Q\) in other ways as well. You might, for example, have already known, before arriving at the department meeting, that the minimum of ten faculty members required for a quorum are present because a colleague, already there, told you as much by phone.

[ 21 ] 2.4 Abominable Conjunctions Another objection lodged against sensitivity accounts, and one that applies to any closure-denying view, is that closure denial seems to license assertions that are intuitively infelicitous (or &ldquo;abominable&rdquo;), such as &ldquo;I know that I have hands but I don&rsquo;t know that I&rsquo;m not a (handless) \(BIV\)&rdquo;. [ 22 ] According to the &ldquo;knowledge rule&rdquo; of assertion&mdash;that I should only assert what I know&mdash;I can assert \(H\) but not \(\neg BIV\) (Williamson 2000, ch. 11). But it would be bizarre to complacently assert the former and yet steadfastly refuse to assert the latter (Hawthorne 2013). Our commitment to closure, it is claimed, provides the best explanation for these intuitions. An alternative explanation is, however, available to the closure denier. I initially take myself to know \(H\) and then realize&mdash;not that I can&rsquo;t know \(H\) if I don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\), as per closure&mdash;but that I can&rsquo;t know \(H\) if I am a \(BIV\). But I can&rsquo;t affirm &ldquo;\(\neg BIV\) but I don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\)&rdquo;; that&rsquo;s infelicitous. [ 23 ] So I can only either take myself to know \(\neg BIV\), refuse to express an opinion on the matter, or retract my claim to know \(H\). But I&rsquo;m convinced that I don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\), and it would be bizarre to refuse to express an opinion when I am fully aware that I must not be a \(BIV\) if, as I claim, I know \(H\). So I retract my claim to know \(H\). That doesn&rsquo;t mean that I don&rsquo;t know \(H\). I can felicitously assert of someone else that they have hands, and so aren&rsquo;t a \(BIV\), but don&rsquo;t know that they&rsquo;re not; I just can&rsquo;t felicitously assert this of myself. I might nevertheless have hands, and so not be a \(BIV\), despite not knowing the latter. Correspondingly, I can coherently assert of someone else that they know that they have hands, and so aren&rsquo;t a \(BIV\), but don&rsquo;t know that they&rsquo;re not. I just can&rsquo;t felicitously assert this of myself either, and for the same reason. I might nevertheless know that I have hands, and so not be a \(BIV\), but not know the latter. It might be objected that third-person conjunctions are also infelicitous. But, when the relevant cases are carefully described, the infelicity dissipates. My wife and I note that, according to the posted schedule, the train we&rsquo;re taking leaves in a half-hour. My wife goes to get coffee. While she&rsquo;s gone it occurs to me that the schedule might have recently changed; I ask the ticket agent, who assures me that it hasn&rsquo;t. If queried by someone whether my wife knows when the train leaves&mdash;they&rsquo;re worried she won&rsquo;t get back in time&mdash;I would assure them that she does. But I wouldn&rsquo;t claim that she knows that the schedule hasn&rsquo;t changed; I investigated that, not her. &ldquo;Although she doesn&rsquo;t know it, the schedule hasn&rsquo;t changed; so she knows that the train leaves in a half-hour&rdquo; doesn&rsquo;t seem particularly infelicitous. My knowing that the schedule hasn&rsquo;t changed makes all the difference (Alspector-Kelly 2019, &sect;10.7). 2.5 The Spreading Problem Here are three closure principles that are implied by SPC: Addition Closure (AC): if \(S\) knows \(P\), and believes \((P \vee Q)\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \(P\), then \(S\) knows \((P \vee Q)\). Simplification Closure (SC): if \(S\) knows \((P \amp Q)\), and believes \(P\) because it&rsquo;s implied by \((P \amp Q)\), then \(S\) knows \(P\). Equivalence Closure (EC): If \(S\) knows \(P, P\) is logically equivalent to \(Q\), and \(S\) believes \(Q\) because it is implied by \(P\), then \(S\) knows \(Q\). It is widely claimed that renouncing these closure principles is extremely unintuitive (Hawthorne 2013, Holliday 2015, McGlynn 2014a). But we can get to \(\neg BIV\) from \(H\) by a combination of either AC and EC or SC and EC. Here&rsquo;s the first way: \(H\) So, \(H \lor \neg BIV\) 1, AC So, \(\neg BIV\) 2, EC [ 24 ] And here&rsquo;s the second: \(H\) So, \(H \amp \neg BIV\) 1, EC [ 25 ] So, \(\neg BIV\) 2, SC So both Nozick and Dretske must either deny both SC and AC or deny EC on pain of allowing knowledge of \(\neg BIV\) by inference from \(H\). If a belief is basis-sensitive then so is any belief logically equivalent to it. [ 26 ] So both Nozick and Dretske have to, and did, affirm EC. But Nozick, while denying SC, affirmed AC (Nozick 1981, 228), and so is susceptible to the first way above; and Dretske affirmed both AC and SC, and so is susceptible to both ways (Dretske 1970, 1009). Since both claim that closure fails from \(H\) to \(\neg BIV\), both accounts are, as written, inconsistent. Any closure denier who repudiates the inference from \(H\) to \(\neg BIV\) will also have to either deny both SC and AC or deny EC, despite the fact that they are all very intuitive principles. In response the closure denier might point out that it is also very intuitive that one can&rsquo;t learn \(\neg BIV\) by inference from \(H\) by any inferential route. [ 27 ] There is a host of problems with basis sensitivity that have little to do with closure; the ice-cube case ( &sect;2.2 ) is one such, but there are many others. [ 28 ] And there are a number of more recent sensitivity accounts that either purport to resolve at least some of these problems, or affirm closure, or both (Adams and Clarke 2005, Becker 2012, Baumann 2012, Black 2008, Murphy and Black 2012, DeRose 1995 and 2010, and Roush 2005). But by far the most popular approach renounces sensitivity entirely in favor of an alternative subjunctive conditional, safety. 2.6 Safety A belief \(P\) is safe if, were you to believe \(P, P\) would be true. [ 29 ] This is the contrapositive of sensitivity. But it&rsquo;s not equivalent to sensitivity because, unlike material conditionals, the contraposition of a subjunctive conditional isn&rsquo;t equivalent to the original conditional. The safety conditional is standardly rendered in possible worlds format as requiring that, in nearby worlds in which you believe \(P\), \(P\) is true. [ 30 ] An oft-advertised advantage of safety over sensitivity is that it preserves closure (Sosa 1999; Luper 1984, 2006; Pritchard 2009a; Williamson 2000). Consider the SCA. There may well be nearby worlds in which \(\neg H\); a hand-maiming car accident, after all, can be caused by the briefest ill-timed glance at your phone. But, in such worlds, you would be well aware that \(\neg H\). So your belief in \(H\) is safe. And your belief in \(\neg BIV\) is safe as well, although for a very different reason: our technological capacities being what they currently are, there&rsquo;s no nearby world in which \(BIV\). For that reason alone, there&rsquo;s no nearby world in which \(BIV\) and you believe \(\neg BIV\). So that belief is also safe. So, contrary to both the skeptic and the sensitivity theorist&mdash;and assuming that you meet whatever other conditions knowledge requires, if any&mdash;you do know \(\neg BIV\), and closure is preserved. [ 31 ] 2.7 Vacuous Safety In the \(BIV\) case your belief in \(H\) is safe due to the basis that you have for it: in nearby worlds in which it&rsquo;s false you don&rsquo;t have the perceptual experiences you actually have that lead you to believe it. However, your belief in \(\neg BIV\) is only safe because the nearest world in which it&rsquo;s false is too distant to matter; it&rsquo;s vacuously safe. In general, skeptical hypotheses imply that you have (or, at least, seem to have) the same basis for the ordinary proposition that you actually have. So you will believe the ordinary proposition on that basis in the nearest world in which the skeptical hypothesis is true (since you actually do so), and so in which the ordinary proposition is false. If the nearest such world is nearby, then you&rsquo;ll believe the ordinary proposition in a nearby world in which it&rsquo;s false; that belief will be unsafe. So, in order for it to be safe, the skeptical hypothesis must be false in nearby worlds. Your belief that it is false is, therefore, vacuously safe. A vacuously safe belief is safe no matter what your basis for it might be. So, if you believe it because your astrologer says so, your belief will still be safe. But it&rsquo;s hard to swallow the claim that you know it on that basis. [ 32 ] The safety theorist can suggest in response that safety is a necessary but not sufficient condition for knowledge.

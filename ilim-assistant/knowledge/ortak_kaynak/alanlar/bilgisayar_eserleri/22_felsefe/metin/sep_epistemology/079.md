[ 9 ] 2. Sensitivity, Safety, and Skepticism The knowledge closure debate was initially triggered, and subsequently dominated, by two closure-denying theories of knowledge: Robert Nozick&rsquo;s tracking theory and Fred Dretske&rsquo;s conclusive reasons account. 2.1 Nozick Nozick (1981, ch. 3) initially proposed that, for you to know \(P\), if \(P\) were false then you wouldn&rsquo;t believe it. This subjunctive (or counterfactual or modal) conditional has come to be called the &ldquo;sensitivity&rdquo; condition. [ 10 ] Sensitivity is standardly interpreted in possible-worlds format as requiring that, in the nearest world(s) to the actual world in which \(P\) is false, you don&rsquo;t believe \(P\). [ 11 ] The view implies closure failure. But, far from regarding this as a disadvantage, Nozick saw it as the key to countering the Cartesian Skeptical Argument (CSA). [ 12 ] Consider the following version of the CSA, where &ldquo;\(H\)&rdquo; is &ldquo;you have hands&rdquo; and &ldquo;\(BIV\)&rdquo; is &ldquo;you&rsquo;re a handless brain-in-a-vat&rdquo;&mdash;your brain resides in a vat where it is electrically stimulated to have precisely the same sensory experiences that you now have: CSA1. You don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\). CSA2. If you don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\), then you don&rsquo;t know \(H\). CSA3. So you don&rsquo;t know \(H\). Since an argument with the same structure could be run for almost any claim about the external world by substituting that claim for \(H\), the ultimate result is skepticism of the external world. [ 13 ] If you didn&rsquo;t have hands, this wouldn&rsquo;t be because you&rsquo;re a \(BIV\)&mdash;a remote possibility beyond our current technological capacities&mdash;but because you lost them in, for example, a car accident. But then you would be well aware that you don&rsquo;t have hands, and so wouldn&rsquo;t believe that you do. So \(H\) is sensitive. Assuming that you also meet whatever other conditions knowledge requires, if any, then you know \(H\). However, if you were a \(BIV\) then, since everything would seem to you precisely as it now does, you would still believe that you&rsquo;re not a \(BIV\); \(\neg BIV\) is insensitive. So you don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\). So CSA2 is false. CSA2 is the contrapositive of &ldquo;if you know \(H\), then you know \(\neg BIV\)&rdquo;, which is an instance of the closure principle that, if you know \(P\) (and \(P\) implies \(Q)\), then you know \(Q\). So that principle is false. [ 14 ] Here&rsquo;s a counterexample to the sensitivity condition that Nozick considered (Nozick 1981, 179). A grandmother is visited by her grandson, who appears well. She presumably knows, in virtue of his so appearing, that he is well. But if he were ill, other family members would prevent her from seeing him and would tell her that he is well, so as to avoid distressing her. So she would still believe that he is well. So her belief is insensitive. But she seems, nonetheless, to know that he is well. Nozick&rsquo;s response was to appeal to methods: if \(P\) were false then you would not believe \(P\) by the same method you actually used to arrive at belief in \(P\). Grandma&rsquo;s actual method was visual inspection of her grandson and, if he were ill and so looked it, she wouldn&rsquo;t believe he was well. So her belief, although insensitive, is method-sensitive. Knowledge, Nozick suggested, requires, not sensitivity, but method-sensitivity. 2.2 Dretske On Dretske 1971&rsquo;s conclusive-reasons view, knowledge requires that you wouldn&rsquo;t have the reason you have for your belief if that belief were false. [ 15 ] In other words, Dretske requires, not that your belief is sensitive, but that your reason is. In the grandmother case, if the grandson were sick she wouldn&rsquo;t have the same reason&mdash;that he looks well&mdash;that she actually has, and so his looking that way counts as a conclusive reason. [ 16 ] Dretske&rsquo;s conclusive-reasons account and Nozick&rsquo;s method-relative account arrive at the same judgments about who knows what in, at least, the cases we will be considering. [ 17 ] So we&rsquo;ll treat them as the same view for our purposes here, calling it the &ldquo;basis-sensitivity&rdquo; account, where &ldquo;basis&rdquo; is neutral between methods of belief formation and reasons for belief. In support of his view Dretske offered the now-famous zebra case (Dretske 1970). You&rsquo;re at the zoo, looking into the zebra paddock at a zebra. Since it looks like a zebra \((LZ)\), you believe, and presumably know, that it is a zebra \((Z). Z\) is basis-sensitive: if it weren&rsquo;t a zebra it would be, say, a rhino, and so wouldn&rsquo;t look like a zebra. Since zebras aren&rsquo;t mules, \(Z\) implies that it&rsquo;s not a mule disguised to look just like a zebra \((\neg DM)\). You infer \(\neg DM\) from \(Z. \neg DM\) is not basis-sensitive: \(DM\) implies \(LZ\), so you&rsquo;d still believe \(Z\) and infer \(\neg DM\) from it. It&rsquo;s obvious, Dretske insists, that you can&rsquo;t learn \(\neg DM\) by so inferring. But you also don&rsquo;t know \(\neg DM\) by its appearance, which it would have if \(DM\). Nor have you washed the animal, conducted a DNA test, or done anything else that would indicate \(\neg DM\). So you know \(Z\) but not \(\neg DM\). Closure fails. Skeptical hypotheses like &ldquo;I&rsquo;m a \(BIV\)&rdquo;, &ldquo;I&rsquo;m dreaming&rdquo;, and the like are wholesale : they are one-size-fits-all hypotheses designed to undermine virtually all external-world knowledge claims. \(DM\) is, instead, a piecemeal skeptical hypothesis, targeting a specific ordinary knowledge claim, with the implied suggestion that similar piecemeal skeptical hypotheses can be wielded against most if not all other external-world knowledge claims. [ 18 ] Skeptical hypotheses of both varieties imply that your grounds for believing the targeted ordinary claims&mdash;you seem to have hands, it looks like a zebra&mdash;remain (or, at least, seem to do so). Call such ordinary proposition/skeptical hypothesis pairs&mdash;where the former implies the denial of the latter&mdash; Dretske cases . Dretske (1970) saw the zebra case as an instance of what he called &ldquo;penetration failure&rdquo;: your basis, \(LZ\), for believing \(Z\) doesn&rsquo;t constitute a basis for \(\neg DM\). He thought that such penetration failures provide an argument for closure failure: if the way in which you come to know the premise isn&rsquo;t inevitably available as a way to come to know the conclusion, then why think that you must end up knowing the conclusion anyway? However, your basis for the conclusion is that it follows from the premise, which inevitably isn&rsquo;t your basis for the premise. Closure doesn&rsquo;t require that the way you know the premise is inevitably a way to know the conclusion. It only requires that, if you do know the premise, and infer the conclusion from it, then you know the conclusion (Klein 1995, Luper 2006; see also &sect;3.1 below). Dretske (2013) also claimed that closure fails when \(P\) is &ldquo;lightweight&rdquo; (an ordinary claim) and \(Q\) is &ldquo;heavyweight&rdquo; (a much-disputed philosophical claim): you can&rsquo;t learn &ldquo;there are physical objects&rdquo; by inference from &ldquo;there are cookies in the jar&rdquo;, &ldquo;the past is real&rdquo; from &ldquo;I finished breakfast&rdquo;, and so on, despite the fact that the latter implies the former. However, at least some heavyweight claims are basis-sensitive: if there weren&rsquo;t any physical objects in the jar, then there wouldn&rsquo;t be any cookies in there, and so it wouldn&rsquo;t look to you as though there were (Hawthorne 2013). And at least some lightweight propositions are not basis-sensitive. You know that the ice cubes in the glass of lemonade that you left on the patio for four hours in 95&deg; heat have melted. But if they hadn&rsquo;t&mdash;someone put the glass in the fridge without your knowing&mdash;you would still remember having put it outside and so believe that they&rsquo;ve melted (Vogel 1987). Others have also argued that closure fails for lightweight-to-heavyweight inferences. Yablo (2014, 2017), for example, suggests that the heavyweight claims introduce subject-matter&mdash;whether there is a physical world, you&rsquo;re a \(BIV\), and so on&mdash;that aren&rsquo;t included in the lightweight claims, and that closure only succeeds from \(P\) to \(Q\) when \(Q\) doesn&rsquo;t introduce subject-matter not included in \(P\). And Schaffer (2004, 2007) suggests that knowledge ascriptions are implicitly contrastive: you know that those are cookies rather than pickles, but not that they&rsquo;re cookies rather than cookie-hallucinations. So knowing that they are cookies in the first sense doesn&rsquo;t require knowing that they&rsquo;re not hallucinations. Such views introduce restrictions on SPC that, their advocates hope, block lightweight-to-heavyweight inferences without blocking intuitively acceptable inferences. [ 19 ] 2.3 Red Barn At first glance, basis-sensitivity helps to resolve a well-known counterexample to sensitivity (Kripke 2011). In red-barn country there are both real barns and fake barns that look real; but only real ones are painted red. Knowing none of this, you see a red barn, believe that it&rsquo;s a red barn, and infer that it&rsquo;s a barn. &ldquo;It&rsquo;s a red barn&rdquo; is sensitive: if it weren&rsquo;t a red barn it wouldn&rsquo;t look like one, so you wouldn&rsquo;t believe that it is. But &ldquo;it&rsquo;s a barn&rdquo; isn&rsquo;t sensitive: if it weren&rsquo;t a barn&mdash;and so were a non-red fake barn&mdash;it would still look like a barn.

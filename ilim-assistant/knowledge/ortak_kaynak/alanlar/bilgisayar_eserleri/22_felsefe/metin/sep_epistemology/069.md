Some contextualists again&mdash;notably Lewis (1996) and Greco (2003, 2010)&mdash;have assayed extending EC to the Gettier problem. According to them, EC explains why certain cases of justified true belief are not correctly said to be &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;, as Gettier showed. (On the Gettier problem, see Ichikawa and Steup 2026, Section 3). But this remains a much more controversial move among proponents of EC. (Cohen 1998 and Heller 1999b, e.g., criticize Lewis on this score.) Further, there is some disagreement among contextualists as to the status of the closure principle for knowledge, mentioned in Section 3.1 above. While Mark Heller dissents (see note #7), the majority view is that closure should be preserved, but also that it should be seen as holding only within a given context, on pain of equivocation. As such disagreement illustrates, what one makes of closure and of EC are orthogonal issues. Each of EC, closure, and RA, then, may be endorsed/rejected without taking a stand on either of the remaining issues. Finally, while the differences among particular versions of EC can be significant, they tend to recede into the background in critical discussions of the view. Objections to EC (a) deny that EC really has the advantages that have been claimed for it, (b) assert that EC has certain problematic features or consequences, and/or (c) allege that EC does not in fact constitute the best response to the data which are supposed to motivate it. And, to a very great extent, such objections to EC are directed towards the central contextualist thesis per se and so are independent of the details of any particular contextualist theory. 4. Critical Reactions, Contextualist Responses 4.1 Some Initial Objections Among objections to EC which have been made, some are more easily dealt with than others. For instance, Fred Dretske objects to the contextualist&rsquo;s response to skeptical problems as follows: Skepticism, as a doctrine about what ordinary people know, cannot be made true by being put in the mouth of a skeptic. Treating knowledge as an indexical&hellip;[, however,] seems to have, or to come dangerously close to having, exactly this result. For this reason (among others) I reject it. (Dretske 1991, 192) To this, however, the contextualist can reply that it is incorrect to say that EC, even where it allows the skeptic to successfully state some truth in uttering &lsquo;You don&rsquo;t know that p &rsquo;, thereby makes it easy for skepticism to be true. To suppose that it does requires denying the EC view that &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences have context-sensitive contents; it assumes that the skeptic&rsquo;s denials are true at the expense of the truth of our ordinary claims to &ldquo;know&rdquo;. (As we will see presently, there is another way of construing Dretske&rsquo;s objection, whereby it does not misfire in this way.) For a second objection to EC that is not terribly effective, consider Palle Yourgrau&rsquo;s contention that EC licenses bizarre dialogues, such as the following: A: Is that a zebra? B: Yes, it is a zebra. A: But can you rule out its being merely a cleverly painted mule? B: No, I cannot. A: So, you admit you didn&rsquo;t know it was a zebra? B: No, I did know then that it was a zebra. But after your question, I no longer know. (1983, 183) At least some versions of EC do not license such dialogues, however. According to them, the last reply by B is incorrect. Once the disguised zebra possibility is mentioned by A, B&rsquo;s &ldquo;knowing&rdquo; that it is a zebra requires B&rsquo;s being able to rule out that possibility and B cannot do that. According to these versions of EC, B would be speaking truly if B were then to admit that he did not &ldquo;know&rdquo; it was a zebra (DeRose 2000). According to Keith DeRose ( ibid ., Section 6), what EC does license, in terms of legitimate ways for B to close out the dialogue, are only metalinguistic claims like: I was previously such that an utterance of &lsquo;B knows it is a zebra&rsquo; would have expressed a true proposition, but the different and more demanding proposition which such an utterance would now express would not be true. Nikola Kompa (2002, 5) has observed that EC would also seemingly permit B to say, Had I uttered &lsquo;I know it is a zebra&rsquo; earlier, I would thereby have expressed a truth, but I do not know that it is a zebra. Kompa calls this &ldquo;an unpleasant consequence&rdquo; of EC. And one might similarly regard claims in which there is explicit relativization of &lsquo;know&rsquo; to the relevant standards, as in, I know S1 that it is a zebra, but I don&rsquo;t know S2 that it is a zebra. As Kent Bach (2005, 58&ndash;61) says, there is nothing in EC that precludes the legitimacy of such claims. After all, they merely involve making explicit what are, according to EC, the propositions expressed by the relevant utterances. But the contextualist may reply that any unpleasantness or feeling of unfamiliarity attending such claims stems from our failing to be fully aware of the context-sensitivity of the expressions in question. (Compare how Cohen (2001, 89) responds to a certain objection of Feldman&rsquo;s (2001, 77).) Whether this sort of error theory is problematic is a separate issue, discussed below. That EC concerns &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo;, rather than knowledge, might help to defuse other concerns about EC. As noted above, Lewis says dramatic and apparently troubling things when discussing his contextualist theory. For example, he says that once certain skeptical possibilities are raised &ldquo;[knowledge] vanishes&rdquo; (1996, 560). But his theory implies this only because Lewis is using the word &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; in a context where skeptical possibilities are not properly ignored and not excluded by his evidence. A contextualist can contend that this is less troubling than it might seem. Lewis&rsquo;s theory does not imply that this vanishing extends to ordinary claims to &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; outside of skeptical contexts. They remain true. Because it suggests otherwise, Lewis&rsquo;s speaking of knowledge &ldquo;vanishing&rdquo;, as opposed to the falsity of certain propositions expressed by &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences, is misleading (see Bach 2005, 54&ndash;55, and DeRose 2000). 4.2 EC and Skepticism While a reminder that EC is a semantic or metalinguistic thesis helps both to defuse certain objections and to expose as misleading Lewisian presentations of the view, it also sets the stage for a more difficult objection for contextualists to counter: namely, that it does not successfully respond to skepticism. On one version of this complaint (e.g., Feldman 2001, Conee 2014), it is said that EC per se does not generate the results essential to the contextualist resolution of SA, for example. That the propositions expressed by utterances of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences in ordinary contexts are true, for example, is not secured by EC on its own; only a substantive theory of what is required for the truth of a &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; claim (in context) can deliver that result. On another version, the objection is simply that EC does not correctly characterize the skeptic&rsquo;s position at all. As we have seen, EC has it that skeptical claims express truths only relative to extraordinarily high epistemic standards. But surely, the objection runs, what is at issue between skeptics and non-skeptics is the quality of the evidence we in fact have, and whether we satisfy even our ordinary epistemic standards (see Kornblith 2000, 27; Feldman 2004, 32; cf. Feldman 1999, 2001). While he is prepared to grant the contextualist his/her semantic thesis, Ernest Sosa (2000) likewise takes it to have limited relevance to epistemology generally. For, just because it is restricted to certain metalinguistic claims, EC has only limited significance for skepticism: from the fact that, in non-skeptical contexts, we can use &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo; to express propositions which are true, nothing follows about whether we know anything&mdash;a question which we can and do wonder about in philosophical contexts. The proponent of EC might deny that whether an utterance of &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo; expresses a truth admits of any acontextual answer. But Sosa&rsquo;s objection does not imply that those utterances have a truth value outside of any context. The objection specifies one or more contexts&mdash;namely, the context(s) of philosophical discourse about &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;. Several proponents of EC have offered replies to the charge that EC does not fairly characterize and/or engage with skepticism.

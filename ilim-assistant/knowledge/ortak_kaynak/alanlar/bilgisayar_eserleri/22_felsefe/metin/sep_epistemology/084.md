One problem with the entitlement strategy is that of leaching (Wright 2004). Since \(H\) implies \(\neg BIV\), if \(\neg BIV\) is false then so is \(H\). So any risk that \(\neg BIV\) is false&mdash;that you are a \(BIV\)&mdash;is inherited by \(H\), notwithstanding your evidence for \(H\). But, having no evidence for \(\neg BIV\), you have no epistemic safeguards against that risk: your entitlement for \(\neg BIV\) has nothing to do with whether it&rsquo;s true, or likely so, but only reflects your being constrained to rely on it. So your belief in \(H\) is equally at risk, and so isn&rsquo;t known (or justified). See Wright 2004, 2012 and 2014, and Moretti and Wright 2023 for responses. A related problem is that of alchemy (McGlynn 2014a). Suppose that, in light of your entitlement to \(\neg BIV\), it&rsquo;s seeming to you as though you have hands provides you with an evidential warrant for \(H\). Since \(H\) implies \(\neg BIV\), closure seems to require that you have, not merely an entitlement, but also an evidential warrant for \(\neg BIV\). In response Wright concedes that closure for evidential warrants fails, but insists that closure for warrants per se, which may be evidential or not, is still preserved (Wright 2004). But the denials of wholesale skeptical hypotheses, and most other cornerstones, are contingent; and the idea that we could enjoy warrants for belief in contingent propositions without any evidence is, to many, doubtful. Wright suggests that we don&rsquo;t believe cornerstones, but only trust or accept them. But knowledge is also standardly taken to require, not mere acceptance or trust, but also belief. And even if the attitude that knowledge requires is expanded to include acceptance or trust, knowledge is also standardly understood to require proper basing. But it&rsquo;s not clear how your acceptance/trust in \(Q\) can be based on a default entitlement. The frontloader who mobilizes the entitlement strategy will, then, need another way to handle such hypotheses. 3.4 Transmissivism Since &sect;3.1 we&rsquo;ve been pursuing the preservationist option, according to which transmission sometimes fails&mdash;in Dretske cases, at least&mdash;but, when it does fail, something ensures that the conclusion ends up being known anyway, so that closure is preserved. In this section we&rsquo;ll explore the transmissivist option, according to which transmission (and, therefore, closure) succeeds, if not always, then at least in some Dretske cases. According to dogmatism , its perceptually seeming to you as though \(H\) suffices on its own, in the absence of defeaters, to warrant the belief that \(H\) (Pryor 2000, 2004, and 2012). [ 59 ] According to disjunctivism , in ordinary, non-skeptical scenarios your evidence doesn&rsquo;t merely consist in your seeming to have hands (as it would if you were a \(BIV)\), but in your seeing that you have them, where &ldquo;seeing&rdquo; is factive: you can only see that you have hands if you have them (McDowell 1986 and 1995, Pritchard 2012). And according to \(E = K\), your evidence is all and only that which you know. Since, in the &ldquo;good&rdquo;, non-skeptical case, you know \(H, H\) constitutes evidence that you can then wield against skeptical hypotheses (Williamson 2000). Frontloading is false on each of these views, at least with respect to wholesale skeptical hypotheses: you don&rsquo;t need to know \(\neg BIV\) in order to acquire your knowledge of \(H\). As a result, it seems, nothing stands in the way of learning, in the style of G. E. Moore, \(\neg BIV\) by inferring it from \(H\) (Moore 1939 [1959]). Correspondingly, advocates of these views very typically endorse that Moorean inference. [ 60 ] These views confront the robust intuition that the Moorean inference doesn&rsquo;t transmit. One explanation for this is that we&rsquo;re confusing the question whether an argument can rationally resolve initial open-mindedness about the conclusion with whether warrant transmits. If you are, like the skeptic, initially open-minded about whether \(\neg BIV\) is true, then you are indeed rationally obliged to discount your putative visual evidence for \(H\) (since it isn&rsquo;t probative if you are a \(BIV)\), and so can&rsquo;t resolve your initial open-mindedness concerning \(\neg BIV\) by inferring it from \(H\). Since this only applies if you are initially open-minded, that inability is a dialectical failing (even if the dialectic is, as it were, in your head) and varies depending on the audience. Warrant transmission, however, is an epistemic, not a dialectical, matter, and doesn&rsquo;t so vary. If you can acquire a warrant for \(H\) solely on the basis of its seeming that \(H\), as per the dogmatist, or on the basis of your seeing that \(H\), as per the disjunctivist, then you can acquire a warrant for \(\neg BIV\) by inference from \(H\) whether or not doing so resolves antecedent open-mindedness about the conclusion (Pryor 2004; see also Brown 2005). (Simion 2023 criticizes this explanation of our intuition but offers a different explanation in defense of transmissivism.) One problem with this explanation is that virtually everyone thinks that they&rsquo;re not \(BIV\)s, and so aren&rsquo;t open-minded on the issue, but still intuit that there&rsquo;s something wrong with the Moorean inference. The transmissivist can, however, respond that we&rsquo;re intuiting, not that the argument doesn&rsquo;t work for us, but that it would beg the question against a skeptic. (Kelp 2023, however, argues that you can&rsquo;t come to know a proposition by means of a question-begging argument.) It&rsquo;s unclear, however, what purpose the Moorean inference serves. Skeptical hypotheses are of little intrinsic interest aside from the skeptic&rsquo;s wielding such hypotheses against ordinary knowledge. But, according to the transmissivist, we don&rsquo;t need to know beforehand that skeptical hypotheses are false in order to acquire ordinary knowledge. So ordinary knowledge is secure whatever the fortunes of skeptical hypotheses might be; the Moorean inference can play no coherent role in the defense of that knowledge. At best, it can show that closure can also be preserved. But transmissivists are precisely those who believe in SPC because they believe in SPT. So this amounts to showing that if we endorse transmissivism we&rsquo;ll preserve transmission (see Alspector-Kelly &sect;5.1). Two problems for transmissivism have already been canvassed above. First, it countenances knowledge acquisition through inferences from \(P\) to \(Q\) in Dretske cases, wherein evidence \(E\) favoring \(P\) lowers the probability of \(Q\). It&rsquo;s at least odd to think that you can nevertheless learn \(Q\) by means of such an inference (see &sect;3.1 ). Second, you can recognize beforehand that, in Dretske cases, your method for evaluating \(Q\) will deliver \(Q\) whether or not \(Q\) is true (see &sect;3.2 ). It&rsquo;s very implausible that such inferences transmit. A third problem is that transmissivism appears to license bootstrapping. You look at a series of colored cards. In each case you note the color the card appears to be, believe that it is that color, and conjoin these: &ldquo;card 1 appears red and is red&rdquo;, for example. You do the same for the rest of the 1000 cards, and infer from the 1000 propositions that \(A\), your color vision has been accurate 1000 times. Finally, you inductively infer from \(A\) that \(R\), your color vision is reliable. As a warrant for \(R\), this is intuitively absurd. But it&rsquo;s difficult for the transmissivist to say why. Only skeptics will deny that you can learn the color of something by noting the color it appears to have. And the subsequent inferences to \(A\) are instances of MPC. The transmissivist could resist the inference from \(A\) to \(R\), which is inductive. But A seems to constitute excellent inductive grounds for \(R\). [ 61 ] You can also recognize that, before you get started, you will inevitably end up endorsing \(R\) (and \(A)\) whether or not it is true. But, as with Dretske cases, if you know this beforehand, there&rsquo;s no point in your going through the motions. (See Bergmann 2004, Cohen 2005, Markie 2005, and Pryor 2004 for closure-preserving responses to bootstrapping, and Alspector-Kelly 2019, &sect;11.1.2. for a closure-denying response.) [ 62 ] A fourth concern is that transmissivism seems to license a pernicious resistance to evidence. [ 63 ] It follows from \(P\) that any future evidence against \(P\) must be misleading. Suppose you know \(P\) and infer the latter from it. If transmissivism is true, then you know the latter. But, surely, you shouldn&rsquo;t discount future evidence against \(P\) (Harman 1973, attributing the problem to Saul Kripke). Audi 1988 argues that closure fails in light of this problem.

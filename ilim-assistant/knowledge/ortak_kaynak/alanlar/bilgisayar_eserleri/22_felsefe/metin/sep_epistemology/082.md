[ 38 ] A warrant is not exhausted by the specific feature that prompts your belief, which we might call your basis; it also includes whatever other conditions, both within your mind and in the external environment, must be in place in order for you to know on that basis. For example, it&rsquo;s not enough that your colleague told you that a quorum is present. After all, she could have been lying. And even if truthful, she could have hallucinated the twelve faculty that she &ldquo;saw&rdquo;, which was, coincidentally, the right number. Or you could have misheard her, thinking she said &ldquo;quorum&rdquo; when she actually said &ldquo;forum&rdquo;. In any of these cases her testimony doesn&rsquo;t provide you with a way of knowing&mdash;a warrant&mdash;that a quorum is present. So having a warrant requires, in addition to whatever the basis for your belief might be, also those internal and external conditions that must be in place in order for you to know in response to that basis. Your already having a warrant for \(Q\), then, doesn&rsquo;t prevent your acquiring a second warrant for \(Q\) because you inferred it from \(P\). So, for SPT to be true, you must always acquire a new warrant for \(Q\) whenever its antecedent applies, whether or not you already know \(Q\). Since the antecedents of SPC and SPT are the same, and the consequent of SPT requires what the consequent of SPC requires and more, transmission implies closure, and closure failure implies transmission failure. But closure doesn&rsquo;t imply transmission, and transmission failure doesn&rsquo;t imply closure failure. There are, as a result, two different kinds of closure advocate. The first endorses closure because they endorse transmission. Call them transmissivists. Transmissivism hews as closely as possible to the knowledge-expansion intuition behind closure advocacy referenced in &sect;1.1 and, indeed, amounts to a formalization of that intuition. The second concedes that transmission occasionally fails but insists that, whenever it does fail, closure is nevertheless preserved. Call them preservationists . We&rsquo;ll discuss preservationism first. 3.1 Preservationism: Warrant for \(P\) as Warrant for \(Q\) The preservationist needs to explain why transmission fails and also why, whenever it fails, an alternative warrant for \(Q\) steps in to save the day. One such explanation is that your warrant for \(P\) always also suffices as a warrant for \(Q\) whenever \(P\) implies \(Q\). Another is that, when transmission fails, it does so because you can only have the warrant you have for \(P\) if you are already warranted in believing \(Q\). On the first explanation, although you can&rsquo;t acquire a warrant for \(\neg DM\) by inference from \(Z\), for example, \(LZ\)&mdash;your basis for \(Z\)&mdash;suffices on its own to warrant \(\neg DM\). Probabilistic considerations encourage this idea. Since \(Z\) implies \(\neg DM\), the probability axioms imply that \(p(\neg DM\mid LZ) \ge p(Z\mid LZ)\). On probabilistic threshold conceptions of knowledge, if \(LZ\) suffices for knowing \(Z\) then it also suffices for knowing \(\neg DM\). So you know \(\neg DM\) as well. However, since \(DM\) implies \(LZ, p(\neg DM\mid LZ) \lt p(\neg DM)\): \(LZ\) lowers , rather than raises, \(\neg DM\)&rsquo;s initial probability. [ 39 ] On a popular view, evidence only counts as such if it raises the probability of the hypothesis it supports. So, on that view, \(LZ\) counts against , rather than for, \(\neg DM\) (White 2006). [ 40 ] More generally, \(LZ\) presumably doesn&rsquo;t count against \(DM\) when \(DM\) implies, and so predicts, \(LZ\). But \(LZ\) can only be evidence for \(\neg DM\) if it&rsquo;s evidence against \(DM\). So it&rsquo;s not evidence for \(\neg DM\). It remains true that, since \(Z\) implies \(\neg DM, p(\neg DM\mid LZ) \ge p(Z\mid LZ)\). Since \(p(\neg DM\mid LZ) \lt p(\neg DM), p(\neg DM) \gt p(Z\mid LZ)\): the prior probability of \(\neg DM\) is higher than the probability of \(Z\) given \(LZ\). So, if \(LZ\) confers a sufficiently high probability for knowledge on \(Z\), then \(\neg DM\) must already be sufficiently probable in light, presumably, of background evidence \((BE)\). [ 41 ] Such evidence might, say, indicate that reputable zoos are unlikely to disguise their animals. [ 42 ] So transmission fails: you don&rsquo;t learn \(\neg DM\) by inference from \(Z\), but from \(BE\). But closure is still preserved (Klein 1995). However, since \(p(Q) \ge p(P)\) whenever \(P\) implies \(Q\), your inferring \(Q\) from \(P\) is, on this approach, not only irrelevant in Dretske cases, but in all cases. Even if, unlike Dretske cases, your evidence \(E\) for \(P\) raises \(Q\)&rsquo;s antecedent probability provided by \(BE\) above the threshold required for knowledge, the warrant you have for \(Q\) is still provided by the combined evidence \((E \amp BE)\). And you have that warrant as soon as you acquire \(E\), and so before you infer \(Q\) from \(P\). That inference delivers no more evidence than you already have. But transmission requires that you acquire a new warrant because you infer \(Q\) from \(P\). So transmission fails in every case. But transmission was only supposed to fail in Dretske cases (Alspector-Kelly 2019, &sect;5.5). More generally, if your warrant for \(P\) inevitably suffices as a warrant for \(Q\) whenever \(P\) implies \(Q\), then this counts against, rather than in favor of, transmission: it implies that transmission always fails or, at least, that it is aways superfluous (see Moretti and Piazza 2023&rsquo;s &ldquo;indirectness template&rdquo; for transmission failure). [ 43 ] , [ 44 ] 3.2 Preservationism: Frontloading The second explanation for why you inevitably end up knowing \(Q\) when transmission fails is that, in Dretske cases at least, you need to already know \(Q\) in order to learn \(P\), given the way that you learned \(P\). In order to learn \(Z\) on the basis of \(LZ\), for example, you need to know \(\neg DM\). Call this frontloading. [ 45 ] One line of reasoning for this is that, without knowing \(\neg DM\), all you&rsquo;ll be in a position to learn from \(LZ\) is the disjunction &ldquo;\(Z\) or \(DM\)&rdquo;, since both predict \(LZ\). [ 46 ] Another such line is that the inference from \(Z\) to \(\neg DM\) is viciously circular. That inference only delivers knowledge of \(\neg DM\) if you know \(Z\). But a condition of your knowing \(Z\) on the basis of \(LZ\) is that the animal isn&rsquo;t disguised to look like a zebra and, so, isn&rsquo;t a mule disguised to look that way: precisely \(\neg DM\). So, if you initially don&rsquo;t know \(\neg DM\), then you don&rsquo;t know that \(LZ\) warrants \(Z\), and so can&rsquo;t appeal to \(Z\) in support of \(\neg DM\). [ 47 ] On either reasoning, the story goes, transmission fails: you can&rsquo;t acquire a new warrant for the conclusion when you needed one already in order to learn the premise. [ 48 ] This last step&mdash;if frontloading is required then transmission fails&mdash;is virtually universally assumed. But it&rsquo;s not clear why. True, you can&rsquo;t acquire your first warrant by the inference from \(Z\) to \(\neg DM\), since the latter must be already warranted. But recall that you can have multiple warrants for the same proposition. Of course, if your only putative warrant for \(\neg DM\) is by inference from \(Z\), then transmission clearly fails: you need that warrant to start with, but won&rsquo;t get it until you&rsquo;ve finished. But so long as you start out with a prior warrant for \(\neg DM\) that doesn&rsquo;t depend on this inference, it&rsquo;s not obvious why the inference can&rsquo;t deliver another one (White 2006, Silins 2005, and Alspector-Kelly 2019; for possible examples of cases where frontloading is required but transmission succeeds, see the latter, &sect;6.7.2). It&rsquo;s also widely assumed that, if frontloading is not required, then transmission succeeds. But there are other accounts of transmission failure that don&rsquo;t require antecedent knowledge of \(Q\), and so that are compatible with closure denial. For example: \(DM\) implies \(LZ\). So your method for evaluating \(\neg DM\)&mdash;namely, determining whether \(\neg DM\) by inference from what you take yourself to learn when you look in the paddock&mdash;will inevitably deliver \(\neg DM\) whenever \(DM\). You can, in fact, recognize this before you look in the paddock: knowing what you&rsquo;ll do if \(LZ\), and knowing that \(DM\) implies \(LZ\), you know that you&rsquo;ll arrive at \(\neg DM\) if \(DM\). Moreover, if \(LZ\) is false, you&rsquo;ll still arrive at \(\neg DM\), since \(\neg LZ\) implies \(\neg DM\). So you can know before you look that, no matter what you see, and no matter what&rsquo;s there, your method will deliver \(\neg DM\). It&rsquo;s very plausible that no such method can deliver a warrant for \(\neg DM\). But this explanation of transmission failure, while compatible with your knowing \(Z\) on \(LZ\), doesn&rsquo;t require that you initially know \(\neg DM\), and so is compatible with closure failure (White 2006 and Alspector-Kelly 2019, ch. 4). [ 49 ] Since both lines of reasoning for frontloading appear to apply to any skeptical hypothesis, front-loading requires prior knowledge of the falsehood of every such hypothesis, both wholesale and piecemeal. Since knowledge is widely taken to require belief and proper basing, you would also have to believe that they are false on appropriate bases. [ 50 ] But some will likely mobilize concepts&mdash;hitherto undreamt-of animal-disguising technology, for example&mdash;that you don&rsquo;t even understand.

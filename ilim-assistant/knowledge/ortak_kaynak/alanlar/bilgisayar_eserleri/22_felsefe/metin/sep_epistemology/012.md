Of course, the question about how I can be justified in believing that I&rsquo;m not a BIV is not especially hard for externalists to answer. From the point of view of an externalist, the fact that you and the BIV have the very same states of mind need not be at all relevant to the issue of whether you&rsquo;re justified in believing that you&rsquo;re not a BIV, since such justification isn&rsquo;t fully determined by those mental states anyway. The philosophers who have had to do considerable work to answer the question how I can be justified in believing that I&rsquo;m not a BIV have typically done this work not directly in reply to BKCA, but rather in reply to BJUA. What might justify your belief that you&rsquo;re not a BIV? According to some philosophers, you are justified in believing that you&rsquo;re not a BIV because, for instance, you know perfectly well that current technology doesn&rsquo;t enable anyone to create a BIV. The proponent of the BIV hypothesis might regard this answer as no better than the Moorean response to BKCA: if you are allowed to appeal to (what you regard as your) knowledge of current technology to justify your belief that you&rsquo;re not a BIV, then why can&rsquo;t the Moorean equally well rely on his knowledge that he has hands to justify his belief that he&rsquo;s not a BIV? Philosophers who accept this objection, but who don&rsquo;t want to ground your justification for believing that you&rsquo;re not a BIV in purely externalistic factors, may instead claim that your belief is justified by the fact that your own beliefs about the external world provide a better explanation of your sense experiences than does the BIV hypothesis (see Russell 1912 and Vogel 1990 for influential defenses of this argument against skepticism, and see Neta 2004 for a rebuttal). 6.4 Responses to the Defeasibility Argument The most influential reply to BKDA is to say that, when I acquire evidence that I don&rsquo;t have hands, such evidence makes me cease to know that I have hands. On this view, when I acquire such evidence, the argument above is sound. But prior to my acquiring such evidence, (4) is false, and so the argument above is not sound. Thus, the truth of (4), and consequently the soundness of this argument, depends on whether or not I have evidence that I don&rsquo;t have hands. If I do have such evidence, then the argument is sound, but of course it has no general skeptical implications: all it shows that I can&rsquo;t know some fact whenever I have evidence that the fact doesn&rsquo;t obtain (versions of this view are defended by Harman 1973 and Ginet 1980). Plausible as this reply has seemed to most philosophers, it has been effectively challenged by Lasonen-Aarnio (2014b). Her argument is this: presumably, it&rsquo;s possible to have more than enough evidence to know some fact. But if it&rsquo;s possible to have more than enough evidence to know some fact, it follows that one might still know that fact even if one acquires some slight evidence against it. And yet, it would be wrong to leave one&rsquo;s confidence entirely unaffected by the slight evidence that one acquires against that fact: though the evidence might be too slight to destroy one&rsquo;s knowledge, it cannot be too slight to diminish one&rsquo;s confidence even slightly. So long as one could continue to know a fact while rationally diminishing one&rsquo;s confidence in it in response to new evidence, the most popular reply to the defeasibility argument fails. Other replies to the defeasibility argument include the denial of premise (2), [ 65 ] the denial of (4) (McDowell 1982, Kern 2006 [2017]), and the claim that the context-sensitivity of &ldquo;knows&rdquo; means that (4) is true only relative to contexts in which the possibility of future defeaters is relevant (see Neta 2002). But neither of these replies has yet received widespread assent. 6.5 Responses to the Epistemic Possibility Argument The most common reply to BEPA is either to deny premise (1), or to deny that we are justified in believing that premise (1) is true. Most writers would deny premise (1), and would do so on whatever grounds they have for thinking that I can know that I&rsquo;m not a BIV: knowing that something is not the case excludes that thing&rsquo;s being epistemically possible for you. [ 66 ] But a couple of influential writers&mdash;most notably Rogers Albritton and Thompson Clarke (see Albritton 2011 and Clarke 1972)&mdash;do not claim that premise (1) is false. Rather, they deny that we are justified in believing that premise (1) is true. According to these writers, what normally justifies us in believing that something or other is epistemically possible is that we can conceive of discovering that it is true. For instance, what justifies me in believing, say, that it&rsquo;s possible that Donald Trump has resigned is that I can clearly conceive of discovering that Donald Trump has resigned. But if I attempt to conceive of discovering that I&rsquo;m a BIV, it&rsquo;s not clear that I can succeed in this attempt. I may conceive of coming upon some evidence that I&rsquo;m a BIV&mdash;but, insofar as this evidence tells in favor of the hypothesis that I&rsquo;m a BIV, doesn&rsquo;t it also undermine its own credibility? In such a case, is there anything at all that would count as &ldquo;my evidence&rdquo;? (see Neta 2019 for an elaboration of this point). Without being able to answer this question in the affirmative, it&rsquo;s not clear that I can conceive of anything that would amount to discovering that I&rsquo;m a BIV. Of course, from the fact that I cannot conceive of anything that would amount to discovering that I&rsquo;m a BIV, it doesn&rsquo;t follow that I&rsquo;m not a BIV&mdash;and so it doesn&rsquo;t even follow that it&rsquo;s not possible that I&rsquo;m a BIV. But, whether or not it is possible that I&rsquo;m a BIV, I can&rsquo;t be justified in thinking that it is. And that&rsquo;s to say that I can&rsquo;t be justified in accepting premise (1) of BEPA.

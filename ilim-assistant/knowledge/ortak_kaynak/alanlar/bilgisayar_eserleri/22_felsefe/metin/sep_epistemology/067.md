In this way, an utterance of SA&rsquo;s conclusion expresses a truth only because, owing to the introduction of a high-standards context, what it expresses is that the subject does not stand in an extraordinarily strong epistemic position with regard to the proposition that she has hands. But that , of course, is compatible with her meeting less demanding standards, such as those in play in ordinary contexts. Thus, DeRose says: &hellip;our ordinary claims to know [are] safeguarded from the apparently powerful attacks of the skeptic, while, at the same time, the persuasiveness of the skeptical arguments is explained. (DeRose 1992, 917) Of course, the latter is explained only if we also have an explanation of why we thought that the skeptical argument threatened our ordinary claims to &ldquo;know&rdquo;. The contextualist seeks to explain this by claiming that competent speakers can fail to be aware of the context-sensitivity of sentences involving &lsquo;know&rsquo;: This misleads them into thinking that certain knowledge ascriptions conflict, when they are in fact compatible. Contextualism thus combines a contextualist semantics for knowledge ascriptions with a kind of error theory&mdash;a claim that competent speakers are systematically misled by the contextualist semantics. (Cohen 1999, 77; see too: Cohen 2014, 72; DeRose 1995, 40&ndash;41; 1999, 194; 2004b, 37; 2009, 159). 3.3 Some Specific Forms of EC While the general form of the preceding response to skeptical arguments is widely accepted among contextualists, different proponents of EC have proposed different specific versions of the view, and so explanations of what is going on in such arguments that differ in their details. Thus, Keith DeRose proposes a conversational rule, the &ldquo;Rule of Sensitivity&rdquo;, whereby an utterance of &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo; will tend to express a truth only if S believes that p , p , and S &rsquo;s epistemic position is such that his belief that p is sensitive (DeRose 1995, 36). (The notion of sensitivity derives from Robert Nozick (1981): one&rsquo;s belief that p , formed via a particular method M , is sensitive just in case if p were false one would not believe via M that p . Nozick, notably, is concerned with general conditions on knowing and not with conversational rules, and unlike DeRose he does not assert the attributor context-sensitivity of &ldquo;know&rdquo;.) In the case of SA, the mere uttering of P1 induces very high standards for &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;, as the belief that I&rsquo;m not a BIV is manifestly in sensitive; and at those very high standards, P1 is true. As for P2, DeRose claims that it is true &ldquo;regardless of what epistemic standard it is evaluated at&rdquo; ( ibid ., 39). As it happens, though, in SA P2 is evaluated at the very high standards put into play by P1. But then, the only reading on which SA&rsquo;s conclusion is true is at the unusually inflated standards induced by P1. The truth of C thus allows that we correctly say we &ldquo;know&rdquo; that we have hands when lower standards are in play, as they usually are. DeRose&rsquo;s brand of EC is externalist, in the sense that it deploys a notion, sensitivity, that has figured in certain externalist theories of knowledge. Likewise externalist is Mark Heller&rsquo;s brand of EC, which is both reliabilist in spirit and an instance of the RA approach. On Heller&rsquo;s view (1995), just how reliable a belief-forming process must be for an attribution of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; to express a truth depends upon context, in the attributor sense introduced above; and the relevant alternatives are those such that, if the process in question is reliable to the contextually required degree, the subject will be able to discriminate between those alternatives and what is in fact the case. By contrast, Stewart Cohen&rsquo;s version of EC is internalist. [ 8 ] According to Cohen (1986, 1987, 1988, 1999, 2005, 2014) &lsquo;know&rsquo; inherits its indexical nature (1988, 97) from that of &lsquo;justified&rsquo;. Justification comes in degrees; and what counts as justification simpliciter &mdash;i.e., justification to the level required for an attribution of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; to express a truth&mdash;is governed by a &ldquo;rule of salience &rdquo;(1998, 292, n. 11), whereby one&rsquo;s evidence/reasons must be good enough to preclude salient possibilities of error. What the skeptic does is make salient certain extraordinary not- p possibilities, with the result that the standard of how good one&rsquo;s evidence/reasons must be goes way up . Similar to Cohen&rsquo;s account is David Lewis&rsquo; (1996). According to Lewis: Subject S knows proposition P iff P holds in every possibility left uneliminated by P&rsquo;s evidence; equivalently, iff S &rsquo;s evidence eliminates every possibility in which not- P . (1996, 551) Why doesn&rsquo;t this just amount to infallibilism, and so a dramatic shrinking of what we can be said truly to know? Because, according to Lewis, the extension of &lsquo;every&rsquo; is restricted to a particular conversational domain ( ibid ., 553&ndash;554). Thus, Lewis thinks, certain not- p possibilities will be &ldquo;properly ignored&rdquo; in any given situation. As to what determines whether a given possibility is properly ignored, Lewis suggests a number of rules, including the Rule of Attention: &ldquo;a possibility that is not ignored is ipso facto not properly ignored&rdquo; ( ibid. , 559). And this is why the skeptic&rsquo;s argument is so irresistible, Lewis thinks. For skepticism&mdash;indeed, epistemology generally&mdash; just is the making salient of certain possibilities of error. For example, to consider whether we know that we are not BIVs, as assessing the first premise of SA requires, is ipso facto not to ignore that possibility. If one&rsquo;s evidence consists in one&rsquo;s perceptual and memory experiences, as Lewis holds, then (he thinks) it definitely does not eliminate the BIV possibility. So we do in fact have lots of knowledge, according to Lewis, but it is &ldquo;elusive&rdquo;: &ldquo;Examine it, and straightway it vanishes&rdquo; ( ibid ., 560). Michael Blome-Tillman (2009, 2014) has proposed a modification to Lewis&rsquo; view. Specifically, Blome-Tillman recommends that Lewis&rsquo;s Rule of Attention be replaced by the following Rule of Presupposition: If w is compatible with the speakers&rsquo; pragmatic presuppositions in C , then w cannot be properly ignored in C . (2009, 256; 2014, 20) Here, &lsquo;pragmatic presupposition&rsquo; is to be understood along the lines suggested by Stalnaker (1974, 2002). As stated by Blome-Tillman, x pragmatically presupposes p in C [ iff ] x is disposed to behave, in her use of language, as if she believed p to be common ground in C . ( Ibid. ) Blome-Tillman regards the resultant Presuppositional Epistemic Contextualism as superior to Lewis&rsquo;s, not least because it allows that the mere mentioning or thinking of a skeptical possibility needn&rsquo;t make a difference to the content and truth-value of a given &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; claim&mdash;whether it does so depends on whether it effects or reflects a shift in the pragmatic presuppositions of the parties involved. Still other versions of EC take the shifting standards to directly govern ascriptions of evidence. For Robert Hambourger, it is &ldquo;the amount of evidence needed to know a proposition&rdquo; which &ldquo;varies with standards of caution&rdquo; (1987, 260, emphasis added). For Ram Neta, it is not the quantity of evidence required for a truthful utterance of &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo; that is governed by context-sensitive standards, but rather whether certain mental states of the subject&rsquo;s count as evidence at all. The relevant rule once again bears similarities to those proposed by Cohen and Lewis: (R) When one raises an hypothesis H that is an uneliminated counterpossibility with respect to S&rsquo;s knowing that p at t, one restricts what counts in one&rsquo;s context of appraisal&hellip; as S&rsquo;s body of evidence at t to just those mental states that S has, and would have, at t whether or not H is true. (2002, 674; 2003a, 23&ndash;4) By exploiting this rule, the skeptic is able to disqualify certain mental states&mdash;e.g., those involved in my current experience (as) of seeing my hands&mdash;as constituting evidence. Although, once again, when the relevant skeptical possibilities are not raised, those experiences qualify as evidence sufficient for truthful ascriptions of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; that I have hands. Finally, some theorists have suggested that the context-sensitivity of sentences involving &lsquo;know&rsquo; is owing to a more general context-sensitivity in certain explanatory relations and claims. For example, Steven Rieber (1998) has proposed that, in general, S knows that P iff &ldquo; the fact that P explains why S believes that P &rdquo; (1998, 194). According to Rieber, however, explanations are always at least implicitly contrastive, and whether one thing explains another depends upon which contrasts are salient.

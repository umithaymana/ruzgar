The second premise is &ldquo;if you don&rsquo;t know \(\neg BIV\) then you don&rsquo;t know \(H\)&rdquo;. In &sect;2.1 this was understood to derive from closure. But now this means &ldquo;if you don&rsquo;t already know \(\neg BIV\) then you don&rsquo;t know \(H\)&rdquo;. One might indeed wonder whether the force of the argument comes from an intuition, not that ordinary knowledge would implausibly put you in a position to learn that the skeptical hypothesis is false, but instead that we need to know the denials of skeptical hypotheses in order to acquire ordinary knowledge in the first place. To avoid this version of the skeptical argument, the frontloader needs sources of warrant against every skeptical hypothesis whose denial is implied by the mundane knowledge claims that they want to endorse. There are two possible sources. [ 52 ] First, background information might provide such a warrant. Second, you might have a default warrant, or &ldquo;entitlement&rdquo;. 3.3.1 Warrant by Background Information One might claim that background information&mdash;we don&rsquo;t yet have the technology to envat a brain, for example&mdash;provides you with a warrant for believing \(\neg BIV\), as well as the denials of other wholesale skeptical hypotheses (Leite 2015, 207). An objection is that, if you&rsquo;re a \(BIV\), you don&rsquo;t just seem to have hands; your total experience, and so what you take to be your background information, remains in place. So if you&rsquo;re in no position to infer \(\neg BIV\) from \(H\), you&rsquo;re in no more a position to infer it from putative background information. But the fact that it wouldn&rsquo;t be information if you were a \(BIV\) doesn&rsquo;t imply that it isn&rsquo;t information if you&rsquo;re not. On certain externalist views of evidence, your evidence in the &ldquo;good&rdquo; \(\neg BIV\) case is much richer than it is in the &ldquo;bad&rdquo; \(BIV\) case, and the former may well provide you with the wherewithal to know \(\neg BIV\). [ 53 ] However, that evidence will also certainly include \(H\). So you don&rsquo;t need a warrant for \(\neg BIV\) in order to acquire your warrant for \(H\) after all; the frontloading claim is false. And that seems to allow transmission from \(H\) to \(\neg BIV\). Closure-preserving externalist views of evidence sit more comfortably with transmissivism than with frontloading (see &sect;3.4 below). [ 54 ] The situation is quite different with respect to piecemeal skeptical hypotheses. \(DM\), for example, doesn&rsquo;t incorporate whatever background information that you might have against it (to the effect that reputable zoos aren&rsquo;t likely to deceive the public, say). However, it remains intuitive, not just that you can&rsquo;t learn that it&rsquo;s not a disguised mule by inference from &ldquo;it&rsquo;s a zebra&rdquo;&mdash;which intuition the frontloader endorses&mdash;but also that, given your circumstances, you don&rsquo;t know it at all. That intuition is particularly strong with respect to lottery propositions. Perhaps background evidence suffices to warrant &ldquo;most people lose the lottery&rdquo;, &ldquo;cars aren&rsquo;t often stolen&rdquo;, &ldquo;this report is likely not a misprint&rdquo;, and the like. But you need a warrant for the specific claim that this particular ticket, car, or report isn&rsquo;t one of the exceptions. And it&rsquo;s difficult to see how merely statistical information&mdash;the only kind you seem to have in these cases&mdash;suffices for that purpose when you know that such exceptions exist and nothing that you&rsquo;re aware of distinguishes your case from any other (Sharon and Spectre 2017a, 24&ndash;27). So the frontloader needs to explain these intuitions. One popular approach is that of the contextualist. The standards for knowledge, according to the contextualist, can vary from one context of attribution to another. In ordinary contexts, wherein the standards are relatively low, you count as knowing both the ordinary claim \(P\) and the denial of the skeptical hypothesis \(Q\). But when the person who attributes knowledge (who may or may not be you) contemplates \(Q\), doing so typically raises the standards applicable in their context to the point where you count as knowing neither \(P\) nor \(Q\). So, when the attributor intuits that you don&rsquo;t know \(Q\), they&rsquo;re right; but that&rsquo;s only because they&rsquo;re contemplating it. In ordinary contexts, when they&rsquo;re not contemplating \(Q\), you do know it. In neither context is closure false. The contextualist applies this explanation of our intuitions, not just to lottery propositions, but to all piecemeal and wholesale skeptical hypotheses (Cohen 1988, DeRose 2009, Lewis 1996; see also the entry on epistemic contextualism ). Contextualism does not, however, require that closure is true. Heller 1999 claims that, although you know neither \(P\) nor \(Q\) in high-standards contexts, you know \(P\) but not \(Q\) in ordinary, low-standards contexts, so that closure fails in the latter contexts. The closure-affirming contextualist needs to explain both why we need to attribute knowledge of the denials of skeptical hypotheses in ordinary cases and where that knowledge comes from. With respect to the latter question, the contextualist isn&rsquo;t likely to claim that knowledge of \(Q\) in ordinary contexts is acquired by transmission since it&rsquo;s wildly unlikely that people in ordinary (or any other) contexts believe \(Q\) in Dretske cases because they&rsquo;ve inferred it from \(P\). So the closure-preserving contextualist is presumably a frontloader: even in ordinary contexts, you need to know \(Q\) in order to acquire your knowledge of \(P\). But while it&rsquo;s plausible that, in high-standards contexts wherein \(Q\) is salient, you need to know \(Q\) in order to know \(P\), it&rsquo;s far from clear why that would be so in ordinary contexts as well. Why, for example, would you need to know that your car isn&rsquo;t stolen (as well as the denial of every other wholesale and piecemeal skeptical hypothesis), which possibility hasn&rsquo;t crossed your mind (or that of the attributor, if different), in order to know that it&rsquo;s in 3b where you left it, so long as, in fact, it isn&rsquo;t stolen? The obvious answer is that requiring knowledge of \(Q\) preserves closure in ordinary contexts. But if the contextualist affirms closure because of, for example, the abominable conjunction objection, contextualism already addresses that objection without requiring closure. [ 55 ] In my high-standards context in which I contemplate &ldquo;your car is stolen&rdquo;, &ldquo;you know your car is in 3b but not that it&rsquo;s not stolen&rdquo; is infelicitous because you know neither, and so don&rsquo;t know that your car is in 3b. It&rsquo;s entirely compatible with this that, in low-standards contexts wherein I&rsquo;m not contemplating car thefts, &ldquo;you know that it&rsquo;s in 3b but not that it&rsquo;s not stolen&rdquo; is true. And any inclination to think that the conjunction is abominable in ordinary contexts as well is explicable as an instance of the &ldquo;semantic blindness&rdquo; tendency to fail to notice that standards that apply in high-standards contexts don&rsquo;t apply in low-standards contexts. So this objection to closure denial, at least, doesn&rsquo;t discriminate between closure-affirming and closure-denying contextualism (Alspector-Kelly 2019, &sect;&sect;10.3&ndash;10.4).

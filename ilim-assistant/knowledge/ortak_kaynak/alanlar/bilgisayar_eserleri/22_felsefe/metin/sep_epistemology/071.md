Bolstering the latter suggestion are cases involving what Thomas Hofweber (1999, 98ff.) calls &ldquo;hidden relativity&rdquo;. For example, according to Hofweber, our claim, &ldquo;August is a summer month,&rdquo; presumes that we are in the Northern hemisphere. [ 17 ] A speaker may not be aware of this, however; and even those who are aware of it do not feel compelled to make that parameter explicit whenever they utter the relevant sort of sentence. So, when Schiffer says, &ldquo;no ordinary person would dream of telling you that what he meant and was implicitly stating was that he knew that p relative to such-and-such standard&rdquo; (1996, 326ff.), that in itself does not show that no such relativization is in play. It is not clear, however, whether Hofweber&rsquo;s hidden relativities provide a model for our supposed ignorance of the context-sensitivity of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences. For, while many competent speakers of English are unaware of the (alleged) relativity of &lsquo;summer month&rsquo;, once they are made aware of it, they do not actively resist its being made explicit. Whereas, when the alleged relativizations within knowledge sentences are made explicit&mdash;&ldquo;Smith doesn&rsquo;t know S that the flight stops in Chicago,&rdquo; etc. (Bach 2005, Section I)&mdash;they are often met with resistance and regarded as highlighting the controversial character of EC. [ 18 ] Further, as Hofweber says (1999, 101&ndash;2), with regard to &lsquo;summer month&rsquo;, say, there is a plausible explanation of why the relativization in question can be hidden for many speakers and, even among those who are aware of it, rarely be made explicit&mdash;namely, that most of the people with whom we discuss such things are geographically close to us. Because of this, the relativity to a hemisphere very rarely makes a difference to uses of &lsquo;summer&rsquo;. Whereas, the evidence for contextualism requires that there be some interesting variation among the standards which determine a &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentence&rsquo;s truth-conditions within this or that linguistic community, and it requires that we be guided in our everyday &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;-attributing behavior by an awareness of just this fact. This last point brings out the second question concerning the contextualist&rsquo;s error theory mentioned above&mdash;namely, whether that theory raises any problems internal to the contextualist view. For, to the extent that the context-sensitivity of the relevant expressions can remain deeply hidden, even after careful reflection, it becomes less clear that in the cases of concern (SA, the airport example, etc.) what is driving our judgments as to whether what speakers say is &lsquo;true and proper&rsquo; is, as the contextualist says, our awareness of that context-sensitivity (Rysiew 2007, 653; 2012a, 137). The contextualist must thus strike &ldquo;a delicate balance&rdquo; (Conee 2014, 67&ndash;68) between crediting us with enough of a grasp of the context-sensitivity of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences to adjust what we say to the context(s) so as to make the sentences largely express truths, while at the same time attributing to us a failure recognize such context-sensitivity when it is pointed out and when recognizing it would easily resolve puzzling apparent conflicts. Finally, as Ram Neta observes, in Hofweber&rsquo;s cases it is some worldly fact about the phenomenon in question of which people are ignorant. Whereas, &hellip;the contextualist does not want to say that our semantic ignorance about our knowledge attributions results from our ignorance of the real nature of knowledge. Rather, it is supposed to result from our ignorance about the way in which the content of those attributions depends upon contextual factors. (2003a, 404) And, as we have seen, far from referring to various worldly phenomena, &lsquo;context&rsquo; is generally said by contextualists to refer to such things as the purposes, intentions, expectations, presuppositions, etc., of the speakers who utter those sentences. (The same sorts of things, plausibly, which are responsible for determining what is expressed by attributions of flatness, say.) According to some (e.g., Rysiew 2001, 485, 507), this last point of dissimilarity between Hofweber&rsquo;s hidden relativities and that which contextualists allege intensifies concerns about the latter&rsquo;s error theory. For it seems to imply a quite specific deficit in &ldquo;our powers of semantic self-knowledge&rdquo; (Neta 2003a, 408): to say that we conflate contexts (Cohen 2014, 82) is to say that we are mixed up about what our intentions, purposes, etc., are when we utter &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences. That sort of thing does sometimes happen. (Neta&mdash; ibid ., 407&ndash;408&mdash;describes one such case.) Still, and whether or not it constitutes a deficit in &ldquo;semantic self-knowledge,&rdquo; exactly, it would be good if we had some explanation as to why many have such a hard time coming to terms with the truth of EC when the context-sensitivity of other terms is fairly easily accepted. Cohen has tentatively suggested that considerations of value might explain this: We value justification and knowledge. But contextualist theories are deflationary. Contextualism about knowledge says that most of our everyday utterances of the form &lsquo;S knows P&rsquo; are true, even though the strength of epistemic position in those instances does not meet our highest standards. In the same way, contextualism about flatness says that most of our everyday utterances of sentences of the form &lsquo;X is flat&rsquo; are true, even though X&rsquo;s surface may fall short of perfect flatness. In other words, contextualism is a &lsquo;good news, bad news&rsquo; theory. The good news is that we have lots of knowledge and many surfaces are flat; the bad news is that knowledge and flatness are not all they were cracked up to be. We find this much easier to accept in the case of flatness than knowledge, because ascriptions of flatness do not have the normative force that ascriptions of knowledge/justification do. (2014, 74; cf. 2004, 193) It is not clear whether this suggested explanation represents a step forward. At most, it seems, it would explain why we might be disappointed by what &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; typically designates; it would not explain why we have difficulty recognizing the alleged context-sensitivity in question. In any case, it should by now be clear that we have moved beyond considerations of the contextualist&rsquo;s imputation of error to ordinary speakers per se . While we are not faultless in out tacit semantic understanding, the specific ignorance asserted in defense of EC is disputable. 4.4 Linguistic Issues According to EC, &lsquo;know&rsquo; is a context-sensitive term. Among proponents of EC, however, there has been relatively little discussion, and little agreement, about what linguistic model best captures this fact. Thus, Cohen (1988, 97) speaks of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; as &ldquo;an indexical&rdquo;, [ 19 ] Hambourger (1987, 262) likens it to &lsquo;large&rsquo;, Heller (1999a, 206; 1999b, 121) says that it is a vague term, and DeRose, while at one point (1992, 920&ndash;921) using an analogy with the demonstrative &lsquo;this&rsquo;, tends to be noncommittal as to the appropriate model for &lsquo;know&rsquo;. It has been objected, however, that regardless of which model is adopted, the linguistic data surrounding &lsquo;know&rsquo; and cognate expressions is not what one would expect were the term genuinely context-sensitive. For example, Jason Stanley (2004) argues that, unlike terms like &lsquo;flat&rsquo; and &lsquo;tall&rsquo;, &lsquo;know&rsquo; is not clearly gradable. Thus, it makes sense to describe someone as &ldquo;very tall&rdquo;; but while I might say that someone knows something &ldquo;very well,&rdquo; &lsquo;very&rsquo; does not appear here to be functioning as a degree modifier. And while &lsquo;justified&rsquo; is obviously gradable, even if gradability were sufficient for context-sensitivity, from the fact that knowledge requires justification it would not follow that &lsquo;know&rsquo; is context-sensitive as well ( pace Cohen 1999, 60): it could be that the degree of justifcation required for knowledge is unchanging (Feldman 2001, 67), indeed that it is fixed at the highest degree of justification (Bach 2005, 58, n. 13). Nor, Stanley argues, does &lsquo;know&rsquo; behave like indexicals (&lsquo;I&rsquo;, &lsquo;here&rsquo;) or relational terms (such as &lsquo;enemy&rsquo;). In a related argument, John Hawthorne points out that with uncontroversially context-dependent terms, we naturally employ &ldquo;the clarification technique&rdquo;. An example from the previous Section illustrates the point: I balk at your claim that Kansas is flat, pointing out that there is a small rise just ahead. Rather than admitting to a mistake (&lsquo;concession&rsquo;) or simply repeating your claim (&lsquo;sticking to one&rsquo;s guns&rsquo;), you clarify: &ldquo;Well, what I meant was that there are very few mountains.&rdquo; Hawthorne&rsquo;s point is that we have very few techniques of clarification in the case of &lsquo;know&rsquo;; whereas, it &ldquo;is through the clarification technique that sensitivity to context-dependence is manifested&rdquo; (2004, 104&ndash;106). Finally, Herman Capellen and Ernie Lepore (2003) argue that, according to certain tests for genuine context-sensitivity, &lsquo;know&rsquo; just does not pass muster.

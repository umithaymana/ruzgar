Commenting on Stanley, Barbara Partee (2004) agrees that &lsquo;know&rsquo; is indeed unlike expressions such as &lsquo;tall&rsquo;, but that perhaps better models are available. Nikola Kompa (2002) suggests that the context-sensitivity of &lsquo;know&rsquo; is best understood as deriving from a sui generis sort of &ldquo;unspecificity&rdquo;. And Rob Stainton (2010), while sympathetic to the claim that &lsquo;know&rsquo; is not itself context-sensitive, believes that the &ldquo;spirit&rdquo; of EC can be saved: if there are pragmatic determinants of what is stated/asserted, then what is stated (/etc.) in different uses of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences can vary in truth-conditional content, even if &lsquo;know&rsquo; is not context-sensitive. (Here, there is a connection between EC and its philosophy-of-language namesake, mentioned in Section 2. On one formulation of the latter, as a quite general matter &ldquo;what is said&rdquo; is importantly determined by extra-semantic factors. A similar connection is evident in Charles Travis&rsquo; 2005 paper.) Ceding less ground, Peter Ludlow argues that questions about gradability are too crude a standard by which to judge whether &lsquo;know&rsquo; is context-sensitive. Ludlow disagrees with Hawthorne about the prevalence of clarificatory devices for &lsquo;know&rsquo; and argues that there is good reason to think its semantics includes placeholder(s) for variable standards. Like DeRose (2005), Ludlow casts EC as a piece of &lsquo;ordinary language&rsquo; philosophy, and in that spirit he presents the results of Google searches in which clauses like, &ldquo;&hellip;by objective standards&rdquo;, &ldquo;&hellip;with some certainty&rdquo;, &ldquo;&hellip;doggone well&hellip;&rdquo;, and so on, accompany uses of &lsquo;know&rsquo;. The latter are more data to be mulled over in considering possible linguistic bases for EC. In general, from the fact that different standards are employed, even explicitly adverted to, in making some evaluative judgments in different domains, it does not immediately follow that a contextualist semantics for the relevant terms is correct. Some standards that we employ in making a statement, for example, are understood to be merely guidelines or rules of thumb, not direct applications of the statement&rsquo;s truth conditions (Conee 2014, 64). Questions about the proper handling of such data as Ludlow cites&mdash;e.g., whether the relevant utterances involve the making-explicit of context-variable standards for &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;, or the conveying of information over and above that encoded in the word itself&mdash;arise as much here as with the considerations initially used to motivate EC. 4.5 Further Issues&mdash;Epistemic Modals, Thought, Preservation and Transfer of Information, Practical Reasoning, Attitude Reports Questions have been raised about EC&rsquo;s ability to account for certain other data. Andy Egan et al . (2005) argue that Relativism, rather than EC, gets the semantics of epistemic modals right. According to Relativism (see Section 6), the truth values of knowledge sentences depend upon the standards in play in the contexts in which they are assessed, as opposed to the standards operative in either the subject&rsquo;s or the attributor&rsquo;s context. Bach (2005, 66) and Feldman (2004, 27) question whether the contextualist model might apply to one&rsquo;s thoughts about whether various &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences express truths. Timothy Williamson (2005a, 100&ndash;101) and John Hawthorne (2004, 109&ndash;110) raise related concerns about the preservation of information in memory and testimony: storing or conveying the information that &lsquo; S knows that p [plus some date index]&rsquo; may lead to problems, since subsequent uses of that information may occur in contexts in which different standards are in effect. In addition, Williamson ( ibid ., 102ff.) argues that EC, because it privileges attributor over subject factors, does not respect the autonomy of the subject, qua agent, in settling the contents of questions involved in practical decision making. And Hawthorne ( ibid ., 98ff.) contends that EC has implausible consequences for reports of propositional attitudes in which &lsquo;know&rsquo; occurs. Whether such criticisms are effective against EC is controversial. (See DeRose 1995, 6&ndash;7 and Rieber 1998, 197, e.g., on the extension of EC to one&rsquo;s thoughts; DeRose 2006, Section 4; 2009, 161&ndash;166; and Cohen 2005, 201&ndash;206 reply to Hawthorne&rsquo;s arguments concerning belief reports.) However, whatever we make of these issues, not to mention the ones earlier raised, they could give us reason to reject EC only if there were some viable alternative explanation of the relevant data&mdash;in particular, of the apparently inconsistent &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; claims described in Section 3. Whereas, if we could explain those data without introducing a novel view of the semantics of &lsquo;know&rsquo;, that would considerably weaken the case for EC. 4.6 Some Proposed Invariantist Treatments of the Data Among critics of EC, at least three such purported explanations have emerged. [ 20 ] Since each is intended to preserve the thought that we do ordinarily know many things, the granting of knowledge in the relevant &lsquo;low standards&rsquo; case is typically taken to express a truth. What needs explaining, then, is why denying knowledge to the same subject can seem correct once the standards are raised, even though none of what would normally be regarded as epistemically relevant features have changed. Though they are not obviously competing, each attempt to explain this in non-contextualist terms focuses on different factors. Framed in terms of the airport example described in Section 3.4 above, and in broad outline only, these sample non-contextualist proposals are as follows: 4.6.1 Pragmatic Factors According to some (e.g., Blaauw 2003, Black, 2005, Brown 2006, Hazlitt 2007, Prades 2000, Pritchard 2010, Pynn 2015, Rysiew 2001, 2005, 2007), [ 21 ] pragmatic factors explain the relevant knowledge-attributing behavior. In the airport case, it is mutually obvious to John and Mary that they want to ensure that their epistemic position with respect to the flight plan is very strong&mdash;strong enough to rule out the possibility of a misprint, e.g. Being in an epistemic position of such strength may or may not be required for knowing. Either way, however, &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo; entails that S is in a good epistemic position&mdash;this is why granting someone knowledge involves representing them as entitled to their belief. But it would be odd of Mary and John to grant Smith such an entitlement (by saying &ldquo;he knows&rdquo;) and represent him as being in a good epistemic position if they thought that his evidence wasn&rsquo;t so good as to put their concerns to rest. Whereas, by denying knowledge to Smith, they are able to express the thought, which seems not just relevant but true, that his epistemic position is not so good that they do not need to check further. And if they read what is conveyed by the relevant utterance onto the sentence uttered, the knowledge denial will strike them as expressing a truth. Meanwhile, others have suggested that pragmatic factors such as hyperbole and loose use coupled with stricter invarantist requirements on knowing can explain the type of data that inspire contextualism while not simply ceding victory to the skeptic (e.g., Schaffer 2004a, Davis 2007). 4.6.2 Psychological Presuppositions of Attributing Knowledge Several philosophers have suggested an essentially psychological explanation of why we would deny that the subject in the high standards case knows. Here is one such proposal: Mary does not say &lsquo;Smith knows that the plane will stop in Chicago&rsquo; and goes so far as to assert its negation because of her own doxastic situation. Because she is not sure Smith&rsquo;s itinerary is reliable, she herself is not confident enough to believe that the plane will stop in Chicago. So she cannot coherently attribute knowledge of it to Smith, not if knowledge implies truth.&hellip;Not only that, she has to deny that she knows it, since she thinks that it is not yet established. And, since Smith has no evidence that she doesn&rsquo;t have, she must deny that he knows it [too]. (Bach 2005, 76&ndash;77) In a similar spirit, Adler (2006) suggests that such cases are best explained in terms of the subject&rsquo;s diminished confidence as to p , where the latter does not imply a lack of belief. Meanwhile, Nagel (2008) notes that, when a person has much riding on some matter, we normally expect them to engage in more (/more diligent) evidence-seeking behavior before arriving at confident belief. For this reason, we naturally attribute to the subject in the high standards case either a lack of solid belief or compromised accuracy due to such factors as time pressure or haste, and either of these attributions can lead to a judgment that the subject does not know. Further, even in cases where the subject does not share the evaluator&rsquo;s concerns about certain possibilities of error, epistemic egocentrism &mdash; our natural tendency to overestimate the extent to which others share our beliefs and attitudes &mdash; can lead the evaluator to project those concerns onto the subject. If the subject is not then engaging in the expected extra evidence-seeking behavior, she will once again be judged hasty, and as not knowing (Nagel 2010a,b). 4.6.3 Salience, Conflicting Arguments, and Focusing Effects When we find people seeming to disagree about some matter, that is often because there are considerations on either side of the issue, none of which can be easily dismissed.

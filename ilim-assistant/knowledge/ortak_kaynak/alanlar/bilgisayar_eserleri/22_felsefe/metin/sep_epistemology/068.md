Another version of EC that features explanatory relations and claims is John Greco&rsquo;s (2003, 2008, 2010). At the heart of Greco&rsquo;s view is a certain picture of knowledge&mdash;that it is creditable true belief; when one knows, one believes the truth as a result of one&rsquo;s own efforts and abilities. But when is the attainment of a true belief genuinely creditable to the agent? It is here that contextual matters enter in. For, in general, there can be different&mdash;though, importantly, non-competing &mdash;accounts of &ldquo;the cause&rdquo; of a given thing, event or phenomenon. And now we have an argument for EC: attributing &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; involves giving credit; credit attributions involve a kind of causal explanation; but causal explanations require a contextualist semantics; so &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; attributions require a contextualist semantics too (2010, 106). As the discussion of this section reveals, there is plenty of room for variety among forms of EC&mdash;both about, in Jonathan Schaffer&rsquo;s (2005, 115) phrase, &ldquo;which epistemic gear the wheels of context turn,&rdquo; and about the exact mechanism or rule governing such shifts. Once again, however, there is general consensus among the relevant theorists that context is to be understood in terms of such things as the interests, purposes, expectations, salient possibilities, and so forth, of the attributor. Further, there is consensus that, understood along such lines, context affects the truth-conditional content of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences&mdash;and, in fact, that this phenomenon is what underlies and explains the power of skeptical arguments, even though it also reveals why those arguments do not threaten our ordinary claims to &ldquo;know&rdquo;. Of course, as some proponents of EC point out, contextualists are not forced to any view as to whether and/or under what circumstances, exactly, the skeptic does succeed in raising the standards (Cohen 2001, 92&ndash;93; 2014, 71&ndash;72; DeRose 2006, Section 6; 2004a; 2009, Chapter 4). Still, that EC promises to provide a plausible response to skeptical puzzles has been among its primary selling points. So the contextualist stands to lose something if it turns out that the skeptic is only very rarely or never able to raise the standards for a &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;-ascribing sentence to express a truth. For then some other account would be required to explain the apparent power of arguments like SA. 3.4 Everyday Cases While contextualists have claimed that their view promises a novel and appealing resolution of skeptical puzzles like SA, they have also emphasized what they regard as EC&rsquo;s consonance with our ordinary &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;-attributing practices. There too, they say, we find evidence of the same context-sensitivity which skeptical arguments exploit. As Hookway puts the general claim: &ldquo;Whether I can correctly claim knowledge appears to be relative to the purposes underlying the conversations to which I am contributing&rdquo; (1996, 1). For a couple of reasons, it is very important not to overlook the appeal to everyday cases. First, as we will see, a number of philosophers have questioned how effective EC is in its response to skepticism. If they are right, it matters a great deal that the effectiveness of that response is not EC&rsquo;s sole basis. Second, as Keith DeRose says, &ldquo;the contextualist&rsquo;s appeal to varying standards for knowledge in his solution to skepticism would rightly seem unmotivated and ad hoc if we didn&rsquo;t have independent reason from non-philosophical talk to think such shifts in the content of knowledge attributions occur&rdquo; (2002, 169). But in fact, DeRose says, &ldquo;[t]he best grounds for accepting contextualism concerning knowledge attributions come from how knowledge-attributing (and knowledge-denying) sentences are used in ordinary, non-philosophical talk: What ordinary speakers will count as &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; in some non-philosophical contexts they will deny is such in others&rdquo; (2005, 172; 2006, 316; 2009, 47). Likewise, Cohen claims that examples such as the following &ldquo;strongly [suggest] that ascriptions of knowledge are context-sensitive&rdquo; (1999, 59): [ 11 ] Mary and John are at the L.A. airport contemplating taking a certain flight to New York. They want to know whether the flight has a layover in Chicago. They overhear someone ask a passenger Smith if he knows whether the flight stops in Chicago. Smith looks at the flight itinerary he got from the travel agent and respond, &lsquo;Yes I know&mdash;it does stop in Chicago.&rsquo; It turns out that Mary and John have a very important business contact they have to make at the Chicago airport. Mary says, &lsquo;How reliable is that itinerary? It could contain a misprint. They could have changed the schedule at the last minute.&rsquo; Mary and John agree that Smith doesn&rsquo;t really know that the plane will stop in Chicago. They decide to check with the airline agent. ( Ibid ., 58) Once again, contextualists claim that regarding the truth conditions of sentences using &lsquo;know&rsquo; as context-dependent makes best sense of the flexibility in our &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;-attributing behaviour. While, as we have seen, different specific versions of EC are possible, contextualists tend to agree that, in everyday cases, such as that just described, the increased practical importance of the subjects&rsquo; &ldquo;getting it right&rdquo; tends to raise the standards for the truth of a sentence of the form &lsquo; S knows that p &rsquo;. (Keep in mind, though, a point stressed in Section 2: namely, that just what an utterance of such a sentence expresses changes, in the &lsquo;high stakes&rsquo; case, to some more demanding proposition&mdash;we do not have a fixed such proposition, with different standards for its truth applying in the more demanding context.) The result is that the &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; denial (Mary and John&rsquo;s claim) in the high-stakes situation may be true, without affecting the truth of the low stakes claim to &ldquo;know&rdquo; (by Smith himself). What reason is there, though, for adopting this way of resolving the apparent incompatibility between the two &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; utterances? Well, it is important that the low-stakes claim be true, since that preserves our intuitive anti-skepticism: if you cannot know on the basis of ordinary, non-entailing evidence such as what is printed in the flight itinerary, we will have to deny very many of our ordinary claims to &ldquo;know&rdquo; ( Ibid ., 59). But, it is argued, the &lsquo;high stakes&rsquo; denial seems right too&mdash; that is why the relevant (paired) cases constitute a puzzle. And EC allows us to preserve the sense that in such cases each of the two speakers is speaking &lsquo;properly&rsquo;, and &ldquo;the presumption that what is properly said is true&rdquo; (DeRose 2005, 181). Whereas, if we take John and Mary&rsquo;s stricter standard to be too demanding&mdash;if their denial of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; to Smith is false&mdash;then, not only must we reject the feeling that what they are saying is in some sense correct, but it is &ldquo;hard to see how Mary and John should describe their situation&rdquo;: Certainly they are being prudent in refusing to rely on the itinerary. They have a very important meeting in Chicago. Yet if Smith knows on the basis of the itinerary that the flight stops in Chicago, what should they have said? &lsquo;Okay, Smith knows that the flight stops in Chicago, but still, we need to check further.&rsquo; To my ear, it is hard to make sense of that claim. (Cohen 1999, 58&ndash;9) But we can avoid having to make sense of such claims, and having to explain why we mistakenly thought that what Mary and John were saying was correct, if we accept EC: in everyday no less than skeptical contexts, the sentences used by someone in a &lsquo;high-stakes&rsquo; context and by his/her &lsquo;low stakes&rsquo; counterpart can both be true, since they are made in different contexts, and (in some cases anyway [ 12 ] ) the propositions they express are not really conflicting after all. In short, contextualism promises to deliver up a nice symmetry between the flexibility in our (alleged) judgments as to the truth of a given &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; claim/denial, and a parallel plasticity in the truth conditions (hence, the truth values ) of the &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences we are prepared to assert. On the assumption that speakers realize, however tacitly, that what is expressed by an utterance of &lsquo; S knows (/doesn&rsquo;t know) that p &rsquo; is a context-sensitive matter, this explains the aforementioned flexibility in our &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo;-attributing behavior. 3.5 Other Cases, Other Differences&mdash;the Lottery, Gettier, Closure There are some knowledge claims (/denials) which, though they concern everyday matters, can seem to threaten skeptical consequences.

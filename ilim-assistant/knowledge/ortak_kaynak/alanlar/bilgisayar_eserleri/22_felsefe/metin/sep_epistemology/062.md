A belief can be very likely to be true in a way that is completely irrelevant to the beliefs being, or not being, an instance of knowledge. In that case, it wouldn&rsquo;t be &ldquo;sufficiently likely to be true&rdquo; in a way that is at all relevant to any question of justification. Suppose Jack believes Meyer will win the election. Suppose further Jack&rsquo;s belief originates solely in wishful thinking. Finally, suppose Meyer&rsquo;s winning the election is objectively probable because it&rsquo;s a fact that 80% of those who will vote will vote in his favor. So the p that Jack believes is very likely to be true. As a result, it&rsquo;s very likely that Jack&rsquo;s belief is true. But, since Jack&rsquo;s belief is the result of wishful thinking, it wouldn&rsquo;t be justified or an instance of knowledge. What we need is likelihood of truth that arises not by virtue of what the subject believes (the belief&rsquo;s content), but rather by virtue of the way in which the subject holds, or comes to hold, the belief. But then we must find, in a systematic and principled way, a way of specifying the relevant ways of holding, or coming to hold, a belief. This endeavor raises what has been called the &ldquo;generality problem&rdquo;. See Conee and Feldman 1998 [2004]. 29.

Independently of EC, the relation between knowledge and assertion has been the subject of much recent discussion (see Pagin and Marsili 2025, Section 5.1). 5.3 The Social Role of Knowledge Ascriptions Much of the debate surrounding EC has concerned particular uses of &lsquo;know&rsquo; in various contexts &mdash; specifically, their intuitive truth or falsity. Edward Craig, however, has urged that instead of focusing directly on &lsquo;know&rsquo; and its cognates, we should ask &ldquo;what knowledge does for us, what its role in our life might be, and then ask what a concept having that role would be like&rdquo; (1990, 2, emphasis added). In large part thanks to Craig&rsquo;s work, the social role of knowledge ascriptions has recently come in for focused investigation, with some seeing in that study new arguments for EC. According to Craig, &ldquo;the core of the concept of knowledge is an outcome of certain very general facts about the human situation&rdquo; (1990, 10). The most conspicuous such general fact is that we must rely on others as sources of information (1990, 11), which in turn gives rise to the need for some way to pick out good informants. &ldquo;[A]t its most subjective,&rdquo; the need is for an informant who, among other things, is accessible to and recognizable by me here and now, and as likely to be right about p as my concerns require ( ibid. , 84&ndash;85). However, the high degree of purpose-relativity of the &lsquo;subjectivized&rsquo; notion of the good informant makes it ill-suited to play an effective inter-personal role. According to Craig, for there to be useful sharing of information individuals must share a common point of view concerning the character and presence of good informants. Hence the pressure to form an &lsquo;objectivized&rsquo; concept &mdash; one that retains the &lsquo;common core&rsquo; of the notion without tying it to the needs, abilities, and circumstances of specific individuals &ldquo;and so varying with them&rdquo; (1990, 88). Craig&rsquo;s proposal, in brief, is that the concept of the knower is the objectivized notion of a good informant ( ibid. , 90&ndash;91). [ 24 ] A number of writers have suggested that broadly Craigian reflections on the social role of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; attributions provide a novel line of support for EC. Thus, while Craig&rsquo;s process of objectivization was intended to minimize the context-relativity and -variability of judgments as to a potential informant&rsquo;s reliability, Greco (2008, 432&ndash;435; 2010, 119&ndash;122) thinks that we should acknowledge and embrace the latter. Specifically, Greco suggests, the Craigian story &ldquo;speaks in favor of a version of attributor contextualism that allows that the attributor context be sensitive to the interests and purposes operative in the subject&rsquo;s practical environment&rdquo; (2008, 433; 2010, 120). (Similar views have been presented by Hannon 2013 and McKenna 2013, 2014.) David Henderson (2009, 2011) has also argued that, by reflecting on &ldquo;the point or purpose of the concept of knowledge &rdquo;, a form of EC &ldquo;gets a kind of principled motivation&rdquo; (2009, 125). Henderson also claims, however, that his &lsquo;gate-keeping contextualism&rsquo; has the virtue of respecting the attractions and motivations of Invariantism. Like Craig and Greco, Henderson takes a central function of &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; ascriptions to be the certification of agents as good sources of information. Those agents might belong to applied practical or general-purpose source communities. In the former, where &ldquo;the attributor is engaged in gate keeping for a group focused on some practical project&rdquo;, s/he &ldquo;should be contextually invested in such interests&rdquo; (2009, 126); and here, Henderson argues, we find a new rationale for EC. However, when an attributor is gate keeping for a general-source community, where the information may potentially be very widely shared and there is likely to be no &ldquo;simple decisive understanding of just what counts as actionable information&rdquo;, we get the much more Craigian recommendation that to qualify as knowing that p (in a general purpose source community) an agent must be situated with respect to p so as to be able to distinguish p &rsquo;s obtaining from all the alternatives that would be relevant across all the communities that might draw on it, including those for which the stakes are high. (2009, 127) This yields &ldquo;something like&rdquo; (2009, 127) familiar Invariantism, albeit only within certain contexts: The invariantist pull is honored by treating the invariantist&rsquo;s favored semantic standard for knowledge [or, for the correct application of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo;] as being just one, among various, kinds of semantic standards. Each standard is, in context, motivated by gatekeeping purposes. (Henderson and Horgan 2015, 99) As Henderson notes (2009, 130&ndash;131), the envisaged combination of contexualist and invariantist ideas might prove unstable: actual attributors are members of multiple, overlapping communities; information is constantly being shared multilaterally; and so on. Factors such as these are among the considerations cited by Rysiew (2012b) in arguing that its having a traditional invariantist semantics might make &lsquo;know&rsquo; better suited to fulfilling certain central social roles. (For a reply, see Hannon 2015.) Such claims take us back to considerations of whether EC sits well with the transfer and storage of information (see Section 4.5). More generally, they serve to show that the bearing of certain data upon the semantics of &lsquo;know&rsquo; is just as controversial when we&rsquo;re considering the term&rsquo;s (or concept&rsquo;s) social role or function as when we are considering particular uses of it. 6. Views Similar to EC We have seen that the arrival of EC prompted non-contextualist epistemologists to give more attention to such matters as the psychology and pragmatics of knowledge attributions, and various other features of our actual knowledge-attributing practices. EC has also been instrumental in the development of other competing theories. These are said by their proponents to do a better job of accommodating the data which inspire EC. These views are: Contrastivism, of which Jonathan Shaffer (e.g., 2004) is the leading proponent (but see too Karjalainen and Morton 2003). Subject Sensitive Invariantism or Interest Relative Invariantism&mdash;defended, in various forms, by Jeremy Fantl and Matt McGrath (2002, 2007, 2009), John Hawthorne (2004), and Jason Stanley (2005b). Relativism, of the sort defended by John MacFarlane (2005; 2014, Chapter 8). According to Contrastivism, &lsquo;knows&rsquo; denotes a three-place relation, with a contrast variable included among the relata. The identity of the contrasted propositions may be &lsquo;shifty&rsquo; when not explicitly provided. Thus, in the same way that an utterance of &lsquo;Jane prefers vanilla&rsquo; may express a true proposition when the contextually-provided contrast is strawberry but a false proposition when the contrast is chocolate, &lsquo;Jane knows that she has hands&rsquo; may be true when the contrast is her having lost her hands in an accident, but false when the contrast is her being a handless BIV. (Some see contrastivism as a species of EC; for Schaffer on the differences between Contrastivism and canonical versions of EC, see his 2004b.) According to Subject Sensitive Invariantism, whether a subject knows depends upon facts about his/her practical interests (or, depending on the view, what the subject believes about such), especially the degree of practical importance of getting things right. As a rule, on this view, the more that&rsquo;s at stake the harder it is to know. According to Relativism about knowledge, the truth values of &lsquo;knowledge&rsquo; sentences depend upon the standards in play in the contexts in which they are assessed, as opposed to the standards operative in either the context in which they are uttered or the context of the subject. (When the assessor is identical to the subject of the &ldquo;knowledge&rdquo; attribution these standards of course coincide.) The various strengths and weakness of each of these views &mdash; considered both on their own and as compared with each other, with EC, and with traditional (insensitive) Invariantism&mdash;is the subject of much recent debate. And lively discussion of EC itself continues. (See, for example, the papers collected in Ichikawa 2017b.)

Since the most normal worlds wherein you have the same evidence are those in which these aberrations don&rsquo;t occur, and so in which you have indeed lost, your belief that you lost is justified (case \(B)\). Since you&rsquo;re not justified in believing that you will lose the lottery given only that you&rsquo;re very likely to lose, Smith concedes that you&rsquo;re not justified in believing that you won&rsquo;t be able to afford an African safari. However, he suggests, this skepticism doesn&rsquo;t extend to lottery-esque propositions: you are justified in believing, for example, that your car hasn&rsquo;t been stolen, because its being stolen would be abnormal and so call for explanation. So you&rsquo;re justified in believing both that your car is in spot 3b where you parked it and that it wasn&rsquo;t stolen; and similarly for other lottery-esque cases. SPJC is preserved (Smith 2016a, 56&ndash;58). [ 69 ] MPJC is also preserved. You&rsquo;re not justified in believing, of each lottery ticket, that it loses solely on the basis of the probability of its doing so, so MPJC doesn&rsquo;t imply that you&rsquo;re justified in believing that they&rsquo;ll all lose. And if you&rsquo;re justified in believing every sentence in your book then, if one of those is in fact false, some explanation of its being so is called for. But the conjunction of those sentences is only false if one or more of them is false. So the conjunction is only false if an explanation of its being false is called for. So you&rsquo;re justified in believing that the conjunction is true, and so not justified in believing its negation, that at least one of those claims is false (Smith 2016a and 2022; but see Littlejohn and Dutant 2020 and Backes 2026). In general, there&rsquo;s much yet to explore concerning the relationship between closure and recent accounts of justification now on the market.

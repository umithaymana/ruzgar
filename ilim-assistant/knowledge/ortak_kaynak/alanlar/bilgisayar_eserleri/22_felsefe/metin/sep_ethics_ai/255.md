He argued, for example, that even if search engines were to &ldquo;get it wrong&rdquo; in returning the best results for a particular search query, &ldquo;there was an objective fact of the matter to be gotten wrong&rdquo; (Hinman 2005: 22). And even though the early search engines ranked their sites in terms of &ldquo;popularity&rdquo;, there was, as Hinman (2005: 22) put it, a &ldquo;technical and objective meaning&rdquo; of popularity. But we also saw that this formula has changed dramatically in the case of Web 2.0-era search engines, where increasingly &ldquo;personalized algorithms&rdquo; tend to tailor search results to fit the profile of the user entering the query. This trend toward the &ldquo;personalizing&rdquo; of algorithms feeds directly into concerns affecting (B) . Even though many sophisticated users might suspect that the lists of returns for their search queries are biased, for any number of possible reasons&mdash;e.g., paid advertising, unfair influence by large corporations, editorial control, and so forth&mdash;many search engine users still tend to assume, perhaps na&iuml;vely, that when any two users enter the exact same search query in a major search engine such as Google, they would receive identical lists of responses. In other words, even if the formula used is skewed, or biased in a way that favors some sites over others, the search algorithm would nonetheless return results based on a formula that is internally consistent, and thus standard or &ldquo;objective&rdquo; in some sense. But, this is no longer the case in an era where formulas based on &ldquo;personalization&rdquo; generate search results tailored to a user&rsquo;s profile. For example, if I enter the term &ldquo;eagles&rdquo; in a search box, the list and the order of returns that I receive will likely depend on the profile that the search engine company has constructed about me. If the company determines that I am interested in biology, for instance, I may be directed to a site sponsored by the Audubon Society. But, if instead, it determines that I am a sports enthusiast living in the Philadelphia area, I may be directed first to the Philadelphia Eagles Web site. On the contrary, if my profile suggests that I like rock/pop music, I may be directed first to the site for the Eagles music group. So there would not appear to be any overall &ldquo;objective&rdquo; formula used by the search engine in question. Some question whether a lack of objectivity with respect to the results returned to users in response to their search queries is necessarily a problem. For example, Blanke (2005: 34) suggested that we should neither demand nor expect to receive information from search engines that is objective (i.e., information that is either neutral or complete). Thus, he argued that any critique of search engines as being biased &ldquo;misses its target&rdquo; (2005: 35), because one should not expect search engines to deliver only neutral and objective results. His rationale for this claim, however, seems to be based on the view that search engine technology &ldquo;is not designed to do so&rdquo; (2005: 34). But this analysis seems to beg the question, unless, of course, Blanke means that search engine technology could not, in principle, be designed to deliver neutral and objective results. Questions concerning objectivity in the context of search engines are also examined by E. Goldman (2008), who seems to defend&mdash;and, at times, perhaps even applaud&mdash;search engine bias in that respect. First, he noted that search engine companies &ldquo;make editorial judgments about what data to collect and how to present that data&rdquo; (2008: 122). However, he also held that search engine bias is &ldquo;necessary and desirable&rdquo;&mdash;i.e., it is &ldquo;the unavoidable consequence of search engines exercising editorial control over their databases&rdquo; (2008: 127). So he appears willing to concede that search engine companies, like other media companies, skew results. But while many assume that search engine bias is undesirable, Goldman sees it as a &ldquo;beneficial consequence of search engines optimizing content for their users&rdquo;. He also argued that &ldquo;the &lsquo;winner takes all&rsquo; effect caused by top placement in search results will be mooted by emerging personalized search technology&rdquo; (2008: 121). He further suggested that &ldquo;personalized ranking algorithms&rdquo; will reduce the effect of search engine bias because there will likely be &ldquo;multiple &lsquo;top&rsquo; search results of a particular search term instead of a single &lsquo;winner&rdquo; and that personalized algorithms will &ldquo;eliminate many of the current concerns about search engine bias&rdquo; (2008: 130). Thus Goldman seems to suggest, paradoxically perhaps, that any problems affecting objectivity will be solved by increased subjectivity in the form of personalization of results achieved by personalized search algorithms. However, this direction in search engine evolution can have serious negative effects for democracy and democratic ideals (discussed further in Section 3.4 ). Concerns affecting objectivity and bias in the context of search engines are also closely related to controversies pertaining to the lack of &ldquo;openness&rdquo; or &ldquo;transparency&rdquo; (see, for example, Noble 2018). Some critics point out that search engine companies are not fully open, or transparent, both with respect to why they (a) include some sites and not others (in their lists of results for users&rsquo; queries), and (b) rank some pages in their list of search results higher than others. These kinds of opacity/non-transparency-related concerns tend to fall under a description that Hinman (2005) called &ldquo;the problem of algorithm&rdquo;. Hinman noted that the algorithms that govern searches are well-kept secrets, which he also believes is appropriate. Because Google&rsquo;s PageRank algorithm is &ldquo;a patented and closely guarded piece of intellectual property&rdquo; (Halpern 2011: 4), we don&rsquo;t know the algorithm&rsquo;s formulas. And this factor, of course, makes it difficult to comment on an algorithm&rsquo;s objectivity or lack thereof. Another set of worries affecting opacity/non-transparency arise because search engine companies do not always disclose, either fully or clearly, their practices with respect to two important points: (i) whether (and to what extent) they collect information about users; and (ii) what they do with that information once it has been collected. These kinds of opacity/non-transparency concerns involving search engine companies, which are also related to privacy issues affecting monitoring and surveillance, are examined in detail in Section 3.3 . 3.2 Privacy, Consent, and Non-voluntary Disclosure of Personal Information At least two distinct kinds of privacy concerns arise in the context of search engines. One set of privacy issues emerges because search engine companies can collect personal information about search engine users; in this scheme, the users are, in effect, &ldquo;data subjects&rdquo; for the search engine companies and their advertisers. However, search engine users themselves&mdash;whether acting on their own behalf or on behalf of organizations that hire them&mdash;can use the technology to conduct online searches about people. In this case, the targeted people (some of whom may never have used or possibly have never even heard of a search engine) are the subjects of search engine users. In both cases, privacy concerns arise in connection with questions about fairness for the data subjects involved. Consider that many of those who become the subjects of the search queries have not explicitly consented either to having certain kinds of personal information about them collected or having personal information about them (that has been collected in some other context) also being made available on the Web, or both. In this section, we examine search-engine-related privacy concerns affecting people who have become the subjects, or &ldquo;targets&rdquo;, of queries by search engine users. This kind of privacy concern is exacerbated by the ever expanding amount of personal information about ordinary people that is currently discoverable by search engines and thus accessible to Internet users. But why, exactly, is this problematic from the vantage point of privacy? For one thing, it is not clear that most people have voluntarily consented to having information about them placed in databases or in online forums that are accessible to search engines (and thus potentially available to any Internet user). And we noted that search engine users, whether they are acting simply on their own, or as representatives of business and corporations, can and often do access a wealth of information about many of us via search engines. Privacy advocates question whether this practice is fair, especially to people who have not explicitly consented to having personal information about them included in the online forums and databases that are now so easily searchable because of sophisticated search engine technology. Privacy concerns that arise in contexts in which people are the subjects of search queries can be further differentiated in terms of two separate categories, i.e., where search engines are used to: (i) track the location of individuals, sometimes for the purpose of harassing or stalking them; and (ii) acquire personal information about people.

Such errors led to public criticism and concerns over the reliability and bias of AI-generated content, and highlights the ongoing challenges and ethical considerations in developing and deploying AI technologies by search engine platforms. AI-enhanced search engines often rely on extensive data collection to personalize results, which exacerbates ethical concerns about how user data is collected, stored, and used. For example, AI-enhanced search products might perfect the generation of highly detailed user profiles by analyzing search history, browsing behavior, location history, and other personal data. This information could be used by an AI system to infer sensitive details about individuals, such as their political affiliations, health conditions, or financial status. For instance, if a user frequently searches for information about certain medical conditions, an AI-enhanced search engine could potentially identify this as a health concern and use this information to target ads or even share data with third parties without explicit consent. Further, as with other AI platforms, AI-enhanced search engines face significant privacy concerns related to the collection, storage, and use of personal data for training their models. These concerns include unconsented data harvesting of personal information that happened to be available online, risks of re-identification and unwanted information disclosures, and data breaches (King &amp; Meinhardt 2024). Finally, AI-enhanced search engines have sparked new concerns over the accuracy and reliably of the information provided to users. AI models, such as Google&rsquo;s Gemini or Microsoft&rsquo;s Copilot in Bing, generate search results based on patterns learned from vast datasets. However, these datasets can include biased, outdated, or incorrect information, leading to inaccurate search results. Google&rsquo;s &ldquo;AI Overview&rdquo; feature, for example, failed to differentiate between factual and sarcastic content, leading to incorrect search results that suggests users should use glue to get cheese to stick to pizza or drink urine to help pass kidney stones quickly (Cerullo 2024). In others cases, AI systems might generate completely false information&mdash;often referred to as &ldquo;hallucinations&rdquo;&mdash;which can be particularly problematic in contexts where users rely on search engines for factual information, such as health advice or news. The tendency to generate incorrect or nonsensical answers, combined with the opacity of AI algorithms, further erodes trust in the accuracy and reliability of AI-enhanced search engine results (Heikkil&auml; 2023). 5. Conclusion In this entry, we have seen how various ethical issues arose during key developmental stages of search technology, from the early Web, to the &ldquo;Web 2.0-era&rdquo;, and presenting into the AI-enhanced environment. Search technology itself has evolved over the past 80 years&mdash;i.e., from pre-computer-based techniques (such as memex) intended to help scientists and professional researchers locate and retrieve important information in a timely manner, to an Internet-based technology that assisted ordinary users in locating (and linking directly to) relevant Web sites in response to their manifold search queries, to a highly sophisticated technology that, in its current form, has become increasingly commodified and personalized to the point that it might now be regarded as a threat to some of our basic freedoms and democratic ideals. The ethical issues examined in this entry are largely from a deontological perspective. However, in no way is our analysis of ethical issues affecting search engines intended to be exhaustive; rather it merely reflects the standard or &ldquo;mainstream&rdquo; approach that applied ethicists and other scholars have taken thus far in their analyses of search engine controversies. One could easily imagine questions arising from other ethical perspectives as well. From the vantage point of social justice, for example, one could reasonably ask whether search engine companies in the U.S. and other developed nations are morally obligated to help bridge the &ldquo;information divide&rdquo; by providing easier and more ubiquitous access (via search technologies) to users in developing nations, especially to non-English speaking users. Also, from a utilitarian or consequentialist perspective, one might ask whether the overall social consequences would be more beneficial if search engine users were legally permitted to retrieve some forms of proprietary information (e.g., proprietary online information concerning health and public-health studies that were made possible by tax-payer funding) for personal use. Additionally, some alternative ethical frameworks could also be used in analyzing ethical issues affecting search engines. These include, but are not limited to, Hans Jonas&rsquo; &ldquo;Imperative of Responsibility&rdquo; (Jonas 1984), Bernard Gert&rsquo;s &ldquo;Common Morality&rdquo; (Gert 2004), and John Rawls&rsquo; &ldquo;justice as fairness&rdquo; (Rawls 1999). For a very brief description of how ethical aspects of search engines might also be approached from the perspectives of &ldquo;rights&rdquo;, &ldquo;fairness&rdquo;, &ldquo;common good&rdquo; and &ldquo;virtue&rdquo;, see the Markkula Center for Applied Ethics (in the Other Internet Resources ). Even though the primary focus has been on identifying and analyzing ethical issues affecting search engines, in closing it is worth reiterating a point made in the introductory section&mdash;viz., that search engine technology also has implications for some other kinds of philosophical issues. This is especially apparent in the area of epistemology, where some critics raise concerns related to our received notions of the nature and justification of knowledge claims, in an era of widespread use and dependence on search engines. For example, Hinman (2008: 75) argues that search engines &ldquo;contribute significantly to the social construction of knowledge&rdquo;&mdash;i.e., not only do they provide access to knowledge, but they also increasingly &ldquo;play a crucial role in the constitution of knowledge itself&rdquo;. An analysis of this claim, however, as well as other epistemological and (broader) philosophical aspects of search engines, is beyond the scope of the present entry.

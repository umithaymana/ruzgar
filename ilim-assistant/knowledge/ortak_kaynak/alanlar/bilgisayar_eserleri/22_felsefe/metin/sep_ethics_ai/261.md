We begin with an analysis of (i). 4.2 Legal Liability, a &ldquo;Right to Erasure&rdquo;, and Fake News in the Digital Era Should SECs be held accountable for directing users, even if unintentionally, to some Web sites whose content is either illegal or morally controversial, or both? We should note that American SECs, including Google, claim that they provide only a service , and thus are not &ldquo;content providers&rdquo;. These companies believe that it is not reasonable to hold them responsible/liable for the content on sites that they merely identify or provide links to in their lists of search returns. They also correctly point out that under Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act in the U.S. (47 USC &sect; 230), providers of online services are not held legally liable for the online content accessible through their services, provided they comply with official legal requests to remove content that explicitly violate U.S. laws. Analogously, SECs believe that if they comply with official legal requests to &ldquo;de-index&rdquo;, or remove links to, sites that willingly and intentionally violate U.S. laws&mdash;e.g., laws violating copyright, child pornography, and so forth&mdash;they too should not be held legally liable for any content that users happen to access via their services. However, many American-owned SECs operate internationally, where laws and regulatory schemes differ from those in the U.S. (as we saw in the case of Google in China). It is worth noting that in Europe, SECs are viewed as &ldquo;controllers of personal data&rdquo;, as opposed to mere providers of a service (see, for example, Google Spain SL, Google Inc. 2013); as such, these companies, as well as ISPs that operate in EU countries, can be held responsible/liable for the content that is accessible through their services. One major difference regarding how SECs are viewed in the U.S. versus Europe came to the fore in the debate about whether users should have the &ldquo;Right to Be Forgotten&rdquo;, now commonly referred to as the Right to Erasure. 4.2.1 The Right to Erasure (RtE) What is RtE, and why has it been controversial from the point of view of moral accountability issues for SECs? We can trace the origin of what eventually became the RtE debate to 2010, when Mario Costeja Gonz&aacute;lez requested that Google remove a link included in its list of returns for online searches of his name (see, for example, Google Spain SL, Google Inc. 2014). The link in question was to an article in a Spanish newspaper ( La Vanguardia ) about a home foreclosure that occurred 16 years earlier. Gonz&aacute;lez, a Spanish citizen, petitioned Spain&rsquo;s National Data Protection Agency, to have the link removed. He argued that the information about his foreclosure, which was still prominently displayed in Google&rsquo;s list of search returns for &ldquo;Mario Gonz&aacute;lez&rdquo;, was no longer &ldquo;relevant&rdquo;. Although the Spanish court ruled in Gonz&aacute;lez&rsquo;s favor in 2010, many critics were unsure that this court&rsquo;s ruling would also hold in other EU countries. So, these critics were not surprised when Google appealed the Spanish court&rsquo;s ruling. As the debate in Europe escalated, many RtE advocates argued that the (former) EU Directive on Data Protection (Directive 95/46/EC), which protects the privacy rights citizens of all EU countries, includes language that indirectly supported RtE. For example, they noted that Article 12 (&ldquo;Right of Access&rdquo;) of that directive grants &ldquo;the rectification, erasure, or blocking of data the processing of which does not comply with the provisions of the Directive because the information is&hellip;inaccurate&rdquo; (see the European Commission&rsquo;s &ldquo;Fact Sheet on &lsquo;The Right to Be Forgotten&rsquo; Ruling&rdquo;). So, citizens of EU countries already had an explicit legal right to have inaccurate personal information about them removed/erased from the Internet, or at least de-linked or de-indexed from search engines. Some RtE supporters also believed that Article 12 could be interpreted to imply a right to the erasure/de-linking of online personal information that is no longer deemed to be relevant . SECs and other RtE opponents, including publishers and journalists, have viewed RtE from a very different perspective. They argued that RtE would censor information, thereby limiting freedom of expression, and would degrade the overall quality of the Internet by limiting the amount of online information available to users (see, for example, Tavani 2018). Because of these and related concerns, they concluded that the public would be harmed by RtE. However, some RtE supporters, including Bottis (2014: 2), suggested that RtE-related data protection was needed precisely because it would help to eliminate harm&mdash;viz., &ldquo;psychological harm&rdquo; (and possibly even physical harm in some cases)&mdash;that users might otherwise experience. Consider, for example, that victims of &ldquo;revenge porn&rdquo; sites would have explicit legal recourse regarding their requests to remove/de-index links that associated their names with those controversial sites (see the account of revenge porn vis-&agrave;-vis the Right to Be Forgotten, now RtE, in Kritikos 2018). In 2012, the European Commission (EC) proposed a revised Data-Protection Regulation (Article 17) for the (former) EU Directive, which included specific provisions for the removal (i.e., the &ldquo;rectification, erasure, or blocking&rdquo;) of personal data that was irrelevant (as well as personal data that was &ldquo;inaccurate&rdquo;, &ldquo;inadequate&rdquo;, or &ldquo;excessive&rdquo;). The EU Parliament approved the EC&rsquo;s recommendations in 2013, after making a few minor modifications to Article 17 and renaming the &ldquo;right&rdquo; in question the &ldquo;Right to Erasure&rdquo;. Perhaps not surprisingly, the newly revised RtE regulation was challenged by Google and other corporations operating in Europe. However, in May 2014, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) upheld the Spanish court&rsquo;s (2010) ruling in the Gonz&aacute;lez case, also confirming Article 17 of the updated EU Directive on Data Protection. The high court&rsquo;s ruling confirmed that citizens residing in all EU nations have the right, under certain conditions, to request that search engine operators remove links to some kinds of personal information about them that are &ldquo;no longer relevant&rdquo;. The CJEU also noted, however, that RtE is not an absolute right; for example, this right would have to be balanced against other rights, including &ldquo;freedom of expression&rdquo; (see the EU&rsquo;s &ldquo;Fact Sheet on the &lsquo;Right to Be Forgotten&rsquo; Ruling&rdquo;). Although Google initially announced in that it would comply with the CJEU&rsquo;s ruling, it soon expressed worry that it would not be able to respond to all of the users&rsquo; requests that it would likely receive to remove/de-index links (Scott 2014 [Other Internet Resources]); doing so, it suggested, would not only be an onerous task but might also be a practical impossibility. Kelion (2019) notes that in the time period between the CJEU&rsquo;s May 2014 ruling and September 2019, Google had received &ldquo;more than 845,000 requests to remove a total of 3.3 million web addresses&rdquo;, which resulted in approximately &ldquo;45% of the links ultimately getting delisted&rdquo;. So it would seem that Google and its supporters indeed have a reasonable concern, given the magnitude of removal requests it has received. But one can still reasonably ask, as Bottis has, whether the sheer volume of these requests constitutes sufficient grounds for repealing RtE.

Of course, the relevant question for us to consider is whether the Web 2.0 environment itself either changes or significantly affects the functions of search engines and, more importantly, the ethical issues they generate. It is not clear whether we can accurately label current search engines as &ldquo;Web 2.0 search engines&rdquo;, even though they operate in a Web 2.0 environment. For example, many of the participatory tools and functions that apply to applications such as social networks, blogs, and wikis do not necessarily apply to contemporary search engines. So, it may be more appropriate to use Hinman&rsquo;s phrase &ldquo;second-generation search engines&rdquo;. However, O&rsquo;Reilly (2005) suggested that Google&rsquo;s practice of incorporating user-generated content to provide a &ldquo;better&rdquo; Web search environment for users is compatible with interactive dimensions and objectives of Web 2.0. But despite O&rsquo;Reilly&rsquo;s interpretation of Google&rsquo;s practices, one might still question whether the phrase &ldquo;Web 2.0 search engines&rdquo; is warranted; so, we will instead refer to contemporary (or second-generation) search engines as &ldquo;Web 2.0- era search engines&rdquo;. What, exactly, distinguishes a Web 2.0-era search engine from the earlier ones? Hinman (2005) noted that the traditional criteria Web search engine companies used to rank sites was based on two factors: (1) the number of visits to a page (i.e., &ldquo;popularity&rdquo;), and (2) the &ldquo;number of other pages that link to a given page&rdquo;. With respect to the second criterion, Diaz (2008) and others point to an analogy used in ranking the importance of academic papers. They note, for example, that an academic paper is generally viewed to be important if it is cited by many other papers. And that paper is perhaps viewed as even more important if it is cited by highly cited works. Hinman believes that the shift to (what we call) Web 2.0-era search engines occurred when companies, such as Google, &ldquo;looked more closely at what users wanted to find&rdquo; (which, as he also points out, is not always the most popular site). He notes, for example, that Google&rsquo;s formula employs the following strategy: &ldquo;Users&rsquo; needs &rarr; Search terms &rarr; Desired site&rdquo; (Hinman 2005: 22). He also noted that in this scheme, what the user wants becomes an integral part of the formula, as does the set of search terms commonly used to express what the user wants. During this period of personalization, Zimmer (2008: 77) argued that Google&rsquo;s ultimate goal is to &ldquo;create &lsquo;the perfect search engine&rsquo; that will provide only intuitive, personalized, and relevant results&rdquo;. Halpern (2011) pointed out that the search process has already &ldquo;become personalized&rdquo;&mdash;i.e., &ldquo;instead of being universal, it is idiosyncratic and oddly peremptory&rdquo;. And Pariser (2011), who warned that &ldquo;there is no standard Google anymore&rdquo;, also noted that with &ldquo;personalized search&rdquo;, the result(s) suggested by Google&rsquo;s algorithm is probably the best match for the search query. Some ethical implications affecting the personalization of search algorithms are examined in Section 3.4 . Before concluding this section, we should briefly mention two additional kinds of contemporary search engines. First, DuckDuckGo (an alternative to Google and Bing) which prides itself on not profiling its users, claims that it does not personalize search results. So, this search engine&rsquo;s users will typically get the same search results, as well as the same ranking of results, for a given query. Second, a new type of search engine, capable of &ldquo;discovering&rdquo; objects or &ldquo;things&rdquo; on the Internet is now also available. Two examples of this kind of &ldquo;discoverable search engine&rdquo;, Shodan and Thingful, are examined in the final section of this entry. 2.5 The AI-Enhanced Web Era (2020&ndash;present) In the late 2010s, machine learning and artificial intelligence efforts saw rapid advancements, driven largely by breakthroughs in deep learning, improved computational power, and the availability of vast amounts of data. This led to significant improvements in various domains, including natural language processing (NLP), image recognition, and generative systems. Search engines leveraged these new technologies to enhance efficiency, functionality, and user experiences. For example, in 2019, Google began incorporating BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), a novel natural language processing (NLP) model, into its search engine architecture (Nayak 2019 [ Other Internet Resources ]). BERT was designed to improve search results by enabling the search engine to better understand the nuances and context of search queries, ideally leading to more relevant and accurate responses. Microsoft similarly incorporated advanced AI and NLP features into its search products (Baer 2019 [Other Internet Resources]). By 2021, Google released the Multitask Unified Model (MUM), a new AI model 1000-times more powerful than BERT that could handle increasingly complex search queries, understand information across different formats (text, images, video), and utilize knowledge across 75 different languages simulaneously (Nayak 2021 [Other Internet Resources]). Advances in NLP have also increased the usefulness of AI-driven voice assistants like Apple&rsquo;s Siri, Google Assistant, and Amazon&rsquo;s Alexa. Such developements have helped provide an improved understanding of spoken language, dialects and colloquialisms, and also enabling back-and-forth interactions where users can ask follow-up questions and progressively refine their search query (Podesta 2024). Search engines have also increasingly integrated machine learning to enhance image recognition capabilities, leveraging various techniques and advancements to improve the accuracy and efficiency of visual search and image labeling. And since the rapid emergence of user-friendly artificial intelligence systems designed to create new content based on user prompts&mdash;such as ChatGPT or text generation or DALL-E for image generation (Chui et al. 2023 [ Other Internet Resources ])&mdash;search engine providers have been quick to incorporate these new technologies to enhance their capabilities and user experience. Microsoft quickly moved to integrate ChatGPT-like technology from OpenAI into Bing, transforming the search engine into a conversational AI platform that enhances user interaction and information retrieval (Mehdi 2023). Google soon followed with its custom Gemini model to power AI-driven search overviews, providing users with AI-generated overviews to certain queries with the option for users to explore more natively within the search results (Reid 2024). Some ethical aspects of AI-enhanced search are examined in the Section 4.4 . 3. Ethical Implications and Core Ethical Issues Most Internet users are well aware of the virtues of search engines. As we noted earlier, many of us now depend on them to direct us to information that affects nearly all facets of our day-to-day lives&mdash;information about work, travel, recreation, entertainment, finances, politics, news, sports, music, education, and so forth. However, as we also noted earlier, the use of search engines has generated a cluster of ethical concerns (Nagenborg 2005). In Section 3, we organize these concerns into five broad categories: (i) search-engine bias and the problem of opacity/non-transparency, (ii) personal privacy and informed consent, (iii) monitoring and surveillance, (iv) censorship and democracy, (v) (cyber)security issues, &ldquo;discoverable search engines&rdquo;, and the Internet of Things. A different, but also related, cluster of ethical issues &mdash; viz, questions concerning moral accountability and social responsibilities for search engine companies &mdash; are examined in Section 4 . 3.1 Search Engine Bias and the Problem of Opacity/Nontransparency What is search-engine bias, and why is it controversial? In reviewing the literature on this topic, it would seem that the phrase &ldquo;search-engine bias&rdquo; has been used to describe at least three distinct, albeit sometimes overlapping, concerns: (1) search-engine technology is not neutral, but instead has embedded features in its design that favor some values over others; (2) major search engines systematically favor some sites (and some kinds of sites) over others in the lists of results they return in response to user search queries; and (3) search algorithms do not use objective criteria in generating their lists of results for search queries. 3.1.1 The Non-Neutrality of Search Engines While some users may assume that search engines are &ldquo;neutral&rdquo; or value-free, critics argue that search engine technology, as well as computer technology in general, is value-laden and thus biased because of the kinds of features typically included in their design.

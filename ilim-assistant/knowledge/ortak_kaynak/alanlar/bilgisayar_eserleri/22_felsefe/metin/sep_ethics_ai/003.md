The result is that &ldquo;In this vast ocean of data, there is a frighteningly complete picture of us&rdquo; (Smolan 2016, 1:01) &hellip; a remark that already seems trivial now. The data trail we leave behind is how our &ldquo;free&rdquo; services are paid for &ndash; but we are not told about that data collection and the value of this new raw material, and we are manipulated into leaving ever more such data. For the &ldquo;big 5&rdquo; companies (Amazon, Google/Alphabet, Microsoft, Apple, Facebook/Meta), the main data-collection part of their business appears to be based on deception, exploiting human weaknesses, furthering procrastination, generating addiction, and manipulation (Harris 2016; Klenk and Jongepier 2022). The primary focus of social media, gaming, and most of the Internet in this &ldquo;surveillance economy&rdquo; is to gain, maintain and direct attention &ndash; and thus continued data supply. As Schneier said, &ldquo;surveillance is the business model of the Internet&rdquo; (Schneier 2015); this is sometimes captured in the catchword &ldquo;surveillance capitalism&rdquo; (Williams 2018; Zuboff 2019; K&ouml;nigs 2024). 2.1.2 Losing Control of Data? One useful perspective on privacy from the &ldquo;control&rdquo; angle is to see it as the demand that information integrity is preserved in the relevant contexts, i.e. &ldquo;contextual integrity&rdquo; demands that the flow of information is appropriately controlled (Nissenbaum 2004). The problem of AI generated images and videos of actual people may suit this approach. It has been argued that a loss of &ldquo;freedom&rdquo; is the characteristic feature of the AI era (Santoni de Sio 2024). This loss has caused many attempts to escape from the grasp of surveillance capitalism, e.g. in exercises of &ldquo;minimalism&rdquo; (Newport 2019), or through the open-source movement &ndash; but it is doubtful that present-day citizens have the autonomy needed. Surveillance systems will often reveal facts about us that we ourselves wish to suppress or are not aware of: they know more about us than we know ourselves. Even just observing online behaviour allows insights into our mental states (Burr and Christianini 2019) and manipulation (see below (2.1.2)). This has led to calls for the protection of &ldquo;derived data&rdquo; (Wachter and Mittelstadt 2019). With the last sentence of his bestselling book Homo Deus (Harari 2016) asks about the long-term consequences of AI: &ldquo;What will happen to society, politics and daily life when non-conscious but highly intelligent algorithms know us better than we know ourselves?&rdquo; (never mind whether an algorithm can &ldquo;know&rdquo;). Robotic systems are beginning to play a major role in this area, esp. from the air. Together with the &ldquo;Internet of things&rdquo;, the &ldquo;smart&rdquo; systems (phone, TV, oven, lamp, virtual assistant, home, &hellip;), the &ldquo;smart city&rdquo; (Sennett 2018), &ldquo;smart governance&rdquo;, and &ldquo;smart agentic AI&rdquo;, they are set to become part of the data-gathering system that offers more detailed data in real time, with ever more information. There is an &ldquo;arms race&rdquo; between surveillance and individual freedom and there is an important societal question where a good balance lies. Both &ldquo;sides&rdquo; use information technology, e.g. privacy can be protected by encryption, anonymity, cryptocurrency. For some research purposes, there are privacy-preserving techniques that can largely conceal the identity of persons or groups, e.g. &ldquo;differential privacy&rdquo; by adding calibrated noise to encrypt the output of queries (Dwork et al. 2006; Garfinkel 2025). While some companies sell surveillance, others sell protection from surveillance, e.g. in operation systems for computers and phones. One of the major practical difficulties is to actually enforce regulation, both on the level of the state and on the level of the individual who has a claim. They must identify the responsible legal entity, prove the action, perhaps prove intent, find a court that declares itself competent &hellip; and eventually get the court to actually enforce its decision. Well-established legal protection of rights such as consumer rights, product liability and other civil liability or protection of intellectual property rights is often missing in digital products, or hard to enforce. This means that companies with a &ldquo;digital&rdquo; background are used to testing their products on the consumers, without fear of liability, while defending their intellectual property rights. This &ldquo;Internet Libertarianism&rdquo; is sometimes taken to assume that technical solutions will take care of societal problems by themselves (Mozorov 2013). 2.2 Human Autonomy &amp; Manipulation The ethical issues of AI in surveillance go beyond the mere accumulation of data, control of data flow and direction of attention : They include the use of information to manipulate behaviour, online and offline. Assuming choice has the standard &ldquo;control condition&rdquo; and &ldquo;epistemic condition&rdquo;, manipulation can use both. Human action selection is often quite far from cool rational choice, so our tendencies to procrastination and other sub-optimal behaviour, which can be exploited for manipulation. While efforts to manipulate behaviour are as old as humanity (Noggle 2022), they gain a new quality with AI systems (Prunkl 2024; Schneider 2025). Jaron Lanier already wrote in 2014: &ldquo;When you are wearing sensors on your body all the time, such as the GPS and camera on your smartphone and constantly piping data to a megacomputer owned by a corporation that is paid by &rdquo;advertisers&ldquo; to subtly manipulate you ... you gradually become less free.&rdquo; (Lanier 2014). With sufficient prior data, algorithms can be used to target individuals or small groups with just the kind of personalised input that is likely to influence these particular individuals. Given users&rsquo; intense interaction with data systems and the deep knowledge about individuals this provides, they are vulnerable to positive &ldquo;nudges&rdquo; (Thaler and Sunstein 2008), emotional manipulation, as well as negative manipulation and deception. Profit-oriented businesses exploit behavioural biases, deception, and the generation of addiction (Costa and Halpern 2019) &ndash; e.g. through &ldquo;dark patterns&rdquo; on web pages or in games (Mathur et al. 2019; Luguri and Strahilevitz 2021; Klenk 2022). While offline gambling and the sale of addictive substances are highly regulated, online manipulation and addiction is not. Control is increased by system lock-in and software monopolies. One detail of this loss of control is the absence of &ldquo;informed consent&rdquo; (Faden and Beauchamp 1986) when agreeing to &ldquo;terms and conditions&rdquo; of software and IT services. The culmination of this problem is reached when decisions are handed over to an AI agent, and the human does not need to be manipulated any more. Improved AI &ldquo;faking&rdquo; technologies make what once was reliable evidence into unreliable evidence &ndash; this has already happened to digital photos, sound recordings and video &hellip; and it is now quite easy to create (rather than alter) &ldquo;deep fake&rdquo; text, photos and video material with any content desired. Sophisticated real-time interaction with persons over texting, phone or video can be faked, too. So, we cannot trust digital interaction, while we are at the same time increasingly dependent on such interaction. It has been argued that technological manipulation poses significant threats to our opportunities to live meaningful lives (Nyholm 2022) and that the basis of our epistemic practices (see the next section) for establishing belief and knowledge is threatened by this development (Rini 2020; Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025). 2.3 Epistemic Issues: Opacity &amp; Explainability When an AI system makes a decision, e.g. &ldquo;you are denied a credit card&rdquo;, it will often be impossible for the affected person to know how the system came to this output, i.e. the system is &ldquo;opaque&rdquo; or &ldquo;black box&rdquo; to that person. Furthermore, many AI systems rely on machine learning techniques in (simulated) neural networks that will extract patterns from a given dataset, with or without &ldquo;correct&rdquo; solutions provided; i.e. supervised, semi-supervised or unsupervised. With these techniques, the &ldquo;learning&rdquo; captures patterns in the data and these are labelled in a way that appears useful to the decision the system makes, while the programmer does not really know which patterns in the data the system has used. What this means is that the outcome is opaque to the expert programmers, too &ndash; this is standard opacity (Dur&aacute;n and Jongsma 2021). Sometimes, there is perhaps even &ldquo;deep&rdquo; or &ldquo;essential&rdquo; opacity that cannot be removed, in principle (Humphreys 2009; M&uuml;ller 2025a; Beisbart 2026). It is well known that epistemic conditions have an impact on normative issues. In our case, if there is opacity, then any bias in decision-making will be hard to detect. Opacity and bias are central issues in what is now sometimes called &ldquo;data ethics&rdquo;, &ldquo;big data ethics&rdquo; or &ldquo;ethics of algorithms&rdquo; (Floridi and Taddeo 2016; Mittelstadt et al. 2016). AI systems for automated decision support and &ldquo;predictive analytics&rdquo; raise &ldquo;significant concerns about lack of due process, accountability, community engagement, and auditing&rdquo; (Danaher 2016b; Whittaker et al. 2018, 18ff).

Knowledge as Justified True Belief Much of the 20 th century literature on the analysis of knowledge began with a critique of a three-part &ldquo;traditional&rdquo; analysis of knowledge. Although this analysis does derive from ideas articulated throughout the history of epistemology, it&rsquo;s a bit of a convenient fiction to say that this so-called &ldquo;traditional&rdquo; analysis was ever widely accepted; it was primarily articulated in the 20 th century by its attackers. See Kaplan 1985, Dutant 2015, Antognazza 2015, and Le Morvan 2017 on the mismatch between the canonical story and the actual intellectual history. There are three components to the traditional analysis of knowledge. (For this reason, it is sometimes referred to as a &ldquo;tripartite&rdquo; analysis of knowledge.) According to this analysis, justified true belief is necessary and sufficient for knowledge. The Tripartite Analysis of Knowledge: S knows that P if and only if: It is true that P ; S believes that P ; and S is justified in believing that P . The tripartite analysis of knowledge is often abbreviated as the &ldquo;JTB&rdquo; analysis, for &ldquo;justified true belief&rdquo;. Let&rsquo;s consider the three conditions in turn. 1.1 The Truth Condition Most epistemologists agree that what is false cannot be known. For example, eighty-four is not a prime number. Consequently, it could never be the case that anyone knows that eighty-four is a prime number. One can only know things that are true. For this reason, the traditional theory of knowledge includes, as one of the components for the analysans for S knows that P , the condition that it is true that P . (Sometimes, the explicit invocation of truth is left out, and the relevant condition is listed simply as P . See Nozick (1981: 680, n. 6).) Sometimes, when people are very confident of something that turns out to be wrong, it can be natural to describe them as &ldquo;knowing&rdquo; that false proposition. Suppose, for example, that late in a sports match, the home team is so far behind that many spectators are very confident that they will lose. If they turn around in the final seconds to achieve a miraculous comeback and win the game, some people might be inclined to say that, prior to the comeback, many spectators &ldquo;knew&rdquo; that the home team would lose. Allan Hazlett (2010) argues on the basis of data like this that &ldquo;knows&rdquo; is not a factive verb. [ 2 ] Hazlett&rsquo;s diagnosis is deeply controversial; most epistemologists will treat such knowledge ascriptions as a kind of exaggeration, and not as literally true. The analysis of knowledge is about identifying the circumstances under which someone really does know a given proposition&mdash;not necessarily about the circumstances under which it might be conversationally acceptable to say that they know. Relatedly, the idea that a proposition is true does not require that anyone can know or prove that it is true. Not all truths are established truths. If you flip a coin and never check how it landed, it may be true that it landed heads, even if nobody has any way to tell. Truth is a metaphysical , as opposed to epistemological , notion: truth is a matter of how things are , not how they can be shown to be. So when we say that only true things can be known, we&rsquo;re not (yet) saying anything about how anyone can access the truth. As we&rsquo;ll see, the other conditions have important roles to play here. Knowledge is a kind of relationship with the truth&mdash;to know something is to have a certain kind of access to a fact. 1.2 The Belief Condition The belief condition is only slightly more controversial than the truth condition. The general idea behind the belief condition is that you can only know what you believe. Failing to believe something precludes knowing it. &ldquo;Belief&rdquo; in the context of the JTB theory means full belief, or outright belief. In a weak sense, one might &ldquo;believe&rdquo; something by virtue of being pretty confident that it&rsquo;s probably true&mdash;in this weak sense, someone who considered the home team the favourite to win the game, even while recognizing a nontrivial possibility of their losing, might be said to have believed that the home team would win. Outright belief is stronger. [ 3 ] To believe outright that P , it isn&rsquo;t enough to have a pretty high confidence in P ; it is something closer to a commitment or a being sure, or having settled the question. In the paper that explicitly laid out the &ldquo;justified true belief&rdquo; analysis, Edmund Gettier (1963) actually listed three slightly different tripartite analyses, which differed in how this condition was articulated: one was given in terms of &ldquo;believes&rdquo;, one in terms of &ldquo;accepts&rdquo;, and one in terms of &ldquo;is sure that&rdquo;. Epistemologists have tended to follow Gettier in treating these as effectively equivalent. For further discussion, see McGlynn 2014: 25&ndash;9 and Ichikawa 2017-a: &sect;7.1. Although initially it might seem obvious that knowing that P requires believing that P , a few philosophers have argued that knowledge without belief is indeed possible. Suppose Walter comes home after work to find out that his house has burned down. He says: &ldquo;I don&rsquo;t believe it.&rdquo; Critics of the belief condition might argue that Walter knows that his house has burned down (he sees that it has), but, as his words indicate, he does not believe it. The standard response is that Walter&rsquo;s avowal of disbelief is not literally true; what Walter wishes to convey by saying, &ldquo;I don&rsquo;t believe it&rdquo;, is not that he really does not believe that his house has burned down, but rather that he finds it hard to come to terms with what he sees. If he genuinely didn&rsquo;t believe it, some of his subsequent actions, such as phoning his insurance company, would be rather mysterious. Colin Radford (1966) gave a more substantive critique of the belief condition. Suppose Albert is quizzed on English history. One of the questions is: &ldquo;When did Queen Elizabeth die?&rdquo; Albert doesn&rsquo;t think he knows, but answers the question correctly. Moreover, he gives correct answers to many other questions to which he didn&rsquo;t think he knew the answer. Let us focus on Albert&rsquo;s answer to the question about Elizabeth: (E) Elizabeth died in 1603. Radford makes the following two claims about this example: Albert does not believe (E). Albert knows (E). Radford&rsquo;s intuitions about cases like these do not seem to be idiosyncratic; Blake Myers-Schutz &amp; Eric Schwitzgebel (2013) find evidence suggesting that many ordinary speakers tend to react in the way Radford suggests. In support of (a), Radford emphasizes that Albert thinks he doesn&rsquo;t know the answer to the question. He doesn&rsquo;t trust his answer because he takes it to be a mere guess. In support of (b), Radford argues that Albert&rsquo;s answer is not at all just a lucky guess. The fact that he answers most of the questions correctly indicates that he has actually learned, and never forgotten, such historical facts. Since he takes (a) and (b) to be true, Radford holds that belief is not necessary for knowledge. But both of these intuitions are controversial. Some epistemologists might deny (a), arguing that Albert does have a tacit belief that (E), even though it&rsquo;s not one that he thinks amounts to knowledge. See e.g., David Rose &amp; Jonathan Schaffer (2013). Alternatively, one might deny (b), arguing that Albert&rsquo;s correct answer is not an expression of knowledge, perhaps because it is insufficiently confident. See Nagel 2010 and Ichikawa 2024 on ways in which uncertainty can undermine knowledge by undermining belief. Maria Antognazza (2020) offers quite a different kind of challenge to the belief condition on knowledge: she rejects the orthodox philosophical understanding of belief, according to which it is a kind of doxastic commitment that does not depend on whether or not it amounts to an apprehension of reality, holding instead that belief, by its nature, is a cognitive representation supported only indirectly by experience. Belief, according to Antognazza, is fundamentally unlike knowledge, in that by definition, one can only believe that which is not directly apprehended. This isn&rsquo;t merely a denial that belief is a necessary condition for knowledge; on her view, believing P is actually incompatible with knowing P . This is not a mainstream or widely held approach to belief in contemporary epistemology, but Antognazza argues that it is supported by considerations in the philosophy of mind, and representative of important historical epistemological traditions. 1.3 The &ldquo;Justification&rdquo; Condition Why is a third condition necessary?

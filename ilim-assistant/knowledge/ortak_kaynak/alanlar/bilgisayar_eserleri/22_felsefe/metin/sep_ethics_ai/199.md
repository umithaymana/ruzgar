Humean Skepticism Humean Skepticism presupposes a distinction between inferential and non-inferential belief. Let&rsquo;s say that a belief is inferentially justified for a subject if its justification is due (at least in part) to the justification of other beliefs the subject holds. A non-inferential belief, on the other hand, is a belief that is not based on other belief&mdash;and so, if it is justified at all, it is non-inferentially, or basically , justified. We will have much more to say about the distinction between inferential and basic belief when we discuss Pyrrhonian Skepticism, but this brief characterization will do for the purposes of our discussion of Humean Skepticism. The Humean Skeptic thinks that we may well have basically justified beliefs. For instance, the Humean Skeptic is happy to grant that if my belief that it is sunny today is based on my experiences and not on any other beliefs, then it may well be justified and amount to knowledge. Moreover, given some initial stock of known propositions, the Humean Skeptic is happy to grant that we can expand that stock by noticing that some other propositions follow deductively from them. But a lot of what we know falls under neither of those categories, for a lot of what we know is acquired by non-deductive inference. One paradigmatic kind of non-deductive inference is inference by inductive enumeration. Thus, our belief that the sun will come out tomorrow may be based on our beliefs that it came out yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that, and so on. Those premise beliefs, as we might call them, do not deductively entail that the sun will come out tomorrow. We certainly have available now more sophisticated inferential bases for believing that the sun will come out tomorrow than a mere inductive enumeration. We know that the solar system is relatively stable and will remain in its present state of equilibrium for billions of years. But that scientific knowledge is itself the result of inductive inferences. Alternatives to the theories we currently hold are compatible with all the non-inferential data on which those theories rest&mdash;although we take those alternatives to be less likely on the data than the theories we hold. So, on the face of it, many of our everyday and scientific beliefs rest on a foundation of inductive inference. What is the argument that those beliefs therefore do not amount to knowledge? The argument that we can extract from Hume starts from the idea that inductive knowledge stands or falls with the following principle (Hume 1739, section 6): Principle of the Uniformity of Nature (PUN): Things we have not yet experienced will resemble those we have in the relevant respects. Thus, going back to the toy model of enumerative induction, if we have experienced the sun coming out every day (and we have not experienced any counterexample to this pattern), then whether we are justified in believing on that basis that the sun will come out tomorrow depends on whether or not PUN is true. And, according to Hume, we have no way of knowing whether PUN is true. This is so because we can know PUN in only one of two ways: by what is ultimately a demonstrative argument (i.e., by a deductive argument whose premises themselves are either known by direct experience or by deduction from what we know through direct experience), or by what is ultimately an inductive argument. Demonstrative arguments can only give us knowledge of necessary truths. But PUN is not a necessary truth. So we cannot know PUN on the basis of a demonstrative argument. But we cannot know it on the basis of an inductive argument either, because in order for an inductive argument to give us knowledge we would first have to know that PUN is true. Therefore, we cannot know PUN, and so we have no inductively based knowledge. Reactions to this argument vary from confrontational to concessive. Confrontational reactions reject some premise of the argument. For instance, friends of the inductive solution to the problem of induction think that there is nothing wrong with the kind of circularity Hume points out is involved in the inductive argument for PUN (see, for instance, van Cleve 1984). And friends of rationalist solutions to the problem of induction think that we can know a priori that PUN is true (Kant 1781). Concessive reactions grant the conclusion of Hume&rsquo;s argument, but insist that we can live without inductive knowledge because it is not needed either for our everyday or scientific life (Popper 1935). For more details on inductive skepticism, see the entry on the problem of induction . 4. The Argument for Cartesian Skepticism Employing the Closure Principle Many contemporary philosophers take the canonical argument for Cartesian Skepticism to involve skeptical hypotheses and a Closure Principle (CP). [ 5 ] A skeptical hypothesis (with respect to a proposition p and a subject S ) is a proposition SH such that if SH were true, then: (a) S would not know p , and (b) S would not be able to distinguish SH from a situation where S knows p . The evil demon scenario that Descartes envisions at the end of his &ldquo;First Meditation&rdquo; functions as a near-universal skeptical hypothesis, for the demon has the power to deceive any subject regarding almost any proposition (Descartes, 1641). One way in which a SH may satisfy (a) is by describing a situation where p is false, but this is not the only way. Descartes&rsquo; evil demon may induce in a disembodied subject&rsquo;s mind an experience as of the subject&rsquo;s own hands in front of her, as a result of which the subject believes that there are hands in front of her, while at the same time dangling some unattached hands in front of the subject (we are waiving here difficulties having to do with how to locate objects relative to disembodied subjects). The subject&rsquo;s belief that there are hands in front of her is in that case true, but she still doesn&rsquo;t know it. The connection between Closure principles and arguments for skepticism gets complicated if we countenance skeptical hypotheses which do not entail the falsehood of the proposition in question, and so in what follows we limit our discussion to those that do. Letting &ldquo; h &rdquo; stand for any proposition about the external world we would ordinarily take ourselves to be justified in believing, for example, G. E. Moore&rsquo;s famous &ldquo;here&rsquo;s a hand&rdquo; (Moore 1939 [1993]), and re-using &ldquo; SH &rdquo; for a skeptical hypothesis relative to h (we leave the subject tacit), we can state the contemporary canonical CP-style argument for Cartesian Skepticism as follows: CP1. If I am justified in believing that h , then I am justified in believing that \({\sim}\textit{SH}\). CP2. I am not justified in believing that \({\sim}\textit{SH}\). Therefore, I am not justified in believing that h . CP1 follows from the following Closure Principle (letting &ldquo; Jp &rdquo; stand for the subject is justified in believing p ): Closure Principle [ CP ]: For all propositions p and q , if p entails q , and Jp , then Jq . (In the argument above, \(p = h\) and \(q = {\sim}SH\).) A crucial feature of CP is that it does not depend upon employing a stringent notion of justification. Suppose that (positive) justification comes in degrees, where the lowest degree is something like mere plausibility and the highest degree is absolute certainty. CP could be recast as follows: CP*: For all propositions, p and q , if p entails q , and Jp to degree u , then Jq to degree v (where \(u \le v)\). There appear to be only three ways that one can respond to the CP-style skeptical argument: deny at least one premise, deny that the argument is valid, or reluctantly accept the conclusion&mdash;if neither of the first two alternatives succeeds. 4.1 Consideration of CP1 Let us begin an examination of CP1 and the general closure principle, CP, of which CP1 is an instantiation.

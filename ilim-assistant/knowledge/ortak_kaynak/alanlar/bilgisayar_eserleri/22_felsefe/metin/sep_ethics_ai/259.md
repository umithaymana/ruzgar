(2005: 25) 3.4.3 Election Interference and Misinformation The concept of the Search Engine Manipulation Effect (SEME) has been presented by Epstein and Robertson (2015) to describe the significant influence that search engine rankings can have on user opinions and behavior, particularly in the context of elections. Their research indicates that biased search rankings can sway undecided voters, potentially influencing the outcomes of close races (Epstein et al. 2017). Given the high reliance on search engines for information, this manipulation can distort democratic processes by amplifying certain narratives while suppressing others, thereby altering public opinion without the users&rsquo; conscious awareness. During the 2016 U.S. presidential election, there were widespread concerns about the role of misinformation and biased information dissemination through online platforms, including search engines. Manipulated search results could amplify certain political narratives while suppressing others, thereby shaping public perception. This manipulation was not limited to the United States; similar concerns were raised in other countries such as India and Australia where search engine rankings could influence voter behavior by presenting skewed information that favored specific candidates or political parties. The ability of search engines to influence voter behavior through biased algorithms underscores the need for greater transparency and regulation to ensure fair and impartial access to information . If Berners-Lee, Hinman, Epstein and Robertson, and others are correct, it would seem that we have much to worry about indeed as we go forward trying to preserve our basic freedoms in a democracy, while at the same time taking advantage of many of the personalizing- and customizing-based features that search engines such as Google now offer us. It would also seem that increased transparency on the part of search engine companies could would be an important step in helping us to alleviate some of these concerns. But who, exactly, should be responsible for regulating search engine companies and for holding them accountable? We examine these and related questions in Section 4 . First, however, we briefly consider some ethical issues, particularly cyber-security related concerns, that arise because of a relatively new kind of search engine that is capable of &ldquo;discovering&rdquo; the location of &ldquo;things&rdquo; (objects) via the Internet. 3.5 (Cyber)Security and the Internet of Things In Sections 3.1 through 3.4, we examined a wide range of ethical concerns affecting the use of search engines, focusing mainly on what can be viewed as the &ldquo;standard&rdquo; or received ethical concerns that have come to be associated with search engines during the past three decades. One topic that arguably has been neglected, or perhaps considered only indirectly (e.g., in our earlier discussion of privacy-related issues in Section 3.2 and Section 3.3 ), has to do with ethical concerns affecting (cyber)security . In addition to data security, which is closely related to data privacy (described earlier), issues associated with system and network security, as well as with national and international security, also now arise in light of a specific kind of search search engine that is capable of locating &ldquo;things&rdquo;. In this section, we briefly consider some emerging security-related challenges generated by the use of this type of search engine in the context of the Internet of Things (IoT). What is IoT, and how does it raise security-related ethical concerns affecting search engines? IoT generally refers to the interconnection of &ldquo;objects&rdquo; on the Internet; these (networked and &ldquo;intelligent&rdquo;) objects can include webcams, smart speakers, wearables, and other devices that are not easily identifiable, locatable, or accessible to users via conventional search engines (see, for example, Burgess 2018). So users might assume that since information about these objects would not be accessible via typical Internet searches, the objects would be fairly secure from network hackers. Many users might also be enamored by the kinds of conveniences they could enjoy if their &ldquo;intelligent&rdquo; objects were networked together. Consider, for example, a &ldquo;smart home&rdquo; where these objects could communicate with one another as well as with that homeowner&rsquo;s mobile devices (and possibly also communicate directly with networked devices and applications embedded in the homeowner&rsquo;s automobile). On the one hand, we can imagine that the owner of this home would be delighted if its &ldquo;intelligent&rdquo; refrigerator communicated with her while driving home to alert her that the refrigerator&rsquo;s supply of milk is low. The refrigerator might also communicate directly with the homeowner&rsquo;s smart phone to trigger an app designed to display the lowest prices for milk at nearby grocery stores. So, ordinary users could easily come to enjoy the kinds of conveniences made possible via their interconnected &ldquo;intelligent&rdquo; objects. On the downside, however, it is likely that these objects may not have an appropriate level of network security. Thus users could be vulnerable to having their objects hacked and also be subject to having personal information about them (vis-&agrave;-vis their interactions with these objects) acquired by unauthorized individuals and organizations, if search engines were indeed capable of discovering and locating their (non-sufficiently-secure) objects and devices. Whereas traditional search engines have assisted users in locating online information pertaining to the names of people and places, commercial/governmental/educational organizations and institutions, etc. (via a standard protocol involving HTTP addresses and HTML programming code), some search engines are now also capable of searching the Internet for certain kinds of &ldquo;things&rdquo; (or objects). Two of these search engines are Thingful and Shodan . As such, Thingful boasts that it can index across multiple IoT networks and infrastructures.

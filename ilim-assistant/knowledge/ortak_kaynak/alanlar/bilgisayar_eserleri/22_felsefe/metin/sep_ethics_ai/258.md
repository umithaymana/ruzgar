We next shift our focus away from privacy concerns about monitoring in the commercial sector to worries about surveillance by government actors with respect to information acquired as a result of users&rsquo; search queries. Earlier in this section we noted that in 2005, the Bush Administration informed Google that it must turn over a list of all users&rsquo; queries entered into its search engine during a one week period (the exact dates were not specified by Google). The Bush Administration&rsquo;s decision to seek information about the search requests of ordinary users triggered significant criticism from many privacy advocates. Although the Bush Administration claimed that it had the authority to seek electronic information in order to fight the &ldquo;war on terror&rdquo; and to prevent another September 11-like attack, some critics worried that the government was trying to use the subpoenaed information, not for national defense or anti-terrorism purposes, but rather to gain data to support its stance on the Child Online Protection Act, which had been challenged in a U.S. District Court and was being revisited by Congress (Nissenbaum 2010: 29). These critics also worried about the implications this has for privacy (as an important human value) in the ongoing tension involving security vs. privacy interests. And even if privacy is not an absolute value but is sometimes outweighed by security concerns, as Himma (2007) argued, some critics question the rationale used for obtaining records of search requests made by ordinary citizens. Hinman (2005) noted that the Patriot Act, passed in the aftermath of 9/11, allowed U.S. government officials to get information from libraries about which books members had borrowed. He then shows how the reasoning used in the case of libraries could easily be extended to search engines&mdash;for example, if the government could see which books someone was taking out of a library, why couldn&rsquo;t it also see which searches we made on search engines? Hinman also pointed out that there are several other ways in which a user&rsquo;s search requests can be disclosed because of practices used by major search engine companies such as Google. He also worried that such practices could eventually lead to surveillance and to suppressing political dissent (as is it has in China), a concern that became greater after Edward Snowden&rsquo;s revelation of government access to search engine data (Greenwald 2013). Hinman questioned whether Google might have been under political pressure from outside interests (e.g., the Bush Administration) to take down photographs of tortured prisoners in the controversial Abu Ghraib detention center (used by the U.S. during the Iraq War in 2004), which were posted but then soon removed with no apparent explanation by that search engine company. (Some related, as well as additional, questions concerning moral-responsibility-related issues for search engine companies are examined in detail in Section 4 .) 3.4 Democracy, Censorship, and the Threat to Liberty and Freedom 3.4.1 Democratic Deliberation and Access to Information In this section, we consider some implications that search-engine companies can have for a free and open society. In the early days of the Internet, many people assumed that search engine technology favored democracy and democratic ideals. For example, Introna and Nissenbaum (2000: 169) note that search engines were viewed as a technology that would &hellip;give voice to diverse social, economic, and cultural groups, to members of society not frequently heard in the public sphere [and] empower the traditionally disempowered, giving them access both to typically unreachable modes of power and to previously unavailable troves of information. However, Introna and Nissenbaum also described what can be viewed as an &ldquo;anti-democratic&rdquo; aspect of contemporary search technology when they note that search engines &ldquo;systematically exclude&rdquo; certain Web sites, as well as &ldquo;certain types of sites&rdquo;, over others. And Diaz (2008: 11) echoed this concern when he notes that that major search engine companies such as Google direct &ldquo;hundreds of millions of users towards some content and not others, towards some sources and not others&rdquo;. So, following Diaz (2008: 15), we can ask whether the kinds of &ldquo;independent voices and diverse viewpoints&rdquo; that are essential for a democracy are capable of being &ldquo;heard through the filter of search engines&rdquo;. Search engines have often been described as the &ldquo;gatekeepers of cyberspace&rdquo;, and some critics note that this has significant implications for democracy. For example, Diaz (2008: 11) pointed out that if we believe in the principles of deliberative democracy&mdash;and especially if we believe that that the Web is an open &ldquo;democratic&rdquo; medium&mdash;then we should expect our search engines to disseminate a broad spectrum of information on any given topic. Hinman (2005: 25) made a similar point, when he notes that &ldquo;the flourishing of deliberative democracy is dependent on the free and undistorted access to information&rdquo;. And because search engines are &ldquo;increasingly the principal gatekeepers of knowledge&rdquo;, Hinman argued that &ldquo;we find ourselves moving in a philosophically dangerous position&rdquo;. (We briefly return to Hinman&rsquo;s point in the concluding section of this entry.) Morozov (2011) also described some concerns for democracy vis-&agrave;-vis contemporary search engines by calling attention to the filtering of information that search engines make possible. For one thing, he agrees with Sunstein (2001) who worried that the kind of selectivity made possible by Internet filtering can easily trap us inside our &ldquo;information cocoons&rdquo;. And Lessig (2000) suggested that any kind of filtering on the Internet is equivalent to censorship because it blocks out some forms of expression. Morozov points out that whereas Sunstein worries that people could use Internet technology to &ldquo;overly customize what they read&rdquo;, the reality is that contemporary search engine companies have already silently done this for them. Morozov&rsquo;s concerns about what search engine companies are now doing through filtering and customization schemes, and why this is problematic for a democracy, are echoed by Pariser who pointed out that personalization filters serve up a kind of invisible autopropaganda, indoctrinating us with our own ideas, amplifying our desire for things that are familiar and leaving us oblivious to the dangers lurking in the dark territory of the unknown. (2011: 13) Pariser noted that while democracy &ldquo;requires citizens to see things from one another&rsquo;s point of view&rdquo;, we are instead increasingly &ldquo;more enclosed in our own bubbles&rdquo;. He goes on to note that democracy also &ldquo;requires a reliance on shared facts&rdquo;, but instead we are being presented with &ldquo;parallel but separate universes&rdquo;. To illustrate how this trend away from citizens having shared facts can be so dangerous for a democracy, Pariser used the example of the debate about climate change in the U.S. He pointed out that studies have shown that between 2001 and 2010 many people&rsquo;s beliefs about whether the climate was warming shifted significantly, based on one&rsquo;s affiliation with a major political party. Pariser noted that a Web search for &ldquo;climate change&rdquo; will yield very different results for a person whom the search algorithm determines to be a Democrat than for someone it determines to be a Republican. He also showed that the search algorithm will generate different results for someone it determines to be an oil company executive vs. an environmental activist. Along lines similar to Pariser&rsquo;s, Halpern (2011: 5&ndash;6) argued that search engines like Google direct us to material that is most likely to reinforce our own &ldquo;worldview, ideology, and assumptions&rdquo; and thus &ldquo;cut us off from dissenting opinion and conflicting points of view&rdquo;. Pariser pointed out that with Google, a user now gets the results that the search engine company believes are best for that particular user. He described a case where two people entered in the keyword &ldquo;BP&rdquo; (for British Petroleum) during the time period of the accident involving the Deep Water Horizon oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico. In response to one user&rsquo;s query, investment information about BP was returned as the lead result, while the other user received information about the oil spill. 3.4.2 Censorship and Government Restrictions Not only do some practices by search engine companies pose a threat for democracy and democratic ideals, other practices (in which search engine companies are arguably complicit) reinforce censorship schemes currently used by non-democratic nations. Consider that it blocks ordinary Chinese users from access to sites such as &ldquo;Tiananmen Square&rdquo;, &ldquo;Free Tibet&rdquo;, and &ldquo;Dalai Lama&rdquo;.

What is trust, and why is it so important in the context of SECs? Following Baier (1986), many philosophers view trust as an &ldquo;attitude&rdquo;, while others see trust either in &ldquo;relational terms&rdquo; (Robbins 2016) or as a kind of &ldquo;expectation&rdquo; (Walker 2006). And as Govier (1997: 35) notes, one reason why trust is important is because &ldquo;attitudes of trust and distrust affect the nature and quality of our social reality&rdquo;. When discussing the topic of trust, it is important to make some key distinctions at the outset. For example, we need to differentiate trust from trustworthiness. Trust typically refers to the feeling or attitude that one person (the trustor) has toward another person or entity (the trustee); trustworthiness, on the other hand, is often seen as a quality or attribute of the trustee&mdash;whether they deserve to be trusted based on certain characteristics, actions, or qualities they exhibit. Additionally, it is important to distinguish between ethical trust and epistemic trust (both of which are described below), in the context of SECs. Are contemporary SECs trustworthy organizations? If so, why is it that we claim to trust them? Is it because (a) we perceive them as a reliable resource for directing us to the most relevant and accurate online information, in response to our search queries (i.e., epistemic trust)? Or is it because (b) SEC&rsquo;s policies regarding what they do with our personal information, once collected, are perceived as transparent and fair (ethical trust)? Or is it because of both (a) and (b)? We can also ask whether there might be some alternative/additional reasons why one could claim to trust or distrust an SEC. Of course, one could reasonably question whether it is possible for humans to enter into a trust relationship with an SEC, as a corporate entity, or even with a non-human entity of any kind. For example, some might assume that a genuine trust relationship can only exist between humans (or perhaps more specifically, between &ldquo;human agents&rdquo;). Others, however, believe that trust relationships can be extended to include some non-human, or &ldquo;artificial&rdquo;, agents&mdash;i.e., not only corporations, which are sometimes viewed as aggregates of human agents, but also artificial electronic agents such as &ldquo;bots&rdquo;. Because issues affecting trust and responsibility for SECs are distributed over a vast and diffuse network, many diverse kinds of agents can be involved&mdash;e.g., from corporate CEOs and executives/boards, to customer-service representatives, to engineers and software developers, to the many (non-human) artificial (AI) agents or bots that can also &ldquo;make some limited decisions&rdquo; on behalf of an SEC. So what, exactly, is it that we trust/distrust when we say we trust/distrust an SEC? In claiming to trust Google, for example, we may, in effect, be saying that we trust all of the component elements comprising that SEC. One model that can help us to think about trust in contexts as vast and complex as major SECs is Margaret Urban Walker&rsquo;s framework of &ldquo;diffuse default trust&rdquo; (Walker 2006). In Walker&rsquo;s scheme, trust relationships occur in environments (i.e., &ldquo;spaces and circumstances&rdquo;) that she calls &ldquo;zones of default trust&rdquo;. Walker differentiates trust from mere reliance by noting that in a trust relationship between A and B, A not only expects B to do X, but A expects it of B (2006: 79). And because trust &ldquo;links reliance to responsibility&rdquo;, Walker (2006: 80) believes that A has a &ldquo;normative expectation&rdquo; of B. But she also notes that we are not always conscious of these normative expectations, because they are typically &ldquo;unreflective and often nonspecific&rdquo; expectations where &ldquo;strangers or unknown others may be relied upon to behave in acceptable ways&rdquo;. Our reliance on the &ldquo;good and tolerable behavior of others&rdquo; is at the core of Walker&rsquo;s notion of &ldquo;default trust&rdquo; (2006: 85). An important feature of Walker&rsquo;s framework is that it allows for trust relations between individuals who have not met, and who will possibly never meet, in person. And in her scheme, trust relationships can occur not only between humans who are unknown to one another, but also between humans and non-human entities such as corporations. Walker illustrates the latter point in an example where someone encounters particularly bad service on an airplane operated by a major commercial airline. She points out that when we have such an experience of bad service, it is appropriate to feel resentment, not necessarily toward specific individuals who work for the airline, but toward the airline itself. So the entities comprising a zone of trust need not be limited to known (human) individuals. Walker (2006: 85) extends her notion of default trust to include zones of &ldquo;diffuse default trust&rdquo;, as also illustrated in her example involving the major airline corporation. Consider the many ways that responsibility can become &ldquo;diffuse&rdquo; when distributed over a vast zone of trust such as a commercial airline (Walker), or a major SEC like Google which also qualifies as a zone of diffuse-default trust comprising a diverse range of agents (both human and non-human). Among the non-human entities in that zone are artificial electronic agents (bots) who &ldquo;operate behind the scenes&rdquo;, both with humans and other artificial agents. The latter kinds of entities also include multi-agent systems that can &ldquo;act&rdquo; in limited ways on behalf of an SEC. For a detailed description of how Walker&rsquo;s model can be applied in digital contexts, including zones of diffuse default trust comprising both humans and multi-agent systems, see the analysis in Buechner and Tavani (2011). As noted above, it is important to distinguish between epistemic trust and ethical trust, both of which are essential for trusting SECs. Whereas concerns regarding the former sense of trust overlap with issues affecting an SEC&rsquo;s reliability, as well as with the accuracy of information contained on the Web sites it indexes and links to, ethical trust in the case of SECs can overlap with questions/concerns pertaining to a search organization&rsquo;s user policies and whether those policies are open, fair, and trustworthy. Recognizing and complying with the requirements of both aspects of trust is crucial for an SEC like Google to realize a key objective stated in its code of conduct&mdash;viz., to &ldquo;increase&rdquo; and &ldquo;continually earn&hellip;trust&rdquo; with its users (The Google Code of Conduct, 2018). We conclude this section by acknowledging that much more could be said about the important role that trust plays in the context of SECs. But we have at least identified some of the reasons why trust is very important for SECs and also showed how those companies can qualify as &ldquo;zones of diffuse default trust&rdquo; (Walker). Although we have examined only one model of trust&mdash;Walker&rsquo;s framework&mdash;in analyzing some of the many dimensions of trust affecting SECs, we have not argued that Walker&rsquo;s is the only, or even the best, theory for understanding trust issues affecting SECs. Nor have we argued that Walker&rsquo;s theory is without internal problems. Instead, we put aside those kinds of questions in favor of using a specific model to analyze trust-related questions affecting SECs in a systematic way.

The Patch You Didn&#x27;t Have to Install: Why Secure Configuration Outlasts the Zero-Day Race You need to enable JavaScript to run this app. CIS Portal Login CIS Hardened Images &reg; Support CIS WorkBench Sign In Alert Level: elevated Home Insights Blog Posts The Patch You Didn&#39;t Have to Install: Why Secure Configuration Outlasts the Zero-Day Race The Patch You Didn&#39;t Have to Install: Why Secure Configuration Outlasts the Zero-Day Race CIS Benchmarks Technology Here is a number that should reshape how you think about cyber defense: negative seven days. Among the vulnerabilities most actively exploited by attackers, that is the latest estimated average gap between public disclosure and exploitation in the wild. Negative, because the attack now typically begins about a week before the flaw is disclosed. Across successive industry studies, that window shrank from roughly 63 days in 2018-2019 to 32 days in 2021-2022 and about five days in 2023. According to Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 , it has now crossed zero. For the vulnerabilities attackers care about most, the patching race is frequently lost before it starts. This post is about the layer of defense that doesn't need to win that race because it's already in place before the race begins: secure configuration. The settings are documented, the guidance is free to download, and helps neutralize attacks that haven't even been invented yet. The CIS Benchmarks are the most widely adopted expression of that idea. There is a catch. It's the reason this argument ends with complete baselines rather than a highlights reel. The settings that ultimately matter are rarely the ones that look important beforehand. In early 2017, "disable SMBv1" was just one deprecation notice among many. Nothing suggested it would become the control that neutralized an NSA-grade exploit and helped prevent two of the costliest cyber incidents in history. Today's baselines are filled with settings that fit the same profile: unremarkable right up until the day they become famous. Why a Patch-First Strategy Keeps Losing None of this is the fault of the teams doing the patching. The weaknesses are structural. A patch only exists after someone discovers a flaw, making patching inherently reactive. A patch only protects the systems that actually receive it, which at enterprise scale is rarely all of them. There is always a server that can't reboot, a legacy application nobody wants to touch, or a device that has fallen out of inventory. And patching never ends. Missing a single update window can reopen exposure. That treadmill is also accelerating. Most intrusions still stem from ordinary human error and systemic weaknesses rather than AI. What AI does is enlarge the target. It helps organizations ship more code, faster, often carrying the same insecure patterns at machine scale. It also creates entirely new classes of services, including agents, model endpoints, and the connectors between them. Each introduces another capability that must be secured. The treadmill was already struggling to keep up with the volume of known vulnerabilities. AI increases that volume. Exhibit A: Patched in March, Catastrophic by Summer On March 14, 2017, Microsoft released MS17-010, closing a flaw in version 1 of the SMB file-sharing protocol, a full month before the &ldquo;EternalBlue&rdquo; exploit code leaked publicly. Defenders had a genuine head start. It didn&rsquo;t matter. On May 12, WannaCry tore through unpatched machines using exactly that flaw, reaching more than 200,000 computers across roughly 150 countries within a day; the UK&rsquo;s National Health Service diverted ambulances and cancelled procedures. Six weeks later NotPetya used the same vulnerability, months after the patch shipped, and caused an estimated $10 billion in global damage. One example is the global shipping company Maersk that rebuilt roughly 45,000 PCs, 4,000 servers, and suffered an estimated loss of around $300 million. Another is the pharmaceutical company Merck whose claimed losses reached roughly $1.4 billion due to longer-term manufacturing disruptions. Two of the costliest cyber events in history ran on a flaw that had been patchable for months, because &ldquo;a patch exists&rdquo; and &ldquo;every machine is patched&rdquo; are very different statements. SMBv1 is a decades-old protocol that almost no modern environment needs, and guidance to disable it predates the attacks. Microsoft's own storage team publicly urged customers to "stop using SMB1" in September 2016, and US-CERT echoed the recommendation in January 2017. On systems where SMBv1 was disabled, EternalBlue had no attack path, and that protection did not depend on whether MS17-010 had been installed. CIS added the SMBv1 recommendation in October 2017, after WannaCry, once Microsoft released the Group Policy settings required to enforce it. That sequence reveals what a secure configuration baseline really is: institutional memory. A costly lesson paid for in someone else's outage is captured once and shared broadly. Every organization that adopts the recommendation thereafter benefits from the protection from that day forward.

[ 204 ] The privacy Calculus [ edit ] The "privacy calculus" model holds that disclosure decisions reflect a weighing of perceived risks against expected benefits. [ 206 ] [ 207 ] The model has been extended to electronic commerce, where trust and perceived benefits were found to offset privacy concerns in decisions to transact online. [ 208 ] Critics note that the model assumes a degree of information and rationality that users frequently lack, and that it may legitimise practices in which privacy is traded for small or illusory benefits. [ 205 ] Privacy Paradox and Economic valuation [ edit ] However, the relationship between concerns and behaviour is likely only small, and there are several arguments that can explain why that is the case. According to the attitude-behaviour gap , attitudes and behaviors are in general and in most cases not closely related. [ 209 ] A main explanation for the partial mismatch in the context of privacy specifically is that users lack awareness of the risks and the degree of protection. [ 175 ] Users may underestimate the harm of disclosing information online. [ 14 ] On the other hand, some researchers argue that the mismatch comes from lack of technology literacy and from the design of sites. [ 210 ] For example, users may not know how to change their default settings even though they care about their privacy. Psychologists Sonja Utz and Nicole C. Krämer particularly pointed out that the privacy paradox can occur when users must trade-off between their privacy concerns and impression management. [ 211 ] The Economic valuation of privacy [ edit ] See also: Surveillance capitalism and Mass surveillance industry The willingness to incur a privacy risk is suspected to be driven by a complex array of factors including risk attitudes, personal value for private information, and general attitudes to privacy (which are typically measured using surveys). [ 212 ] One experiment aiming to determine the monetary value of several types of personal information indicated relatively low evaluations of personal information. [ 205 ] Providing personal data does not only regard monetary value, but also increased ease of use in voluntary interactions in government processes. [ 213 ] Despite claims that ascertaining the value of data requires a "stock-market for personal information", [ 214 ] surveillance capitalism and the mass surveillance industry regularly place price tags on this form of data as it is shared between corporations and governments. Users are not always given the tools to live up to their professed privacy concerns, and they are sometimes willing to trade private information for convenience, functionality, or financial gain, even when the gains are very small. [ 215 ] One study suggests that people think their browser history is worth the equivalent of a cheap meal. [ 216 ] Another finds that attitudes to privacy risk do not appear to depend on whether it is already under threat or not. [ 212 ] The methodology of user empowerment describes how to provide users with sufficient context to make privacy-informed decisions. Further information: Privacy concerns with social networking services It is suggested by Andréa Belliger and David J. Krieger that the privacy paradox should not be considered a paradox, but more of a privacy dilemma , for services that cannot exist without the user sharing private data. [ 216 ] However, the general public is typically not given the choice whether to share private data or not, [ 213 ] [ 184 ] making it difficult to verify any claim that a service truly cannot exist without sharing private data. Privacy in Non-Human animals [ edit ] Lack of privacy in public spaces, caused by overcrowding, increases health issues in animals, including heart disease and high blood pressure . Also, the stress from overcrowding is connected to an increase in infant mortality rates and maternal stress. The lack of privacy that comes with overcrowding is connected to other issues in animals, which causes their relationships with others to diminish. How they present themselves to others of their species is a necessity in their life, and overcrowding causes the relationships to become disordered. [ 217 ] Some philosophers have argued that non-human animals, too, have interests in privacy. Angie Pepper contends that animals kept in zoos are wronged by constant exposure to observation, because it deprives them of control over how and when they are seen by others. [ 218 ] The naturalist David Attenborough suggested in 2016 that zoos should allow visitors to view gorillas through peepholes rather than glass panels because the animals "value their privacy". [ 219 ] Studies of the "visitor effect" have reported that the presence of human audiences can be associated with changes in behaviour, including increased aggression and reduced social interaction, in a number of primate species. [ 220 ] Protecting privacy [ edit ] Main article: Privacy-enhancing technologies Technical measures [ edit ] Further information: Encryption and Anonymity Encryption protects the confidentiality of communications and stored data. End-to-end encryption , in which only the communicating parties hold the keys, is now widely used in messaging services; proposals to require "exceptional access" for law enforcement have been criticised by security researchers on the ground that any such mechanism would create vulnerabilities exploitable by others. [ 221 ] Anonymity networks such as Tor route traffic through several relays so that no single relay can link a user to the destination of his or her communications. [ 222 ] Differential privacy provides a mathematical guarantee that the output of a statistical analysis reveals little about any individual in the dataset, and has been adopted by statistical agencies and technology companies. [ 223 ] Privacy by design [ edit ] Main article: Privacy by design " Privacy by design ", a concept developed by the Ontario Information and Privacy Commissioner Ann Cavoukian , holds that privacy should be built into systems as a default setting rather than added afterwards. [ 224 ] The GDPR gives the idea legal force by requiring controllers to implement "data protection by design and by default". [ 225 ] Some underlying principles of this concept imply the proactive protections, anticipating privacy-invasive scenarios and defensing properly against them. [ 226 ] Individual and collective action [ edit ] Individuals can reduce their exposure by limiting what they disclose, adjusting privacy settings and using protective tools, but researchers have stressed the limits of individual self-help where services are designed to encourage disclosure and where consent processes are complex. [ 194 ] [ 14 ] Civil-society organisations such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation and Privacy International therefore combine technical advocacy with litigation and campaigns for legal reform.

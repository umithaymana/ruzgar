Banksy 's One Nation Under CCTV (2008), painted beside a working closed-circuit television camera in London Privacy ( English pronunciation: /ˈprɪvəsi/ , English pronunciation: /ˈpraɪvəsi/ ) [ 1 ] [ 2 ] is the condition or claim of an individual or group to be free from unwanted access, observation or disclosure, and to determine for themselves how far they, and information about them, are exposed to others. The concept has no single accepted definition. Lawyers, philosophers and social scientists have variously understood it as a right to be let alone, as limited access to the self, as secrecy, as control over personal information, as a precondition of personhood and intimate relationships, and as the appropriate flow of information within social contexts. [ 3 ] Distinctions between public and private life are found in many ancient societies and religious traditions, but privacy emerged as a distinct legal claim only in the nineteenth century, when new printing and photographic technologies and the popular press prompted calls for legal protection. [ 4 ] [ 5 ] In the second half of the twentieth century the spread of computers shifted attention from intrusion and publicity towards the collection and processing of personal data, producing a body of data protection law that began in Western Europe in the 1970s and has since spread worldwide. [ 6 ] [ 7 ] Privacy is protected as a human right by Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights , Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and regional instruments such as Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights . [ 8 ] [ 9 ] [ 10 ] Many national constitutions protect it expressly, and courts in other countries have derived it from broader guarantees. [ 11 ] The structure of protection nevertheless differs markedly between jurisdictions: European law tends to treat privacy as an aspect of personal dignity and regulates the processing of personal data comprehensively, whereas law in the United States has traditionally emphasised liberty against the state and regulates the private sector by sector. [ 12 ] The Internet , mobile phones and large-scale data analysis have intensified debate about surveillance by governments and corporations, the commercial use of personal data, and the extent to which individuals can meaningfully control information about themselves. [ 13 ] [ 14 ] Responses include legislation, litigation, and privacy-enhancing technologies such as encryption and anonymity tools. Privacy overlaps with, but is distinct from, security : information security concerns the protection of data against unauthorised access, whereas privacy also concerns whether data should be collected and used at all, and for what purposes. [ 15 ] Privacy may also take the form of bodily integrity and protection of the home and person against physical intrusion. In many legal systems, protection of digital privacy has developed as an extension of the older right to privacy , supplemented by legislation regulating both public and private bodies. [ 7 ] Etymology [ edit ] Look up privacy or privatus#Latin in Wiktionary, the free dictionary. The English word privacy is derived from private , which comes from the Latin privatus , "set apart (from what is public), belonging to oneself (not to the state)". [ 16 ] Privatus is the perfect passive participle of the verb prīvō (infinitive prīvāre ), "to bereave, deprive" and also "to free, release", which is itself derived from privus , "one's own, individual". [ 17 ] [ 16 ] Conceptions of privacy [ edit ] Further information: Right to privacy and Philosophy of privacy Scholars have long observed that privacy is a contested and internally diverse concept. Daniel J. Solove describes it as "a concept in disarray" and groups the main theoretical accounts into six families: the right to be let alone, limited access to the self, secrecy, control over personal information, personhood, and intimacy. [ 3 ] Each captures some privacy problems while failing to account for others, which has led Solove and others to favour pluralistic or contextual accounts over a single definition. [ 18 ] The right to be let alone [ edit ] In " The Right to Privacy " (1890), the Boston lawyers Samuel D. Warren and Louis Brandeis argued that the common law should recognise a general "right to be let alone", a phrase they took from Judge Thomas M. Cooley . [ 19 ] Their immediate concern was that "instantaneous photographs and newspaper enterprise" had "invaded the sacred precincts of private and domestic life", a reference to the sensationalist press of the period, often described as yellow journalism . [ 5 ] They grounded the right not in property or contract but in the protection of an "inviolate personality". [ 20 ] The formula proved influential but indeterminate, since it does not explain which forms of being left alone the law should protect. It has been read to include a person's right to choose seclusion from the attention of others, and to be free from scrutiny or observation in private settings such as the home. [ 21 ] Although the concept did not define privacy precisely enough to ground comprehensive legal protection, it strengthened the notion of an individual right to privacy and began a long tradition of debate about such rights in the United States. [ 21 ] Limited access [ edit ] A second family of theories treats privacy as limited accessibility to the self. In 1890 the American journalist E. L. Godkin described private life as "the right of every man to keep his affairs to himself, and to decide for himself to what extent they shall be the subject of public observation and discussion". [ 22 ] [ 23 ] Ruth Gavison developed the idea analytically, arguing that privacy concerns the extent to which a person is known to others (secrecy), the subject of their attention (anonymity) and physically accessible to them (solitude). [ 24 ] Sissela Bok similarly defined privacy as "the condition of being protected from unwanted access by others—either physical access, personal information, or attention". [ 25 ] Secrecy [ edit ] On a narrower view, privacy is the concealment of discreditable or sensitive information. Richard Posner described it as the right of people to "conceal information about themselves that others might use to their disadvantage", and argued on economic grounds that such concealment can amount to a form of misrepresentation that impairs the efficient working of markets. [ 26 ] [ 27 ] When privacy is understood as secrecy, it is usually imagined as a selective secrecy, in which individuals keep some information private while choosing to make other information public. [ 28 ] Critics reply that the secrecy view cannot explain why a person may retain a privacy interest in information that has already been disclosed to some people, or in information that is not discreditable at all. [ 29 ] Control over personal information [ edit ] The most influential definition in the information age is that of the political scientist Alan Westin , for whom privacy is "the claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others". [ 30 ] The philosopher Charles Fried likewise argued that privacy "is not simply an absence of information about us in the minds of others; rather it is the control we have over information about ourselves". [ 31 ] The control model underlies the fair information practice principles on which most data protection legislation is built.

As technology has advanced, the way in which privacy is protected and violated has changed with it. In the case of some technologies, such as the printing press or the Internet , the increased ability to share information can lead to new ways in which privacy can be breached. It is generally agreed that the first publication advocating privacy in the United States was the 1890 article by Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis , "The Right to Privacy", [ 87 ] and that it was written mainly in response to the increase in newspapers and photographs made possible by printing technologies. [ 88 ] In 1948, 1984 , written by George Orwell , was published. A classic dystopian novel, 1984 describes the life of Winston Smith in 1984, located in Oceania, a totalitarian state. The all-controlling Party, the party in power led by Big Brother, is able to control power through mass surveillance and limited freedom of speech and thought. George Orwell provides commentary on the negative effects of totalitarianism , particularly on privacy and censorship . [ 89 ] Parallels have been drawn between 1984 and modern censorship and privacy, a notable example being that large social media companies, rather than the government, are able to monitor a user's data and decide what is allowed to be said online through their censorship policies, ultimately for monetary purposes. [ 90 ] In the 1960s, people began to consider how changes in technology were bringing changes in the concept of privacy. [ 91 ] Vance Packard 's The Naked Society was a popular book on privacy from that era and led US discourse on privacy at that time. [ 91 ] In addition, Alan Westin 's Privacy and Freedom shifted the debate regarding privacy from a physical sense, how the government controls a person's body (i.e. Roe v. Wade ) and other activities such as wiretapping and photography. As important records became digitized, Westin argued that personal data was becoming too accessible and that a person should have complete jurisdiction over their data, laying the foundation for the modern discussion of privacy. [ 92 ] New technologies can also create new ways to gather private information. In 2001, the legal case Kyllo v. United States (533 U.S. 27) determined that the use of thermal imaging devices that can reveal previously unknown information without a warrant constitutes a violation of privacy. In 2019, after developing a corporate rivalry in competing voice-recognition software, Apple and Amazon required employees to listen to intimate moments and faithfully transcribe the contents. [ 93 ] Legal Frameworks [ edit ] Main articles: Right to privacy , Privacy law , Information privacy law , and Right to be forgotten Privacy International 's 2007 ranking of privacy protection by country, the last such global survey the organisation published International law [ edit ] Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights provides that "No one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation." [ 8 ] Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights gives the guarantee binding force, prohibiting "arbitrary or unlawful interference" with privacy. [ 9 ] In 2013 the General Assembly affirmed that the rights people have offline must also be protected online, including the right to privacy; [ 94 ] the Human Rights Council subsequently created the mandate of Special Rapporteur on the right to privacy . [ 95 ] Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights guarantees "the right to respect for [one's] private and family life, [one's] home and [one's] correspondence", subject to interferences that are in accordance with the law and necessary in a democratic society for specified purposes. [ 10 ] The European Court of Human Rights has interpreted it broadly to cover, among other things, the retention of DNA profiles and the bulk interception of communications. [ 96 ] [ 97 ] The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union distinguishes respect for private and family life (Article 7) from a separate right to the protection of personal data (Article 8). [ 98 ] Other regional frameworks include the APEC Privacy Framework of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation , endorsed in 2004, [ 99 ] and the African Union 's Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection (the Malabo Convention), adopted in 2014. [ 100 ] Models of Regulation [ edit ] Comparative lawyers commonly distinguish between omnibus data protection laws, which apply general principles to the public and private sectors alike, and sectoral regimes, which regulate particular industries or types of data. The European Union exemplifies the first model and the United States the second. [ 7 ] [ 12 ] James Q. Whitman attributes the difference to "two Western cultures of privacy": a continental European tradition rooted in personal dignity and honour , and an American tradition centred on liberty against the state, particularly within the home. [ 12 ] A related policy debate opposes market-based approaches, which rely on notice and individual choice, to consumer-protection approaches, which doubt that individuals have the time or expertise to make informed choices; empirical studies have found, for example, that many online privacy policies demand a reading level beyond that of much of the population. [ 101 ] Surveillance and law enforcement [ edit ] Further information: Mass surveillance , Global surveillance disclosures (2013–present) , Police body camera , and PRISM (surveillance program) See also: The Wire The relationship between police powers and privacy has been a central subject of constitutional litigation, particularly as new technologies have enabled forms of monitoring that do not involve physical entry. In Kyllo v. United States (2001), the United States Supreme Court held that using a thermal imaging device to detect heat inside a home was a search requiring a warrant. [ 54 ] In United States v. Jones (2012), the Court unanimously held that attaching a GPS tracker to a suspect's vehicle and monitoring its movements for 28 days was a search under the Fourth Amendment; the majority relied on the physical trespass involved, while five Justices indicated in concurring opinions that long-term location monitoring would infringe a reasonable expectation of privacy in any event. [ 102 ] The Court did not decide whether a warrant was required, because the government had not argued that the search was reasonable without one. [ 102 ] The European Court of Human Rights reached a different result on comparable facts in Uzun v. Germany (2010). It held that GPS surveillance of a suspect in a series of bomb attacks, lasting about three months, interfered with his private life under Article 8, but that the interference had a sufficient legal basis and was proportionate given the gravity of the offences under investigation; it found no violation. [ 103 ] The Court regarded GPS tracking as interfering less with private life than visual or acoustic surveillance or telephone interception. [ 104 ] In Riley v. California (2014) the United States Supreme Court unanimously held that police generally may not search the digital contents of a mobile phone seized at arrest without a warrant, observing that modern phones hold "the privacies of life". [ 105 ] In Carpenter v. United States (2018) a majority of five to four held that obtaining historical cell-site location records from a mobile operator is a search that generally requires a warrant, limiting the "third-party doctrine" under which information voluntarily disclosed to businesses had been treated as unprotected. [ 106 ] Bulk surveillance by intelligence agencies has raised analogous questions. Following the Snowden disclosures , the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit held in 2015 that the NSA 's bulk collection of telephone records exceeded its statutory authority, and Congress ended the programme in its existing form through the USA Freedom Act later that year. [ 107 ] In Big Brother Watch v United Kingdom (2021), the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights accepted that bulk interception regimes are not in themselves incompatible with the Convention, but found that the British regime lacked adequate "end-to-end safeguards" and violated Article 8. [ 97 ] Selected jurisdictions [ edit ] Australia [ edit ] Main article: Privacy in Australian law The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) originally regulated the handling of personal information by Commonwealth agencies through a set of Information Privacy Principles. It was later extended to much of the private sector, and the Australian Law Reform Commission reviewed its operation in the 2008 report For Your Information . [ 108 ] Implementing many of the report's recommendations, the Privacy Amendment (Enhancing Privacy Protection) Act 2012 replaced the separate public- and private-sector principles with a single set of Australian Privacy Principles. [ 109 ] The High Court left open in 2001 whether the common law recognises a tort of invasion of privacy. [ 110 ] A statutory cause of action for serious invasions of privacy, enacted by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024, came into force on 10 June 2025. [ 111 ] The Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Amendment (Data Retention) Act 2015 requires telecommunications providers to retain specified metadata about communications, as distinct from their content, for two years.

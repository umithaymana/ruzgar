[ 61 ] More recent work emphasises that the burdens of weak privacy protection fall unequally: Scott Skinner-Thompson argues that marginalised groups are both more often required to disclose personal information and more severely harmed by exposure. [ 62 ] Richard Posner and Lawrence Lessig focus on the economic aspects of personal information control. Posner criticizes privacy for concealing information, which reduces market efficiency. For Posner, employment is selling oneself in the labour market, which he believes is like selling a product. Any 'defect' in the 'product' that is not reported is fraud. [ 63 ] For Lessig, privacy breaches online can be regulated through code and law. Lessig claims "the protection of privacy would be stronger if people conceived of the right as a property right", [ 64 ] and that "individuals should be able to control information about themselves". [ 65 ] There have been attempts to establish privacy as one of the fundamental human rights , whose social value is an essential component in the functioning of democratic societies. [ 66 ] Leslie Regan Shade argues that the human right to privacy is necessary for meaningful democratic participation, and ensures human dignity and autonomy. Privacy depends on norms for how information is distributed, and if this is appropriate. Violations of privacy depend on context. The human right to privacy has precedent in the United Nations Declaration of Human Rights : "Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers." [ 8 ] Shade believes that privacy must be approached from a people-centered perspective, and not through the marketplace. [ 67 ] Dr. Eliza Watt, Westminster Law School , University of Westminster in London, UK, proposes application of the international human right law concept of "virtual control" as an approach to deal with extraterritorial mass surveillance by state intelligence agencies. Dr. Watt envisions the "virtual control" test, understood as a remote control over the individual's right to privacy of communications, where privacy is recognized under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) Article 17. This, she contends, may help to close the normative gap that is being exploited by nation states. [ 68 ] Critiques [ edit ] Some philosophers doubt that privacy is a distinct value at all. Judith Jarvis Thomson argued that the "right to privacy" is a cluster of rights each of which derives from other rights, such as rights over one's property and person. [ 69 ] Economic critics, notably Posner, argue that much privacy protection permits people to conceal information that others legitimately need. [ 27 ] Feminist scholars have pointed out that appeals to the privacy of the home and family have historically shielded domestic violence and the subordination of women from public scrutiny; Catharine MacKinnon criticised the privacy rationale of Roe v. Wade on this ground, while Anita L. Allen defended privacy as a good that women have historically been denied. [ 70 ] [ 71 ] History [ edit ] Further information: Privacy law Antiquity and religious traditions [ edit ] Distinctions between public and private domains appear in many early societies. In his comparative study, the sociologist Barrington Moore examined conceptions of privacy in classical Athens , in the Hebrew Bible and in ancient China , concluding that some desire for privacy appears in all of them while its content varies with social structure . [ 4 ] Aristotle 's distinction between the polis , the sphere of political life, and the oikos , the household, is frequently cited as an early statement of the public–private division in Western thought. [ 72 ] Hannah Arendt later drew on the same distinction in her account of the decline of the public realm in modern society. [ 73 ] Religious texts also address intrusion. The Quran enjoins believers not to spy on one another (49:12) and not to enter houses other than their own without permission (24:27), verses that have informed the treatment of privacy in Islamic jurisprudence. [ 74 ] In Sinosphere , the term yinsi ( 隱私 ) or siyin ( 私隱 ) long carried connotations of shameful secrecy, and historians have traced how Confucian and later conceptions of the private ( 私 ) and public ( 公 ) diverged from Western liberal ones. [ 75 ] Early modern and modern Europe [ edit ] Historians of private life in Europe have associated the growth of privacy with changes in domestic architecture, the separation of household from workplace, literacy and the rise of individual religious practice. [ 76 ] Jürgen Habermas traced the emergence, in the eighteenth century, of a bourgeois "public sphere" formed by private persons coming together to debate matters of common concern, which presupposed a protected intimate sphere of the family. [ 77 ] Liberal political theory gave the distinction normative force. John Stuart Mill 's On Liberty (1859) argued that the only legitimate ground for interfering with an individual's liberty against his will is to prevent harm to others, and defended a domain of conduct that "merely concerns himself". [ 78 ] At the end of the eighteenth century Jeremy Bentham proposed the Panopticon , a prison in which inmates could be watched at any time without knowing whether they were being observed; Michel Foucault later took it as a model of a disciplinary power that induces people to regulate their own behaviour. The concept of privacy has been explored and discussed by numerous philosophers throughout history. Privacy has historical roots in ancient Greek philosophical discussions. The most well-known of these was Aristotle's distinction between two spheres of life: the public sphere of the polis , associated with political life, and the private sphere of the oikos , associated with domestic life. [ 72 ] Privacy is valued along with other basic necessities of life in the Jewish deutero-canonical Book of Sirach . [ 80 ] Islam's holy text, the Qur'an, states the following regarding privacy: 'Do not spy on one another' (49:12); 'Do not enter any houses except your own homes unless you are sure of their occupants' consent' (24:27). [ 81 ] English philosopher John Locke 's (1632–1704) writings on natural rights and the social contract laid the groundwork for modern conceptions of individual rights, including the right to privacy. In his Second Treatise of Civil Government (1689), Locke argued that a man is entitled to his own self through one's natural rights of life, liberty, and property. [ 82 ] He believed that the government was responsible for protecting these rights so individuals were guaranteed private spaces to practice personal activities. [ 83 ] In the political sphere, philosophers hold differing views on the right of private judgment. German philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) makes the distinction between moralität , which refers to an individual's private judgment, and sittlichkeit , pertaining to one's rights and obligations as defined by an existing corporate order. On the contrary, Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), an English philosopher, interpreted law as an invasion of privacy. His theory of utilitarianism argued that legal actions should be judged by the extent of their contribution to human wellbeing, or necessary utility. [ 84 ] Hegel's notions were modified by prominent 19th century English philosopher John Stuart Mill. Mill's essay On Liberty (1859) argued for the importance of protecting individual liberty against the tyranny of the majority and the interference of the state. His views emphasized the right of privacy as essential for personal development and self-expression. [ 85 ] Discussions surrounding surveillance coincided with philosophical ideas on privacy. Jeremy Bentham developed the phenomenon known as the Panoptic effect through his 1791 architectural design of a prison called Panopticon. The phenomenon explored the possibility of surveillance as a general awareness of being watched that could never be proven at any particular moment. [ 86 ] French philosopher Michel Foucault (1926–1984) concluded that the possibility of surveillance in the instance of the Panopticon meant a prisoner had no choice but to conform to the prison's rules. [ 86 ] More recently, the unequal effects of weak privacy protections have been debated. In Privacy at the Margins , law professor Scott Skinner-Thompson argues that marginalized and vulnerable groups are disproportionately affected both because they are more likely to be required to share personal information and because they experience more severe harm when their privacy is breached.

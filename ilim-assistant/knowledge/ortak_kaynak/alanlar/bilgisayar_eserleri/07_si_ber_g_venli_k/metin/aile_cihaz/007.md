Johnson tied privacy to the psychological concept of "personal control", which he modelled as a process with four stages and two kinds of behaviour–outcome relationship, the outcomes depending on both situational and personal factors. [ 35 ] On this model, privacy consists of "behaviors falling at specific locations on these two dimensions". [ 36 ] The four stages are outcome choice control , the selection between possible outcomes; behaviour selection control , the selection between strategies for attaining the chosen outcome; outcome effectance , the carrying out of the chosen behaviour so as to achieve the outcome; and outcome realisation control , the person's interpretation of the outcome achieved. The two dimensions are primary control, in which behaviour directly causes outcomes, and secondary control, in which it does so indirectly. [ 37 ] Johnson treated privacy as behaviour exercising secondary control over outcomes, and argued that a person's conception of privacy itself depends on these behaviour–outcome relationships; he developed the account in dialogue with the work of Irwin Altman , and of Maxine Wolfe and Robert S. Laufer. [ 38 ] The philosopher Lorenzo Magnani extends the idea of control to personal identity. He argues that consciousness is partly constituted by external representations of the self, such as narratives and data stored outside the body, but that much of it consists of internal representations that remain private and are rarely externalised. [ 39 ] Magnani describes this internal privacy as a form of "information property" or "moral capital" that is essential to free choice and personal agency. When too much of a person's identity is externalised and subjected to scrutiny, he contends, the result is a loss of control, dignity and responsibility; the protection of privacy therefore safeguards the ability to pursue personal projects free from intrusive external forces. [ 40 ] Contextual integrity [ edit ] Main article: Contextual integrity Helen Nissenbaum 's theory of contextual integrity holds that privacy is violated not by disclosure as such but by information flows that breach the norms of the social context in which the information was shared. A patient's disclosure of symptoms to a doctor is appropriate within medicine but would violate contextual norms if passed to an employer. [ 41 ] The theory has been widely adopted in computer science and policy analysis because it explains why aggregating individually public information can nevertheless be harmful. Personhood and Autonomy [ edit ] Privacy may be understood as a prerequisite for the development of a sense of self-identity. Privacy barriers, in particular, are instrumental in this process. According to Irwin Altman, such barriers "define and limit the boundaries of the self" and thus "serve to help define [the self]." [ 42 ] This control primarily entails the ability to regulate contact with others. [ 42 ] Control over the "permeability" of the self's boundaries enables one to control what constitutes the self and thus to define what is the self. [ 42 ] Several philosophers present privacy as a condition for the development of personhood. Jeffrey Reiman argued that privacy is a social ritual by which a society recognises, and conveys to the individual—that one's physical existence is under one's moral ownership. [ 43 ] Hence, a moral right to self-determination devirated from the notion of privacy. [ 43 ] Stanley Benn grounded privacy in respect for persons as choosers, observing that covert observation alters the conditions under which a person chooses without one's knowledge. [ 44 ] Joseph Kupfer linked privacy to the formation of an autonomous self-concept , drawing on studies of child development and on Erving Goffman 's account of "total institutions", in which the systematic deprivation of privacy erodes inmates' sense of self. [ 45 ] [ 46 ] In his understanding, an autonomous self-concept entails a conception of oneself as a "purposeful, self-determining, responsible agent" and an awareness of one's capacity to control the boundary between self and other—that is, to control who can access and experience one's and to which extent. [ 45 ] In United States constitutional law, a related "decisional" conception of privacy protected choices about contraception , abortion and intimate relationships . [ 47 ] [ 34 ] Limited access [ edit ] In addition, privacy may be seen as a state that fosters personal growth, a process integral to the development of self-identity. Hyman Gross suggested that, without privacy—solitude, anonymity, and temporary releases from social roles—individuals would be unable to freely express themselves and to engage in self-discovery and self-criticism . [ 45 ] Such self-discovery and self-criticism contributes to one's understanding of oneself and shapes one's sense of identity. [ 45 ] Intimacy [ edit ] In a way analogous to how the personhood theory imagines privacy as some essential part of being an individual, the intimacy theory imagines privacy to be an essential part of the way that humans have strengthened or intimate relationships with other humans. [ 48 ] Because part of human relationships includes individuals volunteering to self-disclose most if not all personal information, this is one area in which privacy does not apply. [ 48 ] James Rachels advanced this notion by writing that privacy matters because "there is a close connection between our ability to control who has access to us and to information about us, and our ability to create and maintain different sorts of social relationships with different people." [ 49 ] On this view privacy allows people to calibrate what they disclose to friends, colleagues and strangers, and so makes intimate relationships possible. [ 49 ] Hence, protecting intimacy is also at the core of the concept of sexual privacy. The legal scholar Danielle Citron has built on this tradition to argue that "sexual privacy" deserves recognition as a distinct and especially weighty interest. [ 50 ] Forms of privacy [ edit ] Alongside general theories of what privacy is, scholars have distinguished the experiences that privacy makes possible and the domains in which it is claimed, from the physical space around a person to the confidential activities of organisations. States of privacy [ edit ] Alan Westin defined four states—or experiences—of privacy: solitude, intimacy, anonymity, and reserve. Solitude is a physical separation from others; [ 51 ] Intimacy is a "close, relaxed; and frank relationship between two or more individuals" that results from the seclusion of a pair or small group of individuals. [ 51 ] Anonymity is the "desire of individuals for times of 'public privacy.'" [ 51 ] Lastly, reserve is the "creation of a psychological barrier against unwanted intrusion"; this creation of a psychological barrier requires others to respect an individual's need or desire to restrict communication of information concerning themself. [ 51 ] In addition to the psychological barrier of reserve, Kirsty Hughes identified three more kinds of privacy barriers: physical, behavioural, and normative. Physical barriers, such as walls and doors, prevent others from accessing and experiencing the individual. [ 52 ] (In this sense, "accessing" an individual includes accessing personal information about them.) [ 52 ] Behavioral barriers communicate to others—verbally, through language, or non-verbally, through personal space, body language, or clothing—that an individual does not want the other person to access or experience them. [ 52 ] Lastly, normative barriers, such as laws and social norms, restrain others from attempting to access or experience an individual. [ 52 ] Physical privacy [ edit ] Physical privacy may be defined as the prevention of "intrusions into one's physical space or solitude". [ 53 ] An example of a legal basis for physical privacy is the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, which guarantees "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures". [ 54 ] [ 55 ] Physical privacy may be a matter of cultural sensitivity , personal dignity or shyness , and may also involve concerns about safety, for example on the part of people who fear becoming victims of crime or stalking . [ 56 ] [ 57 ] It can involve protection against being watched, directly or through recorded images, during intimate behaviour or while exposing intimate parts , and against unauthorised access to one's possessions or places. Means of securing it, often for reasons of modesty , include clothing , walls , fences , privacy screens, cathedral glass and window coverings . Organisational privacy [ edit ] Government agencies, corporations and other organisations may wish to keep their activities or secrets from other organisations or individuals, and adopt security practices and controls to keep information confidential. They may also seek legal protection for such information. A government may invoke executive privilege [ 58 ] or designate information as classified , and a company may protect valuable proprietary information as trade secrets . [ 55 ] Privacy self-synchronisation [ edit ] Privacy self-synchronisation is a hypothesised state in which the stakeholders of an enterprise privacy programme, such as customers, employees, managers, executives, suppliers, partners and investors, spontaneously contribute to its success. According to the model, when self-synchronisation is reached, individuals' interests in their privacy are in balance with the business interests of the enterprises that collect and use their personal information.

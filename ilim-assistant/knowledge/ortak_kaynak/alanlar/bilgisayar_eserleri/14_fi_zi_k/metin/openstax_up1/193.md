Strategy The pressure at points at the same height on the two sides of a U-tube must be the same as long as the two points are in the same liquid. Therefore, we consider two points at the same level in the two arms of the tube: One point is the interface on the side of the Liquid 2 and the other is a point in the arm with Liquid 1 that is at the same level as the interface in the other arm. The pressure at each point is due to atmospheric pressure plus the weight of the liquid above it. Pressure on the side with Liquid 1 = p 0 + ρ 1 g h 1 Pressure on the side with Liquid 2 = p 0 + ρ 2 g h 2 Pressure on the side with Liquid 1 = p 0 + ρ 1 g h 1 Pressure on the side with Liquid 2 = p 0 + ρ 2 g h 2 Solution Since the two points are in Liquid 1 and are at the same height, the pressure at the two points must be the same. Therefore, we have p 0 + ρ 1 g h 1 = p 0 + ρ 2 g h 2 . p 0 + ρ 1 g h 1 = p 0 + ρ 2 g h 2 . Hence, ρ 1 h 1 = ρ 2 h 2 . ρ 1 h 1 = ρ 2 h 2 . This means that the difference in heights on the two sides of the U-tube is h 2 − h 1 = ( 1 − ρ 2 ρ 1 ) h 2 . h 2 − h 1 = ( 1 − ρ 2 ρ 1 ) h 2 . The result makes sense if we set ρ 2 = ρ 1 , ρ 2 = ρ 1 , which gives h 2 = h 1 . h 2 = h 1 . If the two sides have the same density, they have the same height. Units of pressure As stated earlier, the SI unit for pressure is the pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m 2 . 1 Pa = 1 N/m 2 . In addition to the pascal, many other units for pressure are in common use ( Table 14.3 ). In meteorology, atmospheric pressure is often described in the unit of millibars (mbar), where 1000 mbar = 1 × 10 5 Pa . 1000 mbar = 1 × 10 5 Pa . The millibar is a convenient unit for meteorologists because the average atmospheric pressure at sea level on Earth is 1.013 × 10 5 Pa = 1013 mbar = 1 atm 1.013 × 10 5 Pa = 1013 mbar = 1 atm . Using the equations derived when considering pressure at a depth in a fluid, pressure can also be measured as millimeters or inches of mercury. The pressure at the bottom of a 760-mm column of mercury at 0 °C 0 °C in a container where the top part is evacuated is equal to the atmospheric pressure. Thus, 760 mm Hg is also used in place of 1 atmosphere of pressure. In vacuum physics labs, scientists often use another unit called the torr, named after Torricelli, who, as we have just seen, invented the mercury manometer for measuring pressure. One torr is equal to a pressure of 1 mm Hg.

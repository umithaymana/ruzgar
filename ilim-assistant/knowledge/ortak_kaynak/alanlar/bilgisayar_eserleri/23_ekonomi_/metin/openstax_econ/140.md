The reason for this should be clear: The value of nominal GDP is “inflated” by inflation. Similarly, as long as inflation is positive, real GDP should be greater than nominal GDP in any year before the base year. Figure 19.9 shows the U.S. nominal and real GDP since 1960. Because 2005 is the base year, the nominal and real values are exactly the same in that year. However, over time, the rise in nominal GDP looks much larger than the rise in real GDP (that is, the nominal GDP line rises more steeply than the real GDP line), because the presence of inflation, especially in the 1970s exaggerates the rise in nominal GDP. Figure 19.9 U.S. Nominal and Real GDP, 1960–2020 The red line measures U.S. GDP in nominal dollars. The black line measures U.S. GDP in real dollars, where all dollar values are converted to 2012 dollars. Since we express real GDP in 2012 dollars, the two lines cross in 2012. However, real GDP will appear higher than nominal GDP in the years before 2012, because dollars were worth less in 2012 than in previous years. Conversely, real GDP will appear lower in the years after 2012, because dollars were worth more in 2012 than in later years. Let’s return to the question that we posed originally: How much did GDP increase in real terms? What was the real GDP growth rate from 1960 to 2020? To find the real growth rate, we apply the formula for percentage change: 2020&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP&nbsp;–&nbsp;1960&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP 1960&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP &nbsp;×&nbsp;100 &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change 18,392.3&nbsp;–&nbsp;3,267.5 3,267.5 &nbsp;×&nbsp;100 &nbsp;=&nbsp; 463% 2020&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP&nbsp;–&nbsp;1960&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP 1960&nbsp;real&nbsp;GDP &nbsp;×&nbsp;100 &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change 18,392.3&nbsp;–&nbsp;3,267.5 3,267.5 &nbsp;×&nbsp;100 &nbsp;=&nbsp; 463% In other words, the U.S. economy has increased real production of goods and services by nearly a factor of five since 1960. Of course, that understates the material improvement since it fails to capture improvements in the quality of products and the invention of new products. There is a quicker way to answer this question approximately, using another math trick. Because: Nominal &nbsp;=&nbsp; Price&nbsp;×&nbsp;Quantity %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Nominal &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Price&nbsp;+&nbsp;%&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Quantity &nbsp;OR&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Quantity &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Nominal&nbsp;–&nbsp;%&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Price Nominal &nbsp;=&nbsp; Price&nbsp;×&nbsp;Quantity %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Nominal &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Price&nbsp;+&nbsp;%&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Quantity &nbsp;OR&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Quantity &nbsp;=&nbsp; %&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Nominal&nbsp;–&nbsp;%&nbsp;change&nbsp;in&nbsp;Price Therefore, real GDP growth rate (% change in quantity) equals the growth rate in nominal GDP (% change in value) minus the inflation rate (% change in price). Note that using this equation provides an approximation for small changes in the levels. For more accurate measures, one should use the first formula.

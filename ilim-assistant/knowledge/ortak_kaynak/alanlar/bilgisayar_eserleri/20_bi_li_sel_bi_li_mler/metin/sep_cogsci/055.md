According to Turing, the question whether machines can think is itself &ldquo;too meaningless&rdquo; to deserve discussion (442). However, if we consider the more precise&mdash;and somehow related&mdash;question whether a digital computer can do well in a certain kind of game that Turing describes (&ldquo;The Imitation Game&rdquo;), then&mdash;at least in Turing&rsquo;s eyes&mdash;we do have a question that admits of precise discussion. Moreover, as we shall see, Turing himself thought that it would not be too long before we did have digital computers that could &ldquo;do well&rdquo; in the Imitation Game. The phrase &ldquo;The Turing Test&rdquo; is sometimes used more generally to refer to some kinds of behavioural tests for the presence of mind, or thought, or intelligence in putatively minded entities. So, for example, it is sometimes suggested that The Turing Test is prefigured in Descartes&rsquo; Discourse on the Method . (Copeland (2000:527) finds an anticipation of the test in the 1668 writings of the Cartesian de Cordemoy. Abramson (2011a) presents archival evidence that Turing was aware of Descartes&rsquo; language test at the time that he wrote his 1950 paper. Gunderson (1964) provides an early instance of those who find that Turing&rsquo;s work is foreshadowed in the work of Descartes.) The phrase &ldquo;The Turing Test&rdquo; is also sometimes used to refer to certain kinds of purely behavioural allegedly logically sufficient conditions for the presence of mind, or thought, or intelligence, in putatively minded entities. So, for example, Ned Block&rsquo;s &ldquo;Blockhead&rdquo; thought experiment is often said to be a (putative) knockdown objection to The Turing Test. (Block (1981) contains a direct discussion of The Turing Test in this context.) Here, what a proponent of this view has in mind is the idea that it is logically possible for an entity to pass the kinds of tests that Descartes and (at least allegedly) Turing have in mind&mdash;to use words (and, perhaps, to act) in just the kind of way that human beings do&mdash;and yet to be entirely lacking in intelligence, not possessed of a mind, etc. The subsequent discussion takes up the preceding ideas in the order in which they have been introduced. First, there is a discussion of Turing&rsquo;s paper (1950), and of the arguments contained therein. Second, there is a discussion of current assessments of various proposals that have been called &ldquo;The Turing Test&rdquo; (whether or not there is much merit in the application of this label to the proposals in question). Third, there is a brief discussion of some recent writings on The Turing Test, including some discussion of the question whether The Turing Test sets an appropriate goal for research into artificial intelligence. Finally, there is a very short discussion of Searle&rsquo;s Chinese Room argument, and, in particular, of the bearing of this argument on The Turing Test. (2000), and Shieber (2004). For further information about Turing himself, see, for example: Cooper and van Leeuwen (2013), Copeland et al. Suppose that we have a person, a machine, and an interrogator. The interrogator is in a room separated from the other person and the machine. The object of the game is for the interrogator to determine which of the other two is the person, and which is the machine. The interrogator knows the other person and the machine by the labels &lsquo; X &rsquo; and &lsquo; Y &rsquo;&mdash;but, at least at the beginning of the game, does not know which of the other person and the machine is &lsquo; X &rsquo;&mdash;and at the end of the game says either &lsquo; X is the person and Y is the machine&rsquo; or &lsquo; X is the machine and Y is the person&rsquo;.

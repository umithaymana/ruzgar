Assuming that knowledge entails belief, the basic thought is that intention in action involves the belief that one is doing A . Doing something for a reason involves a belief about one&rsquo;s reason for doing it that constitutes intention in action. And prospective intention, or intention for the future, involves a belief about what one is going to do and why. The idea that intention involves belief is what unifies intentional action, prospective intention, and intention-with-which. (On an alternative view, which may be Anscombe&rsquo;s, intention contrasts with belief: they correspond to radically different kinds of knowledge. See the treatment of mistakes below.) The claim that intention entails belief&mdash;most commonly, that if one intends to do A , one believes that one is going to do it&mdash;is widespread among those who draw no particular inspiration from Anscombe. (See Audi 1973; Harman 1976; Davis 1984; Ross 2009.) As Grice (1971, pp. 264&ndash;6) observed, there is peculiarity of some sort in asserting &lsquo;I intend to do A , but I might not do it,&rsquo; a peculiarity readily explained if intention is a species of belief. It is equally striking that the ordinary expression of intention for the future is &lsquo;I am going to do A&rsquo; (Anscombe 1963, p. 1), which looks like the assertoric expression of belief. The same point holds for intention in action: &lsquo;What are you up to?&rsquo;; &lsquo;I am doing A .&rsquo; Although such evidence is suggestive, however, it might be explained in other ways. (See Davidson 1978, pp. 91, 100; for criticism, Pears 1985; and for more recent discussion, Maru&scaron;i&#263; and Schwenkler 2018, &sect;2.2; Levy 2018; Asarnow forthcoming.) So far, we have only the fragment of a theory, an alleged condition of intending, not an adequate account of what intention is. Here there are several possibilities. It is sometimes said that intention is belief (Velleman 1989; Maru&scaron;i&#263; and Schwenkler 2018). But this creates a risk of confusion. It is not clear that anyone believes there is a value of p such that intending to do A is no more than believing p . Instead they hold that part of what it is to intend to do A is to believe p ; other conditions must be met. The confusion stems from talk of particular &lsquo;tokens&rsquo; of intending which may be identified with tokens of belief. But the idea of a token state may not be coherent (Steward 1997) and the would-be cognitivist need think otherwise. If intention involves belief, what more does it require? On the simplest proposal, to intend an action is to believe that one will perform it and to have an appropriate guiding desire (Audi 1973, p. 395). But a mere conjunction seems insufficient: the desire and belief could be utterly unrelated (Davis 1984, pp. 141&ndash;2). This prompts the suggestion that, when S intends to do A , his belief rests on his desire: to intend an action is to believe that one will perform it on the ground that one wants to do so (Davis 1984, p. 147; see also Grice 1971, pp. 278&ndash;9). The principal defect of this account is that it makes the belief component of intention epiphenomenal. This belief merely registers one&rsquo;s activity: the motivational work is done by a prior desire. (Something similar is true on more subtle theories that divorce the motivational role of intention from belief; as, for instance, Ross 2009, pp. 250&ndash;1.) If the claim that intention involves belief is to capture the essence of the will, not a superficial fact about the word &lsquo;intend,&rsquo; the belief must be implicated in the functions of intending and the explanation of action. (For objections of this kind, see Bratman 1987, pp. 19&ndash;20 and Mele 1992, Ch. 8 on &lsquo;intention*&rsquo;; on the role of beliefs in planning for the future, see Velleman 1989, Ch. 8; Velleman 2007; Maru&scaron;i&#263; and Schwenkler 2018, &sect;2.3; Alonso 2020; Alonso 2021.) There is variation even among those accounts that give a motivational role to belief. On Velleman&rsquo;s early view, intentions are &lsquo;self-fulfilling expectations that are motivated by a desire for their fulfillment and that represent themselves as such&rsquo; (Velleman 1989, p. 109). Such expectations interact with a general desire for self-knowledge to motivate action by which they are confirmed. More recently, Velleman has replaced the desire for self-knowledge with a sub-personal aim or disposition (Velleman 2000: 19&ndash;21). Either way, his view threatens to generate what Bratman (1991, pp. 261&ndash;2) calls &lsquo;the problem of promiscuity&rsquo;: in attributing a general tendency that motivates us to conform to our own expectations, it predicts that we will be just as strongly motivated by beliefs that do not constitute intentions, like the belief that I am going to trip over the step, or mispronounce a word. A different proposal, due to Harman (1976, p. 168) is that intentions are &lsquo;conclusion[s] of practical reasoning&rsquo; (see also Ross 2009, pp. 270&ndash;2; Maru&scaron;i&#263; and Schwenkler 2018). But it seems possible to intend an action spontaneously, for no particular reason. In later work, Harman looks downstream of intention, rather than upstream: an intention is a belief about what one is doing or what one is going to do that has the power to guide and motivate action through practical thought (Harman 1986, pp. 375&ndash;6; Setiya 2007a, pp. 48&ndash;53). This claim interacts with the question about &lsquo;disjunctive&rsquo; theories left unanswered at the end of section 2. If intention involves belief, those who treat knowledge as basic, with mere belief its defective form, will take a similar view of &lsquo;knowledge in intention&rsquo; (R&ouml;dl 2007; Ch. 2; McDowell 2010, &sect;7; Marcus 2012, Ch. 2). In the good case, one&rsquo;s intention in action involves knowledge of what one is doing: it entails, so cannot cause or motivate, one&rsquo;s action. In the bad case, one merely intends to act. If not its causal role, however, what distinguishes knowledge in intention from knowledge of other kinds? Finally, there is Anscombe&rsquo;s own view, on which there is a normative contrast between intention and ordinary belief. If one fails to act as one intends, and not through a mistaken belief about the means or possibilities for action, &lsquo;the mistake is not one of judgment but of performance&rsquo; (Anscombe 1963, pp. 56&ndash;7). Intention sets a standard of success for what does. (For discussions of this point, see Frost 2014; Setiya 2016a; Campbell 2018a; Campbell 2018b.) What is more, there is &lsquo;a difference of form between reasoning leading to action and reasoning for the truth of a conclusion&rsquo; (Anscombe 1963, p. 60). Intention is justified by the former, not the latter: by practical not theoretical reasoning (Anscombe 1963, pp. 57&ndash;62). In this respect, Anscombe&rsquo;s doctrine differs most sharply from Velleman&rsquo;s. As well as thinking of intention as a kind of belief, Velleman holds that &lsquo;practical reasoning is a kind of theoretical reasoning&rsquo; (Velleman 1989, p. 15; see also Ross 2009). In a review of Velleman&rsquo;s book, Bratman (1991, pp. 250&ndash;1) dubbed the conjunction of these claims &lsquo;cognitivism about practical reason.&rsquo; It prompts what Davidson (1978, p. 95) called &lsquo;the strongest argument against identifying [intention] with [belief],&rsquo; that &lsquo;reasons for intending to do something are in general quite different from reasons for believing one will do it.&rsquo; Though Velleman (1989, pp. 122&ndash;5) defends that identification, one need not do so in order to accept the theory of intention as a form of belief or the idea of &lsquo;knowledge in intention.&rsquo; A more modest &lsquo;cognitivism&rsquo; would hold that intention involves belief, that practical reasoning does not reduce to theoretical, but that some requirements that govern intention are best understood as requirements of theoretical reason. It is often regarded as a virtue of such cognitivism that it explains why there should be an indefeasible requirement of consistency among intentions and beliefs (Ross 2009, pp. 244&ndash;7). It has also been argued that the requirement of means-end coherence follows from requirements of theoretical reason on the beliefs that figure in our intentions (Harman 1976, p. 153; Wallace 2001; Setiya 2007b; Ross 2009, pp. 261&ndash;5). If I intend to do E and thus believe that I will do it, and I believe that doing M is a necessary means to doing E , but do not intend or believe that I am going to do M , I fail to believe a practically salient logical consequence of what I believe. The principal challenge for a cognitivist account of means-end coherence is to explain why one must avoid such theoretical failures by forming the relevant intention, not just the corresponding belief (Bratman 2009a). But once again, one need not defend cognitivism, even in its less ambitious form, in conceiving intention as a kind of belief. There are two main arguments against this conception. The first turns on apparent cases of intention without belief. According to Davidson, &lsquo;[a] man may be making ten carbon copies as he writes, and this may be intentional; yet he may not know that he is; all he knows is that he is trying&rsquo; (Davidson 1971, p. 50; see also Davidson 1978, pp. 91&ndash;4). Or imagine I am recovering from paralysis, and movement slowly returns to my hand. At a certain point, I am not sure that I can clench my fist. As it happens, I can. But if I try to do so behind my back, under anesthesia, I may not believe that I am clenching my fist, even though&mdash;on the face of it&mdash;I am doing so intentionally, and that is just what I intend (Setiya 2008, pp. 390&ndash;1).

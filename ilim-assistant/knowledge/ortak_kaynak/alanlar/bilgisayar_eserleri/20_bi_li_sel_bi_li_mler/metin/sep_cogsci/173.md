A central element in their critique is the claim that cases, such as Perry&rsquo;s shopper, that are often thought to show the special connection between self-consciousness (I-thoughts) and the capacity for action really only show that action explanation contexts do not allow for substitution salva veritate , but rather are opaque (Cappelen &amp; Dever 2013: ch. 3). See the supplement: Scepticism About Essential Indexicality and Agency . 2.2 First-Person Reference Because of its connection with the first-person pronoun, it is often taken as platitudinous to say that self-conscious thought is closely associated with the capacity to refer to oneself as oneself . When I think self-consciously, I cannot fail to refer to myself. More than this, it has often been claimed that for a central class of first-person thoughts, there is no possibility of misidentifying myself: not only can I not fail to pick myself out, I cannot take another person to be me. But first-person reference, and indexicality more generally, has sometimes been thought to pose a challenge to theories of reference, requiring special treatment. Indeed, some have argued that the platitude itself should be rejected. Terms whose function it is to refer can, on occasion, fail in that function. A use of the term &ldquo;Vulcan&rdquo; to refer to the planet orbiting between Mercury and the sun, fails to refer to anything for the reason that there is nothing for it to refer to. If I see the head of one dachshund protruding from behind a tree and the rear end of another protruding from the other side of the tree, and utter &ldquo;that dog is huge&rdquo;, my use of &ldquo;that dog&rdquo; has arguably failed to refer due to there being too many objects. It would seem that, by contrast, the term &ldquo;I&rdquo; cannot fail to refer either by there being too few or too many objects. &ldquo;I&rdquo; is guaranteed to refer. As an indexical, the referent of &ldquo;I&rdquo; varies with the context of utterance (see the entry on indexicals ). That is, &ldquo;I&rdquo; refers to different people depending on who utters it. Following Kaplan (1977), it is common to think of the meaning of such terms as determining a function from context to referent. In the case of &ldquo;I&rdquo;, a natural proposal is the &ldquo;Self-Reference Rule&rdquo; (SRR), that the referent of a token of &ldquo;I&rdquo; is the person that produces it (Kaplan 1977: 491; Campbell 1994: ch. 3). &ldquo;I&rdquo; is thus, unlike &ldquo;this&rdquo; or &ldquo;that&rdquo;, a pure indexical, seemingly requiring no overt demonstration or manifestation of intention (Kaplan 1977: 489&ndash;91). SRR captures the plausible thought that &ldquo;I&rdquo; is guaranteed against reference failure. Since every token of &ldquo;I&rdquo; has been produced, the fact that &ldquo;I&rdquo; refers to its producer means that there is no chance of its failing to pick out some entity. This account of &ldquo;I&rdquo;, then, treats it as not only expressive of self-consciousness, but also guaranteed to refer to the utterer. SRR is Kaplan&rsquo;s specification of the character of &ldquo;I&rdquo;, which is fixed independently of the context of any particular utterance. This character is to be distinguished from the content of a tokening of &ldquo;I&rdquo;, which it has only in a context. On Kaplan&rsquo;s account, the content of an utterance of &ldquo;I am F &rdquo; will be a singular proposition composed of the person that produced it and F ness (Recanati 1993; for an account that attributes to the utterance both singular content, and the &ldquo;reflexive content&rdquo; the speaker of this token is F , see Perry 2001; for an alternative &ldquo;Neo-Fregean&rdquo; account in terms of object-dependent de re senses, see Evans 1981; cf. McDowell 1977; and Evans 1982: ch. 1). Intuitively plausible as it is, SRR is open to a number of potential counterexamples. Suppose, for example, that you are away from work due to illness and I leave a note on your door reading &ldquo;I am not here now&rdquo;. Plausibly, whilst it was me that produced this token of &ldquo;I&rdquo;, it nevertheless refers to you . Or consider a situation in which I walk into a petrol station, point to my car, and say &ldquo;I&rsquo;m empty&rdquo;. In this case, it might be suggested, my use of &ldquo;I&rdquo; refers to my car rather than myself (for these and related cases, see Vision 1985; Q. Smith 1989; Sidelle 1991; Nunberg 1993, 1995). If so, SRR cannot specify the character of &ldquo;I&rdquo; and so, arguably, some tokens of &ldquo;I&rdquo; fail to express the self-conscious thoughts of those that produce them. In light of such cases, a variety of alternatives to SRR have been proposed. Q. Smith (1989) suggests that &ldquo;I&rdquo; is lexically ambiguous; Predelli (1998a,b, 2002) offers an intention-based reference rule for &ldquo;I&rdquo;; Corazza, Fish, and Gorvett (2002) offer a convention-based account; and Cohen (2013) argues that the cases can be handled by a conservative modification of Kaplan&rsquo;s original proposal (also see, Romdenh-Romluc 2002, 2008; Corazza 2004: ch. 5; Dodd &amp; Sweeney 2010; Michaelson 2014; &Aring;kerman 2017). Although she didn&rsquo;t have Kaplan&rsquo;s formulation of SRR in mind, an earlier criticism of such a rule can be found in the work of Anscombe (1975) who argues that the rule cannot be complete as an account of the meaning of &ldquo;I&rdquo; (for discussion see O&rsquo;Brien 1994). Anscombe considers a world in which each person has two names, one of which (ranging from &ldquo;B&rdquo; to &ldquo;Z&rdquo;) is printed on their chest, the other (in every case &ldquo;A&rdquo;) is printed on the inside of their wrist. Each person uses &ldquo;B&rdquo; to &ldquo;Z&rdquo; when attributing actions to others, but &ldquo;A&rdquo; when describing their own actions (Anscombe 1975: 49). Anscombe argues that such a situation is compatible with the possibility that the people in question lack self-consciousness. Whilst B uses &ldquo;A&rdquo; to refer to B, C uses &ldquo;A&rdquo; to refer to C, and so on, there is no guarantee that they are thinking of themselves as themselves , for they may be reporting what are in fact their own actions without thinking of those actions as things that they themselves are performing. They may treat themselves, that is, just as the treat any other. This is despite the fact that, in this scenario, &ldquo;A&rdquo; complies with SRR. Can the Self-Reference Rule be reformulated in such a way as to entail self-consciousness on the part of those who use terms that comply with it? According to Anscombe it can, but any such reformulation will presuppose a prior grasp of self-conscious reference to oneself. For example, if we say, employing the indirect reflexive, that &ldquo;I&rdquo; is a term that a person uses to refer to she herself , we have travelled in a tight circle since &ldquo;she herself&rdquo; can be understood only in terms of &ldquo;I&rdquo; (Anscombe 1975; Casta&ntilde;eda 1966; for discussion, see Berm&uacute;dez 1998: ch. 1; Haddock 2019; Teichmann 2022, Part IV). This can be seen clearly in the first-person formulation of such a rule: &ldquo;I&rdquo; is a term that I use to refer to myself . For here the &ldquo;myself&rdquo; must itself be understood as an expression of self-consciousness, i.e., we should really say that &ldquo;I&rdquo; is a term that I use to refer to myself as myself . In response to Anscombe&rsquo;s argument, it has been argued that SRR is not intended to explain the connection between self-consciousness and the first-person (O&rsquo;Brien 1994, 1995a; Garrett 1998: ch. 7; cf. Campbell 1994: &sect;4.2; Peacocke 2008: &sect;3.1). On this view, all that the example of &ldquo;A&rdquo; users shows is that self-consciousness has not been fully accounted for by SRR, not that SRR fails as an account of the character of &ldquo;I&rdquo;. Kaplan himself, however, does appear to be more ambitious than this, claiming that the &ldquo;particular and primitive way&rdquo; in which each of us is presented to ourselves is simply that each &ldquo;is presented to himself under the character of &lsquo;I&rsquo;&rdquo; (Kaplan 1977: 533). This claim, it would seem, is indeed open to Anscombe&rsquo;s challenge. Anscombe&rsquo;s (1975) paper is perhaps most notable for her claim that &ldquo;I&rdquo; is not a referring expression at all. Assuming that if &ldquo;I&rdquo; refers it must be understood on the model of either a proper name, a demonstrative, or an abbreviation of a definite description, Anscombe argues that each of these kinds of referring expression requires what she calls a &ldquo;conception&rdquo; by means of which it reaches its referent. This conception must explain the seemingly guaranteed reference of &ldquo;I&rdquo;: the apparent fact that no token of &ldquo;I&rdquo; can fail to pick out an object. However, she argues, no satisfactory conception can be specified for &ldquo;I&rdquo; since either it fails to deliver up guaranteed reference, or it succeeds but only by delivering an immaterial soul.

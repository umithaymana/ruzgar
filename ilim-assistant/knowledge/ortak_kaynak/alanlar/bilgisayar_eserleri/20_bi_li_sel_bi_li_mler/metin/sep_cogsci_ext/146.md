What exactly is the difference? What does it take to know something? It&rsquo;s not enough just to believe it&mdash;we don&rsquo;t know the things we&rsquo;re wrong about. Knowledge seems to be more like a way of getting at the truth. The analysis of knowledge concerns the attempt to articulate in what exactly this kind of &ldquo;getting at the truth&rdquo; consists. More particularly, the project of analysing knowledge is to state conditions that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient for propositional knowledge, thoroughly answering the question: &ldquo;What does it take to know something?&rdquo; By &ldquo;propositional knowledge&rdquo;, we mean knowing that something is the case , i.e., knowledge of a proposition. For example, if Susan knows that Alyssa is a musician, she has knowledge of the proposition that Alyssa is a musician. Propositional knowledge should be distinguished from knowledge by &ldquo;acquaintance&rdquo;, for example when we say that Susan knows Alyssa , or that she knows Vancouver . (Knowing Susan may involve knowing some propositions about Susan, but it is only the latter kind of knowledge that is our focus here.) &ldquo;Knows that&rdquo; is the canonical English expression for indicating propositional knowledge. We also often attribute propositional knowledge with so-called &ldquo;knows-wh&rdquo; locutions: Sooyung might know whether the shop is open, for instance, or Juan might know who won the election. These cases also involve propositional knowledge: one knows these things by knowing the proposition that is the answer to the embedded question. Sooyung can know whether the shop is open by knowing that the shop is not open. There is some controversy over the particular status of knowledge-how (i.e., &ldquo;Ahmed knows how to ride a bicycle&rdquo;)&mdash;see Stanley 2011 and his opponents discussed therein. The propositional knowledge that is the analysandum of the analysis of knowledge literature is paradigmatically expressed in English by sentences of the form &ldquo; S knows that P &rdquo;, where &ldquo; S &rdquo; refers to the knowing subject, and &ldquo; P &rdquo; expresses the proposition that is known. A proposed analysis consists of a statement of the following form: &ldquo; S knows that P if and only if J ,&rdquo; where J indicates that S satisfies the analysans&mdash;paradigmatically, a list of conditions that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient for S to have knowledge that P . It is not enough merely to pick out the actual extension of knowledge. Even if, in actual fact, all cases of S knowing that P are cases in which S satisfies J , and all cases of the latter are cases of the former, J might fail as an analysis of knowledge. For example, it might be that there are possible cases of knowledge without J , or vice versa. A proper analysis of knowledge should at least be a necessary truth. Consequently, hypothetical thought experiments provide appropriate test cases for various analyses, as we shall see below. Even a necessary biconditional linking knowledge to some state J would probably not be sufficient for an analysis of knowledge, although just what more is required is a matter of some controversy. According to one version of the project, to analyze knowledge is literally to identify the components that make up knowledge&mdash;compare a chemist who analyzes a sample to learn its chemical composition. On this interpretation of the project of analyzing knowledge, the defender of a successful analysis of knowledge will be committed to something like the metaphysical claim that what it is for S to know that P is for some list of conditions involving S and the proposition that P to obtain. Other theorists think of the analysis of knowledge as distinctively conceptual &mdash;to analyse knowledge is to limn the structure of the concept of knowledge. On one version of this approach, the concept knowledge is literally composed of more basic concepts, linked together by something like Boolean operators. Some contributors to the literature write in terms of definitions of knowledge. Consequently, an analysis is subject not only to extensional accuracy, but to facts about the cognitive representation of knowledge and other epistemic notions. In practice, many epistemologists engaging in the project of analyzing knowledge leave these metaphilosophical interpretive questions unresolved; attempted analyses, and counterexamples thereto, are often proposed without its being made explicit whether the claims are intended as metaphysical or conceptual ones. [ 1 ] In many cases, this lack of specificity may be legitimate, since all parties tend to agree that an analysis of knowledge ought at least to be extensionally correct in all metaphysically possible worlds. As we shall see, many theories have been defended and, especially, refuted on those terms. The attempt to analyze knowledge has received a considerable amount of attention from epistemologists, particularly in the late 20 th century, but no analysis has been widely accepted. Some contemporary epistemologists reject the assumption that knowledge is susceptible to analysis. 1. Knowledge as Justified True Belief Much of the 20 th century literature on the analysis of knowledge began with a critique of a three-part &ldquo;traditional&rdquo; analysis of knowledge. Although this analysis does derive from ideas articulated throughout the history of epistemology, it&rsquo;s a bit of a convenient fiction to say that this so-called &ldquo;traditional&rdquo; analysis was ever widely accepted; it was primarily articulated in the 20 th century by its attackers. See Kaplan 1985, Dutant 2015, Antognazza 2015, and Le Morvan 2017 on the mismatch between the canonical story and the actual intellectual history. There are three components to the traditional analysis of knowledge. (For this reason, it is sometimes referred to as a &ldquo;tripartite&rdquo; analysis of knowledge.) According to this analysis, justified true belief is necessary and sufficient for knowledge. The Tripartite Analysis of Knowledge: S knows that P if and only if: It is true that P ; S believes that P ; and S is justified in believing that P . The tripartite analysis of knowledge is often abbreviated as the &ldquo;JTB&rdquo; analysis, for &ldquo;justified true belief&rdquo;. Let&rsquo;s consider the three conditions in turn. 1.1 The Truth Condition Most epistemologists agree that what is false cannot be known. For example, eighty-four is not a prime number. Consequently, it could never be the case that anyone knows that eighty-four is a prime number. One can only know things that are true. For this reason, the traditional theory of knowledge includes, as one of the components for the analysans for S knows that P , the condition that it is true that P . (Sometimes, the explicit invocation of truth is left out, and the relevant condition is listed simply as P . See Nozick (1981: 680, n. 6).) Sometimes, when people are very confident of something that turns out to be wrong, it can be natural to describe them as &ldquo;knowing&rdquo; that false proposition. Suppose, for example, that late in a sports match, the home team is so far behind that many spectators are very confident that they will lose. If they turn around in the final seconds to achieve a miraculous comeback and win the game, some people might be inclined to say that, prior to the comeback, many spectators &ldquo;knew&rdquo; that the home team would lose. Allan Hazlett (2010) argues on the basis of data like this that &ldquo;knows&rdquo; is not a factive verb. [ 2 ] Hazlett&rsquo;s diagnosis is deeply controversial; most epistemologists will treat such knowledge ascriptions as a kind of exaggeration, and not as literally true.

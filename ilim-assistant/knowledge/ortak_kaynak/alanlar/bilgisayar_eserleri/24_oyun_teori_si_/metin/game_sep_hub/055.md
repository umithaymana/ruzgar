The analysis of bad norms isn&rsquo;t just limited to cases where the norms in question are clearly the culprit in bringing about bad outcomes, though. Game theory can also tell us about the ways in which seemingly innocent norms and preferences can give rise to bad outcomes. Perhaps the most famous example of this is Schelling&rsquo;s (1971) model of segregation which helps illustrate why segregation can be so difficult to combat. Schelling showed that even when agents don&rsquo;t mind living alongside members of other groups, even very minimal preferences for having some neighbors like oneself can lead to individuals sorting themselves into relatively segregated communities over time. Hardin 1968) where game theory has been used to show how resources can easily be depleted or degraded when they are held in common and use of the resource is not regulated. The idea of the tragedy of the commons is that when access to a scarce resource is not limited in some way there is a tendency for use of the resource to exceed the environment&rsquo;s capacity to regenerate the resource. So, for example, we overgraze land, deplete fish and game stocks, run down the water level in aquifers, or emit too much pollution into the environment. Generally speaking, common pool resource problems arise in situations where (i) individuals stand to benefit from using the resource to the greatest extent possible and (ii) the actions of any one individual have a negligible impact on the resource, but (iii) the actions of every individual taken together have a large impact. In these cases it&rsquo;s often easy for an individual to see that the actions of the community as a whole are problematic, but because any individual&rsquo;s decision to refrain from exploiting a resource is unlikely to make a difference by itself, individuals reasonably infer that they might as well exploit a resource for themselves while they can. As such, common pool resource problems are a species of collective action problem and are analogous in some ways to problems we&rsquo;ve discussed in previous sections (e.g., how to ensure that members of a community are willing to police norms even when doing so is costly). Common pool resource problems are generally solved in one of two ways: (1) by taking the resource in question out of the commons, that is by creating property rights in it, or (2) by finding ways to collectively manage the resource, for instance by regulating who can access the commons at a given time. Which approach is best suited to solving a particular problem, and what form that approach should take, depends on many factors, e.g., how excludable the resource is, whether it is stationary, and how quickly the resource can be regenerated. By providing a framework for analyzing the strategic behavior of individuals in different contexts, game theory provides a useful tool for identifying the solutions most likely to work in a given case. Here Elinor Ostrom&rsquo;s work on common pool resources, which won her the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics, has been especially influential. Ostrom used game theoretic analysis complemented by extensive field work to show that, although there is a surprising amount of diversity in the ways that communities have managed to sustainably govern common resources, the success of these approaches tends to be a function of a number of underlying principles (Ostrom 1990). Some of these principles reflect things we&rsquo;ve already discussed in other contexts, e.g., the importance of monitoring and sanctions. While others are perhaps less obvious. For instance, the importance of accommodating nested or overlapping jurisdictions of responsibility. Because the ways in which these principles manifest themselves is highly dependent on context, though, rather than saying more about them here, we&rsquo;ll instead turn to another problem that has received a lot of attention in environmental ethics: climate change. Game theory has played an important role in the moral and political analysis of climate change. In particular, it has been crucial to the analysis of why addressing climate change is difficult. Here, the consensus in the literature is that states (and other entities) interested in addressing climate change confront a massive free rider problem stemming from that fact that many of the things that can be done to prevent further climate change or mitigate its effects are either quasi-public goods or things which generate substantial positive externalities . For example, drastic reductions of carbon emissions and increased investment in carbon sequestration or alternative energy technologies are things which benefit most countries regardless of who invests in them. And, because investing in these things is costly (at least in the short term), we tend to underinvest in them, a problem that is exacerbated by the fact that the most serious costs of climate change arguably fall on future generations.

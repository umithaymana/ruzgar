Things aren&#8217;t perfect&#8212;the vendors could spread out further (out of equilibrium) by dividing themselves uniformly&#8212;but it&#8217;s a step up if you previously thought that all of the vendors would take the median. Proof The proof isn&#8217;t terribly insightful, but here goes. In equilibrium, each individual earns a payoff of 1/n. This is because each position attracts 1/n customers on either side of the position. That 2/n sum is divided two ways for the two players occupying the position, so each earns 1/n. The rest of the proof involves showing that there are no profitable deviations. There are three cases to consider. First, consider deviating to any other occupied position. Now three individuals are placed at the same spot. Their collective domains remains only 2/n portion of the interval. This is now split three ways, giving the individual a payoff of 2n/3, which is strictly less than 1/n. Second, consider a deviation to a position beyond one of the more extreme locations&#8212;i.e., less than 1/n or greater than (n-1)/n. The argument is symmetric, so I will only consider an amount less than 1/n. Let x &lt; 1/n be the deviation position. Consider the following hastily made but nonetheless helpful figure: Because customers go to the closest location, the deviator takes all the customers between 0 and x as well as all customers to the left of the midpoint between x and 1/n, which is (x + 1/n)/2. The picture clearly shows that this is not a profitable deviation: the deviator&#8217;s share is now strictly smaller than the interval between 0 and 1/n, which is how much the deviator would have received if he stayed put. (Some algebra quickly verifies this.) Finally, consider a deviation to a position between two locations already being occupied. Formally, we can describe this as any position between (m+1)/n and (m+3)/n, for any even integer m. For example, that could be any spot between 1/n and 3/n, 3/n and 5/n, and so forth. Letting y be the deviator&#8217;s new position, here&#8217;s another hastily made but helpful figure: Now the deviator&#8217;s captured customers are bounded on the left of the midpoint between (m+1)/n and y and bounded on the right by the midpoint between y and (m+3)/n. It takes some algeabra to show, but this winds up being exactly equal to 1/n. In other words, the deviation isn&#8217;t profitable. That exhausts all possible deviations. None are profitable, and thus this is a Nash equilibrium. End A couple notes. First, this doesn&#8217;t extend to odd cases, which tend to have&#8230;well&#8230; odd solutions . Second, there are other Nash equilibria for an even number of players, including some weird mixing . Institutions &#8211; Game Theory 101 Skip to content Game Theory 101 Game Theory 101: Game Theory Made Easy Menu Category: Institutions Fun with Institutional Design: Seattle Seahawks Edition A central lesson that game theory and institutional design teach us is that actors play to the rules that the institution creates, not the behaviors that the institution intends to elicit. Thus, you have think through the perverse incentives that an institution might create. Contract incentives in sports seem to be one of the worst violators in this regard. These incentives usually read &#8220;if you achieve x milestone, you will receive $y bonus.&#8221; The key problem in the design here is in the discontinuity. There is basically no substantive difference from a team&#8217;s perspective between 210 innings pitched and 209 and 2/3rds innings pitched. Yet you will have contracts that create massive paydays at 210 but provide nothing extra at 209 and 2/3rds. ( See Phil Hughes as an example.) Today&#8217;s contract failure comes from the Seattle Seahawks. Wide receiver David Moore&#8217;s contract called for a $100,000 bonus if he caught 35 passes this season. With 22 seconds left in Week 17 and Seattle up three points, Moore sat at 34. In a world that makes sense, Seattle would take a knee and win the game. Coach Pete Carroll called in just that. Instead, this happened: Apparently Russell Wilson knew about the incentive and changed the play in the huddle so that Moore could hit the guarantee&#8212;a great response to the incentive structure put in front of them! But also a completely unnecessary injury risk for a team that had secured a win and a spot in the playoffs. The solution to this type of problem is to eliminate the crazy discontinuities in contract incentives. If Seattle thinks that Moore should be incentivized to hit catch goals, they should incrementally pay some amount of money per catch rather than concentrate all of the value in the 35th.
